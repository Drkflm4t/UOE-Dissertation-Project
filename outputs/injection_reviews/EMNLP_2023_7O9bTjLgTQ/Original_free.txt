This paper presents VISIT, a visualization and interpretation framework for transformer language models that aims to expose “semantic information flow” during a forward pass. The core idea is to project hidden states and selected weight-derived vectors into vocabulary space using the logit lens, then use these projections to trace how meanings propagate through attention and MLP sub-blocks. The paper first studies attention internals in GPT-style models, arguing that attention values and head outputs become interpretable when projected appropriately through the OV circuit, and that only a small number of high-norm heads and high-attention memory values dominate the semantic contribution of an attention block. Based on these observations, the authors build an interactive flow-graph visualization tool in which nodes correspond to hidden states or neurons and edges correspond to interactions such as attention weighting, neuron activation, or residual addition. They then illustrate the tool on GPT-2 case studies, including indirect object identification, layer norm behavior, and a proposed class of “regularization neurons.”

Overall, I found the paper interesting, creative, and potentially useful to the interpretability community, especially as a systems-and-analysis paper rather than a purely methodological one. The work sits at a productive intersection of mechanistic interpretability, representation probing, and visualization. The strongest aspect is that it tries to unify several strands of prior work—logit lens, OV/QK circuit interpretations, MLP neuron semantics, and interactive model inspection—into a single coherent interface for examining a forward pass. The visual examples are compelling. For instance, the attention-block visualization on page 5 and the IOI example on page 6 make the intended use case very concrete: the reader can see how a small number of heads and memory values appear to promote or suppress specific tokens. The paper is also commendably transparent about limitations, including the non-causal nature of the analysis and the dependence on projection-based semantics.

That said, I also have substantial reservations about the strength of the scientific claims relative to the evidence provided. The paper makes several interpretive claims that are suggestive but not yet convincingly established. In particular, the notions of “semantic information flow,” “layer norm as semantic filter,” and “regularization neurons” are intriguing hypotheses, but the current evidence is largely correlational, based on top-k token overlap under logit-lens projections and qualitative inspection of selected examples. As a result, I think the paper is valuable and promising, but some of its conclusions should be framed more cautiously, and the empirical validation would benefit from stronger causal or quantitative support.

The main technical contribution in Section 3 is the analysis of attention internals. I think this section contains the paper’s clearest and most useful findings. The comparison on page 4 between projecting attention heads with and without the WO projection is sensible and supports the claim that raw WV-space vectors are not directly interpretable in the model’s vocabulary space, whereas OV-projected head outputs are more aligned with downstream predictions. This is a nice empirical confirmation of a principle that is consistent with circuit-based interpretability. Likewise, the observation that only the top few heads by norm show meaningful overlap with the attention block output is plausible and practically useful for pruning the visualization. The memory-value analysis on page 5 is also intuitive: values receiving higher attention scores are more semantically aligned with the resulting head output. These findings together motivate the graph simplifications used later.

However, the quantitative metric used throughout, the top-k intersection score Ik, is fairly weak as a semantic similarity measure. The paper acknowledges this in the limitations, but the issue is central enough that it affects the interpretation of nearly all results. Top-k overlap can be unstable, insensitive to rank within the top-k, and blind to semantic equivalence among different but related tokens. It is also unclear how much of the observed overlap is driven by generic token-frequency biases or by the final layer norm and tied embedding geometry rather than by meaningful intermediate computation. The appendix partially addresses random baselines and shows some comparisons, but I would have liked a more systematic validation of Ik against alternative measures, such as rank correlation, KL divergence between projected distributions, cosine similarity in embedding space, or overlap after clustering semantically related tokens. Even a simple robustness analysis over different k values would strengthen confidence that the reported patterns are not artifacts of choosing k=50.

A related concern is that the paper often moves from “projection overlap exists” to “this component carries the semantics of token X” somewhat too quickly. Logit-lens projections are known to be informative but imperfect, especially in earlier layers and for vectors not naturally living in residual space. The authors are careful in some places—for example, they explicitly test whether WO is needed before projecting head outputs—but elsewhere the semantic interpretation seems more speculative. The discussion of QK-space in Appendix D.3 is actually a good example of appropriate caution: the authors find little alignment and offer multiple possible explanations. I would have liked that same level of caution in the main text when interpreting LN and regularization neurons.

The visualization contribution is appealing and likely to be useful in practice. The formalization of the graph construction in Appendix A is clear, and the examples are easy to follow. The figure on page 14, which walks through how GPT-2 small predicts “Argentina” from “Buenos Aires is the capital of,” is particularly effective as a demonstration of the tool’s intended workflow. The pruning strategy—keeping top attention-scored memory values, top-norm heads, and top-activated MLP neurons—is reasonable from a usability perspective. Still, from a scientific standpoint, this pruning introduces a strong inductive bias: the tool may visually confirm the hypothesis that a few dominant components matter most because it is designed to show only those components. The paper does acknowledge this risk in the limitations, but I think it deserves more emphasis in the main discussion. It would help to quantify how much predictive mass or attribution is retained after pruning, or to compare the visualization’s selected components against a causal importance measure. Without such validation, it is hard to know whether the graph is faithfully summarizing the computation or merely presenting a plausible sparse narrative.

The IOI case study is a nice sanity check. The comparison between the table from Wang et al. and the corresponding graph on page 6 is one of the paper’s strongest pieces of evidence that the tool can recover known circuits in an intuitive way. I appreciated that the authors do not overclaim replacement of causal analysis and instead position the tool as a way to accelerate qualitative discovery. That seems exactly right. Still, the evaluation remains anecdotal. Since the paper’s value proposition is partly that VISIT can help identify meaningful components, it would be useful to include a more systematic benchmark: for example, across a set of known heads or circuits from prior work, how often does the visualization surface them among the top displayed components? Or can human users identify known mechanisms faster or more accurately with VISIT than with existing tools such as exBERT or LM-Debugger? Even a small user study or task-based comparison would significantly strengthen the systems contribution.

The layer norm analysis in Section 5.2 is interesting but currently underdeveloped. The plots on page 7 and in Appendix E suggest that applying ln1 or ln2 tends to reduce the probability of common function words and increase the probability of more contentful tokens under the logit lens. This is an intriguing empirical pattern. But the conclusion that layer norm acts as a “semantic filter” feels stronger than what the evidence supports. Layer norm rescales and recenters activations; changes in projected token probabilities may arise from generic geometric effects rather than a dedicated semantic role. To support the semantic-filter interpretation, I would want to see controls that disentangle LN from simpler normalization operations, or analyses across prompts where the “content-word promotion” hypothesis makes falsifiable predictions. For example, does replacing learned LN parameters with identity or with statistics-only normalization preserve the effect? Are the promoted tokens specifically relevant to the context, or merely less frequent/high-variance directions in embedding space? The appendix tables on pages 18–20 are suggestive, but not yet conclusive.

The “regularization neurons” claim in Section 5.3 is perhaps the most speculative part of the paper. The observation itself—that some FF2 neurons in later layers are almost always highly activated, have high norms, high-entropy projections, and outlier weights contributing to rogue dimensions—is interesting. The plots on pages 7 and 8 support that these neurons are unusual. But calling them “regularization neurons” implies a functional role that is not demonstrated. They may indeed act as a kind of bias or stabilizing offset, but they could also be artifacts of basis choice, tied to token frequency, or simply a manifestation of known outlier-dimension phenomena. A stronger analysis would intervene on these neurons: ablate them, clamp them, or subtract their contribution and measure effects on perplexity, calibration, hidden-state anisotropy, or downstream semantic similarity. As written, the section is better viewed as an observational discovery of persistent high-activation neurons related to rogue dimensions, rather than a validated new functional category.

On novelty, I would characterize the paper as moderately novel in synthesis and tooling, and modestly novel in scientific insight. The individual ingredients are mostly known: logit lens, OV/QK circuits, MLP neuron interpretation, and transformer visualization tools all have precedents. The paper’s novelty lies in combining them into a unified flow-graph representation and extending projection-based analysis deeper into attention memory values. That is worthwhile. The scientific findings about OV projection necessity and dominant heads/values are useful but incremental. The LN and regularization-neuron observations are more novel, but also less substantiated. If accepted, I think the paper’s main lasting contribution would likely be the tool and the framing of forward-pass inspection as a semantic flow graph.

The paper is generally well written and easy to follow. The figures are central and mostly effective. Figure 1 on page 2 gives a good overview of the graph structure, and Figures 4, 6, and 11 are especially helpful in showing how to read the visualization. I also appreciated the detailed appendices, which explain graph construction and provide additional plots. One minor issue is that some terminology could be sharpened. For example, “semantic alignment” is defined operationally via top-k overlap, but the term itself suggests a stronger notion than the metric warrants. Similarly, “information flow” may be read causally or mechanistically, whereas the analysis is primarily descriptive and correlational. More careful wording would reduce the risk of overinterpretation.

I have several concrete suggestions that would substantially improve the paper. First, add stronger validation of the semantic metric: vary k, compare against alternative similarity measures, and include stronger baselines. Second, validate the pruning strategy quantitatively, ideally by measuring how much of the attention output or MLP output is reconstructed by the displayed components, or by comparing against causal importance scores. Third, tone down or better support the LN and regularization-neuron claims, perhaps by adding ablation/intervention experiments. Fourth, if feasible, include a small evaluation of the tool itself, either through known-circuit recovery or a user/task study. Fifth, clarify the scope of generalization: the appendix includes some GPT-2 XL evidence on page 15, which is helpful, but broader evidence across models or tasks would make the claims more convincing.

In summary, I think this is a thoughtful and potentially impactful interpretability paper with a compelling visualization tool and several interesting empirical observations. Its strengths are accessibility, integration of prior interpretability ideas, and intuitive qualitative demonstrations. Its main weakness is that several of the stronger scientific claims rest on correlational evidence from a somewhat fragile projection metric, without enough causal validation. I would be positive on the paper if the venue values interpretability tools and exploratory analysis, but more cautious if the bar is primarily for strong mechanistic claims. My overall assessment is that the work is promising and useful, but the paper should better separate well-supported findings from speculative interpretations.
