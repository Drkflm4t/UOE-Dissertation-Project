Summary: This paper studies semantic information flow inside GPT-style transformers using vocabulary-space projections (logit lens). The authors analyze attention values, heads, and related components, arguing that attention outputs can be interpreted semantically when projected through the OV circuit, and that only a small number of high-norm heads and top-attended memory values dominate the semantic contribution to the residual stream. Building on these observations, they introduce VISIT, an interactive flow-graph visualization tool that represents hidden states and neurons as nodes labeled by projected tokens and interactions as edges. Through case studies on GPT-2, the paper shows that the tool can qualitatively recover known circuits for indirect object identification, suggests that layer norms act as semantic filters, and identifies frequently activated MLP neurons interpreted as 'regularization neurons' related to rogue dimensions.

Strengths: Clear and interesting goal: making transformer internals more interpretable and accessible through a unified semantic-flow visualization.; Novel focus on dynamic attention memory values and their relation to attention outputs, extending prior logit-lens-style analyses beyond static weights and MLP outputs.; The empirical observations about OV-projected heads, dominance of top-norm heads, and alignment of top-attended values with head semantics are intuitive and supported by multiple plots across layers.; The visualization tool appears practically useful and more expressive than prior table-based or attention-only interfaces; figures on pages 5, 6, and 14 effectively illustrate how the tool can support qualitative analysis.; The indirect object identification case study is a good sanity check: the tool qualitatively recovers known Name Mover / Negative Name Mover behavior without requiring the same intervention-heavy methodology.; The paper is generally well written, with helpful background, implementation details, appendices, and discussion of limitations.; The observations about layer norm and always-active neurons are intriguing and may motivate further mechanistic work.

Weaknesses: Much of the paper's contribution is qualitative/tool-oriented, while several scientific claims are stronger than the evidence supports.; The central notion of 'semantic alignment' relies heavily on top-k token overlap under the basic logit lens, which is a crude proxy and may be unstable or misleading, especially in earlier layers or for components not naturally in vocabulary space.; The quantitative evaluation is limited: many analyses use only 100 CounterFact prompts that GPT-2-medium answers correctly, which is a narrow and potentially biased subset.; The paper does not provide rigorous validation that the pruned graph faithfully captures the truly important causal pathways; pruning by activation/norm is plausible but not sufficient.; Claims about layer norm acting as a semantic filter and about 'regularization neurons' are interesting but largely correlational and speculative, without causal interventions or ablations to verify function.; Comparison to prior visualization tools is mostly descriptive; there is no user study, task-based evaluation, or quantitative evidence that VISIT improves interpretability or research efficiency.; Generalization beyond GPT-2 family models is asserted but only lightly supported (some additional GPT-2 XL plots); broader architectural coverage is absent.; Some findings may partly restate known phenomena (e.g., dominant heads, outlier dimensions) in a new visualization framework rather than establishing fundamentally new mechanisms.

Soundness Issues: The use of logit-lens projections for intermediate components such as values, heads, and especially lower-level subcomponents assumes these vectors are meaningfully interpretable in vocabulary space; this is only partially justified. The paper itself finds that naive projection fails for some components and then selects circuit-based projections where they work better.; The main metric, top-k intersection I_k, ignores semantic similarity between different but related tokens and can overstate or understate alignment depending on tokenization and frequency effects.; The dataset filtering to prompts answered correctly by GPT-2-medium may bias the observed patterns toward cases where the model's internal representations are unusually clean or aligned with the final answer.; The IOI case study is presented as validation, but it is qualitative and post hoc; it does not establish that the graph identifies causally necessary components.; The interpretation of layer norm as introducing content words and suppressing function words is based on probability shifts before/after LN, but alternative explanations (e.g., rescaling effects interacting with the final decoding geometry) are not ruled out.; The 'regularization neuron' hypothesis is speculative: frequent activation, high norm, and high entropy do not by themselves demonstrate a regularization role during computation.; The pruning strategy may omit low-activation but causally important contributors, which the authors acknowledge; this limits the reliability of conclusions drawn from the displayed graph alone.
