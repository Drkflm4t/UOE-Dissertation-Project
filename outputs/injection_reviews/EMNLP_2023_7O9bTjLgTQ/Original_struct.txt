Summary: The paper introduces VISIT, a visualization and analysis framework for interpreting semantic information flow in GPT-style transformers using vocabulary-space projections (logit lens). The authors analyze attention internals by projecting dynamic memory values, head outputs, and other intermediate representations, arguing that: (i) attention values become semantically aligned with head outputs when projected through the OV circuit; (ii) only a small number of high-norm heads dominate the attention block’s semantic contribution; and (iii) top-attended memory values largely determine head semantics. Based on these observations, they build an interactive flow-graph tool that visualizes a forward pass at the level of hidden states and selected neurons. They demonstrate the tool on GPT-2 case studies, including indirect object identification, a qualitative analysis of layer norm as a semantic filter, and identification of frequently activated “regularization neurons.”

Strengths: The paper tackles an important and timely problem: making transformer internals more interpretable and accessible through visualization grounded in recent logit-lens-style analyses.; The proposed tool is intuitive and potentially useful for researchers, combining hidden states, attention memory values, selected neurons, residual connections, and projections into a single interactive flow graph.; The attention-focused analysis is a meaningful extension beyond prior work that mainly emphasized MLPs or static weights; in particular, examining dynamic memory values is novel and interesting.; The paper includes both conceptual analysis and a practical artifact (code/tool), which increases potential impact for exploratory interpretability research.; The case study on indirect object identification provides a plausible qualitative sanity check against prior mechanistic findings, suggesting the visualization can recover known patterns.; The paper is generally well organized, with helpful figures and appendices that explain the graph construction and implementation details.

Weaknesses: Much of the paper’s central evidence is correlational and qualitative; the claims about semantic information flow, selective gating, layer norm as a semantic filter, and regularization neurons are not validated with causal interventions.; The main quantitative metric, top-k token intersection Ik, is weak as a semantic similarity measure and can be heavily influenced by decoding biases, token frequency, and superficial overlap rather than true semantic equivalence.; The empirical setup is narrow: most quantitative analyses are on 100 CounterFact prompts that GPT-2-medium answers correctly, which introduces strong selection bias and limits generalizability.; Several conclusions are overstated relative to the evidence. For example, saying that only top heads or top memory values determine semantics is stronger than what the reported correlations justify.; The pruning strategy underlying the visualization assumes that high-activation/high-norm components are the most important, but this assumption is not rigorously validated and may hide crucial low-magnitude contributors.; The paper does not compare VISIT against alternative visualization or interpretability tools in any systematic user study or task-based evaluation, so its practical advantage remains anecdotal.; Some analyses rely on interpreting intermediate vectors with the basic logit lens even though the paper itself acknowledges known limitations of such projections, especially in earlier layers and non-residual spaces.; The discovered phenomena around layer norm and regularization neurons are intriguing but underdeveloped; they are presented as insights without sufficiently ruling out simpler explanations tied to normalization geometry or known outlier-dimension effects.

Methodological Flaws: The paper repeatedly infers semantic causation from projection alignment. High overlap in top-k projected tokens does not establish that a component causally contributes that meaning to downstream predictions.; The use of Ik as the primary quantitative measure is methodologically fragile. It ignores token probabilities beyond top-k membership, ignores synonymy/paraphrase, and can be distorted by common high-frequency tokens and shared decoding artifacts.; The dataset filtering to prompts answered correctly by GPT-2-medium creates a biased sample that may preferentially select cases where the proposed projection-based interpretation works well. Although Appendix D partially checks unfiltered prompts, the main claims still rest on a curated subset.; The claim that WV outputs 'operate in a different space' and WO 'adjusts it to the common embedded space' is plausible but not established by the reported evidence; observing better alignment after OV projection is insufficient to prove this scientific interpretation.; The statement in Appendix A.2 that the MLP nonlinearity 'affects the magnitude of each coefficient but not its sign' is not generally true for common GPT-2 activations such as GELU, which can change sign behavior in nontrivial ways. This weakens the formal graph description.; The layer norm analysis compares token probabilities before and after LN and concludes LN acts as a semantic filter, but this may simply reflect rescaling/recentering effects under the decoding map rather than a distinct semantic mechanism. No ablation or intervention isolates the cause.; The 'regularization neurons' claim is not empirically validated. Frequent activation, high norm, and high-entropy projections do not demonstrate a regularization role; no intervention shows that these neurons stabilize computation or act as a bias term.; The validation against Wang et al. (2022) is only qualitative. Recovering visually similar patterns in one IOI example is not enough to establish correctness of the method or equivalence to mechanistic circuit analysis.; There is no statistical testing, uncertainty quantification, or robustness analysis for many reported trends, making it difficult to assess whether observed differences are substantial or incidental.; The visualization evaluation is incomplete: there is no objective criterion for faithfulness, completeness, or usability, despite the tool being a central contribution.
