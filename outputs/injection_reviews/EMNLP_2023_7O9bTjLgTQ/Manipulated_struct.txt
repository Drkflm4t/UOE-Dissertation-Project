Summary: The paper introduces VISIT, a visualization and interpretation framework for transformer language models that uses vocabulary-space projections (primarily the logit lens) to trace semantic information flow through attention and MLP components during a single forward pass. The authors first analyze attention internals in GPT-2, arguing that attention values become semantically interpretable after the OV projection, that only a small number of high-norm heads dominate the attention block’s semantic contribution, and that top-attended memory values align with head semantics. Based on these observations, they construct an interactive flow-graph tool that prunes the computation graph to salient neurons/hidden states and labels nodes by projected tokens. They demonstrate the tool on GPT-2 case studies, including indirect object identification, a qualitative analysis of layer norm as a semantic filter, and the identification of frequently active 'regularization neurons' related to rogue dimensions.

Strengths: The paper tackles an important and timely problem: making transformer internals more interpretable and accessible beyond highly specialized mechanistic analyses.; The proposed VISIT tool is novel in combining token-projection-based interpretation with an interactive flow-graph representation at the level of hidden states and selected neurons, yielding a more holistic visualization than prior tools focused only on attention maps or layerwise tables.; The analysis of attention internals is interesting and reasonably well motivated: the paper distinguishes between raw value/head representations and their OV-projected forms, and empirically shows stronger semantic alignment only after WO/OV projection.; The paper provides a clear conceptual decomposition of semantic flow inside attention: query/key scoring selects memory values, a few dominant heads contribute most strongly, and these are then integrated into the residual stream.; The case study on indirect object identification is compelling as a qualitative validation: the visualization appears to recover patterns consistent with prior mechanistic findings while presenting them in a more intuitive form.; The work is unusually strong on presentation and usability. The figures and interactive graph design appear thoughtfully constructed, and the appendix gives substantial implementation detail that would help reproducibility and adoption.; The paper contributes potentially useful hypotheses about layer norm acting as a semantic filter and about always-active 'regularization neurons,' which may inspire follow-up mechanistic work.; The authors are appropriately transparent that the tool is primarily qualitative and that it does not replace causal analysis, which helps position the contribution honestly.

Weaknesses: Much of the evidence is correlational and based on token-overlap heuristics from the logit lens, so several interpretive claims are suggestive rather than firmly established.; The central semantic similarity metric, top-k token intersection Ik, is fairly crude and may miss semantically related but lexically different projections; this limits the precision of some conclusions.; The empirical scope is narrow: most quantitative analysis is on GPT-2-medium with 100 correctly answered CounterFact prompts, which raises questions about robustness across tasks, prompts, and model families.; Several headline findings, especially the claims about layer norm as a semantic filter and 'regularization neurons,' are based mainly on descriptive statistics and qualitative inspection rather than controlled interventions.; The pruning strategy assumes that high-activation/high-norm components are the most important, but this is not causally validated and could omit low-magnitude yet functionally critical components.; The paper sometimes moves from projection alignment to semantic/process claims somewhat quickly; for example, interpreting projected top tokens as the model's intermediate 'meaning' remains an imperfect proxy.

Methodological Flaws: The paper does not provide causal validation for its main interpretability claims. For example, after identifying dominant heads, memory values, or regularization neurons, it does not intervene on these components to test whether the predicted semantic role actually changes model behavior.; The use of Ik based on top-50 token overlap as the primary quantitative measure is a weak proxy for semantic alignment. It is sensitive to arbitrary k, ignores token probabilities below the cutoff, and conflates lexical overlap with semantic similarity. This weakens the strength of conclusions drawn from small absolute differences in Ik.; The dataset filtering procedure may bias the analysis: selecting only CounterFact prompts that GPT-2-medium answers correctly (about 8% according to the appendix) focuses on unusually favorable cases and may overstate the clarity of the observed mechanisms. Although the appendix includes an unfiltered comparison, the main claims still rely heavily on the filtered setting.; The claim that WO 'adjusts' WV outputs into the common embedding space is plausible but not rigorously established; the observed increase in projection alignment after OV projection could also arise because WO is simply the pathway through which those vectors affect logits, not necessarily because pre-WO vectors are intrinsically semantically uninterpretable.; The layer norm analysis may conflate effects of normalization, learned affine parameters, and the final decoding pipeline. Showing token probability shifts before/after LN is not sufficient to conclude that LN functions as a semantic filter in a mechanistic sense.; The 'regularization neurons' claim is under-validated. Frequent activation, high norm, and high-entropy projections do not by themselves demonstrate a regularization role; alternative explanations such as architectural bias, token-frequency effects, or scaling artifacts are not ruled out.; The flow-graph pruning can introduce confirmation bias: by visualizing only top-scoring components, the tool may produce coherent narratives even when omitted components materially contribute. This is acknowledged by the authors, but it remains a substantive methodological limitation for scientific interpretation.
