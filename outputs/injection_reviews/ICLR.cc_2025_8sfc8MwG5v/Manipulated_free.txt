This paper studies an important and timely problem at the intersection of interpretability, foundation models, and robustness under deployment shift. The core question is whether concept bottleneck models built on top of foundation model representations can remain both interpretable and accurate after deployment when the test distribution changes. The paper argues, convincingly, that simply inserting a concept bottleneck on top of a strong foundation model does not automatically confer robustness, and that concept-based pipelines can fail in distinct ways under low-level and concept-level shifts. To address this, the authors propose CONDA, a source-free test-time adaptation framework that adapts the concept bank, the concept-to-label predictor, and an additional residual concept bottleneck using only unlabeled target data. Overall, I found the paper to be strong, well-motivated, and technically interesting, with a clear empirical story and a compelling practical message.

The main strength of the paper is that it identifies a genuinely underexplored problem. There is substantial recent work on concept bottleneck models and substantial work on test-time adaptation, but very little on adapting concept bottlenecks themselves after deployment, especially in the setting where the backbone is a frozen foundation model and the source data are unavailable. This is a natural and practically relevant setting. The paper’s framing is therefore valuable in its own right: it highlights that interpretability mechanisms are not static artifacts, but components that may themselves need adaptation under shift. That is a useful conceptual contribution.

I also appreciated the paper’s decomposition of failure modes. In Sections 2.2 and 2.3, the authors distinguish low-level shifts, concept-level shifts, and incomplete concept sets, and connect these to three corresponding failure modes: non-robust concept mappings, non-robust concept-level classifiers, and insufficient concept coverage. This taxonomy is intuitive and helps motivate the three-stage design of CONDA. Even if some of the formalism is somewhat idealized, the decomposition gives the method a coherent structure rather than presenting adaptation as a monolithic heuristic. This is one of the paper’s strongest aspects.

Methodologically, CONDA is well designed. The three components are sensible and complementary. Concept-Score Alignment adapts the concept vectors so that target-domain concept scores better match source-domain class-conditional statistics; Linear Probing Adaptation updates the concept-to-label layer using pseudo-labels while encouraging sparsity; and the Residual Concept Bottleneck adds new concepts to compensate for incompleteness of the original concept bank. This modularity is appealing because it maps directly onto the identified failure modes. The overview figure on page 6 is particularly helpful in clarifying how the source CBM is transformed into the adapted main branch plus residual branch. The method is also reasonably lightweight in spirit: the foundation model remains frozen, and only shallow concept-related components are adapted.

The empirical evaluation is broad and generally convincing. The paper evaluates across multiple datasets representing different shift types: CIFAR10-C and CIFAR100-C for low-level corruptions, Waterbirds and Metashift for concept-level shifts, and Camelyon17 for natural medical-domain shift. It also tests three different CBM construction paradigms: post-hoc concept bottlenecks, unsupervised learned concepts, and label-free concept bottlenecks. This breadth is a major positive. It suggests that the proposed adaptation framework is not narrowly tailored to one specific CBM instantiation. Table 1 on page 11 is especially informative: it shows that unadapted CBMs can indeed degrade substantially under shift, and that CONDA often recovers a large fraction of the lost performance, sometimes bringing concept-based prediction close to or beyond zero-shot and linear-probing baselines. The gains on Waterbirds for the post-hoc CBM and on CIFAR10/CIFAR100 for several settings are notable.

The component-wise analysis is another strong point. Figure 3 on page 12 supports the paper’s central claim that different adaptation components matter for different shift types: CSA is most useful for low-level shifts, while LPA and RCB matter more for concept-level shifts. This is exactly the kind of evidence needed to justify a multi-stage method. I also liked that the paper does not merely report aggregate gains, but tries to explain why those gains arise. The interpretability analysis in Figures 4 and 8 is similarly useful. On Waterbirds, the adapted concept weights shift from background-dominated associations toward more target-relevant patterns, and the residual concepts appear to recover bird-related attributes such as feathers, wings, and beak. Even if automated concept annotation is imperfect, this qualitative evidence strengthens the paper’s claim that adaptation improves not only accuracy but also alignment of explanations with the target domain.

The appendices are substantial and add credibility. The runtime analysis and Table 3 suggest the method is computationally manageable. The hyperparameter ablations in Figure 5 are helpful, especially in showing that the role of the Frobenius regularizer differs across shift types. The pseudo-labeling ablation in Table 5 is also valuable because it makes explicit that pseudo-label quality is a key bottleneck and that stronger pseudo-labeling can further improve results. I also appreciated that the paper includes negative results in Table 8 and a limitations section, rather than presenting the method as universally successful.

That said, there are several issues that should be addressed or at least clarified.

My main concern is the dependence on pseudo-label quality and, more broadly, on the strength of the non-interpretable foundation-model predictors used to generate those pseudo-labels. In practice, CONDA relies on an ensemble of zero-shot and linear-probing predictions from the same backbone, and the paper explicitly acknowledges that when these are poor, adaptation can fail. This raises an important question about what exactly is being adapted: is CONDA primarily transferring the robustness of the feature-based predictor into the concept-based pipeline, rather than discovering a genuinely concept-level adaptation principle? That is not necessarily a flaw, but the paper should discuss this more directly. In some sense, the method’s success may be upper-bounded by the quality of the black-box predictor used as a teacher. This is especially relevant because one of the paper’s headline claims is that CONDA can align CBM performance with non-interpretable classification; if the pseudo-label source is already strong, this becomes more plausible, but also somewhat less surprising.

Relatedly, the comparison protocol could be sharpened. The paper compares adapted CBMs to zero-shot and linear probing, but it would be useful to compare against standard source-free or test-time adaptation baselines applied directly to the feature-based classifier, not only to the CBM. The paper states that improving the FM itself is not the focus, which is fair, but from a systems perspective the relevant question is whether one should deploy CONDA rather than a standard TTA method on the feature-based model. Some of the current comparisons suggest parity with LP or ZS after adaptation, but a stronger baseline would be LP plus a competitive TTA method. Without that, it is hard to assess the cost-benefit tradeoff of interpretability versus direct adaptation.

A second concern is the theoretical formalization. The taxonomy of shifts is useful, but some definitions feel stronger than necessary or somewhat stylized. For example, defining concept-level shift as one where the concept distribution changes but the prediction distribution remains unchanged is conceptually neat, yet real-world shifts may alter both in more entangled ways. Likewise, the completeness definition is intuitive but not operational. I do not think this undermines the paper, since the method is empirical and the formalism is mainly motivational, but the authors should be careful not to overstate the rigor of these definitions. It may be better to present them as idealized abstractions rather than exhaustive characterizations of deployment shift.

A third issue is that the source-free setting still assumes access to source-domain concept-score statistics, namely class-conditional Gaussian means and covariances for CSA. This is a reasonable assumption and much weaker than retaining the source data, but it should be emphasized more clearly in the problem statement and discussion. In some applications, storing these statistics may be easy; in others, especially with many classes or privacy constraints, even this may require justification. The method is not fully source-agnostic in the strictest sense.

There are also some questions about the interpretability claims. The paper argues that CONDA yields concept-based interpretations better aligned with the target data, and the qualitative examples are promising. However, the evaluation of interpretability remains mostly anecdotal or proxy-based. The automatic annotation procedure in Appendix B.2 is clever, but it is still an indirect measure. It would strengthen the paper to include a more systematic human-centered or task-grounded evaluation of whether the adapted concepts are indeed more meaningful, less spurious, or more actionable. For example, one could quantify overlap with known causal attributes in Waterbirds or use intervention-based metrics. As it stands, the interpretability evidence is suggestive rather than definitive.

I also found some of the empirical outcomes a bit mixed, though the paper is commendably transparent about this. In Table 1, CONDA does not uniformly improve every CBM baseline on every dataset; for example, some adapted results for the Yeh et al. model are worse than the unadapted model. The limitations section explains this reasonably well, especially when the unadapted CBM already outperforms the pseudo-label teacher. Still, this suggests that the method may need a confidence-aware gating mechanism or a criterion for deciding when not to adapt, or which components to activate. The component analysis already hints that not all stages are beneficial under all shifts. A practical extension would be to infer the shift type online and selectively enable CSA, LPA, or RCB.

There are a few smaller technical and presentation issues. The Gaussian assumption for class-conditional concept scores in CSA is convenient, but it would be useful to know how sensitive the method is to this modeling choice. The paper cites CAFA-style alignment, but concept scores may be highly non-Gaussian, especially for sparse or semantically heterogeneous concepts. Also, the notation in Section 2 is somewhat dense relative to the rest of the paper, and some readers may find the measure-theoretic framing heavier than necessary. Finally, while the appendices are detailed, the main paper could benefit from a slightly clearer summary of how hyperparameters are selected and whether any target-domain tuning is involved.

Despite these concerns, I view the paper very positively. The problem is important, the proposed solution is novel and thoughtfully structured, and the empirical evidence is substantial. The work makes a meaningful contribution by showing that concept bottlenecks should not be treated as static interpretability wrappers around foundation models, but as adaptive components that can and should evolve under deployment shift. This is a valuable insight for both the interpretability and robustness communities. The paper is also constructive in spirit: it does not merely diagnose failures of CBMs under shift, but offers a practical remedy that often works well across diverse settings.

For improvement, I would encourage the authors to do three things in a revision. First, strengthen the baseline section by comparing against direct TTA on feature-based predictors and by clarifying the role of pseudo-label quality. Second, temper and refine the interpretability claims, ideally with one more quantitative evaluation beyond qualitative visualizations and automatic annotation scores. Third, clarify the assumptions behind CSA, especially the need for stored source-domain concept statistics and the implications of the Gaussian model.

In summary, this is a strong paper with clear novelty, solid experiments, and an appealing central idea. It opens up an important new direction on adaptive interpretability for foundation-model pipelines under distribution shift. I believe it would be a worthwhile contribution to a top-tier ML venue.
