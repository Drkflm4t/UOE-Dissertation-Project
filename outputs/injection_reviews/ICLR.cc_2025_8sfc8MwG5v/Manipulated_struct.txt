Summary: The paper studies test-time adaptation for concept bottleneck models (CBMs) built on top of foundation model features under distribution shift. It argues that concept-based pipelines are not inherently robust after deployment, identifies three failure modes (non-robust concept bottleneck under low-level shift, non-robust classifier under concept-level shift, and incomplete concept sets), and proposes CONDA, a three-stage source-free adaptation framework using only unlabeled target data: Concept-Score Alignment (CSA), Linear Probing Adaptation (LPA), and a Residual Concept Bottleneck (RCB). Experiments across CIFAR10/100-C, Waterbirds, MetaShift, and Camelyon17 with multiple CBM constructions and FM backbones show substantial target-domain gains in many settings, sometimes bringing CBM performance close to or above non-interpretable baselines while also yielding qualitatively adapted concept explanations.

Strengths: Timely and important problem: the paper addresses the underexplored intersection of interpretability, foundation models, and test-time adaptation under distribution shift.; Clear decomposition of failure modes for CBM deployment, which provides a useful conceptual framework for thinking about when concept bottlenecks fail after deployment.; Technically modular method: CSA, LPA, and RCB are well motivated and each targets a distinct failure mode, making the overall framework easy to understand and analyze.; Strong empirical breadth: evaluations span multiple datasets, multiple shift types (low-level, concept-level, natural), multiple CBM constructions, and multiple FM backbones.; Results are often compelling, with large gains over unadapted CBMs in several settings and competitive performance relative to zero-shot / linear-probing baselines.; The paper includes useful ablations on component contributions, hyperparameters, pseudo-labeling quality, runtime, and interpretability analyses.; Interpretability analysis is a meaningful addition rather than an afterthought; the visualizations on Waterbirds and MetaShift suggest that adaptation changes concept reliance in intuitively sensible ways.; The source-free setting is practically relevant, and the method avoids requiring source data at adaptation time.; The paper is generally well organized, with detailed appendices, algorithm pseudocode, complexity discussion, and explicit acknowledgment of limitations.

Weaknesses: The formalization of distribution shifts is somewhat idealized and occasionally disconnected from the actual empirical setting; in practice, many shifts may not cleanly satisfy the stated equalities over concept or prediction distributions.; The method depends heavily on pseudo-label quality from zero-shot / linear-probing FM predictions, which limits robustness when the backbone is weak or mismatched; the paper acknowledges this and even shows negative cases.; Some empirical outcomes are mixed: on Waterbirds and for certain CBM constructions, adaptation can substantially hurt performance, so the method is not uniformly reliable across all settings.; Comparisons are mostly against unadapted CBMs and non-interpretable FM baselines; there is limited direct comparison to broader source-free / test-time adaptation baselines adapted to the same CBM setting.; Interpretability claims are largely qualitative; there is no rigorous human evaluation or quantitative faithfulness metric showing that adapted concepts are more semantically valid or causally useful.; The source-domain Gaussian statistics for concept scores are assumed available during adaptation, which is a nontrivial requirement and may not always be preserved in realistic source-free deployment pipelines.

Methodological Flaws: There is a scientific-logic mismatch in the definition of 'concept-level shift' (Eq. 3): it assumes the prediction distribution remains unchanged across domains while concept distributions change, but the empirical benchmarks (e.g., Waterbirds/MetaShift) are precisely settings where deployed classifiers often fail because spurious concept-label correlations change. Thus the formal definition describes an idealized property of a complete/robust concept representation rather than the actual observed shift mechanism.; Definition 1 and the completeness discussion are not operationalized. The paper defines completeness via existence of a classifier g that equalizes prediction distributions across source and target, but this is not testable from unlabeled target data and is not connected to a measurable criterion used by the algorithm.; The CSA objective relies on class-conditional Gaussian modeling of source concept scores and Mahalanobis distances. This assumption is not empirically validated; if concept scores are multimodal or poorly estimated, the alignment objective may be mis-specified. The mixed results on some datasets are consistent with this concern.; Pseudo-label selection by taking the more confident prediction between zero-shot and linear probing is heuristic and lacks calibration analysis. Since all three adaptation stages optimize against these pseudo-labels, systematic pseudo-label bias can directly reinforce errors.; The claim that CONDA can produce interpretations 'better aligned with the test data' is plausible but not rigorously established. Automatic concept annotation and Sankey-style weight visualizations do not by themselves verify semantic correctness or faithfulness.; The complexity analysis in the appendix appears informal and operation-count based rather than a rigorous asymptotic derivation; while acceptable as engineering guidance, it should not be interpreted as a formal computational guarantee.
