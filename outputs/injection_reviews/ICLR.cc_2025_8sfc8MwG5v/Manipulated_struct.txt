Summary: This paper studies concept bottleneck models (CBMs) built on top of foundation-model features under test-time distribution shift. The authors argue that although FM-based CBMs can be accurate and interpretable on the source domain, they can fail badly after deployment under both low-level shifts (e.g., corruptions) and concept-level shifts (e.g., changed background/semantic correlations). They identify three failure modes: non-robust concept mappings under low-level shift, non-robust concept-to-label classifiers under concept-level shift, and incomplete concept sets. To address these, they propose CONDA, a source-free test-time adaptation framework that uses only unlabeled target data and a frozen FM backbone. CONDA has three stages: concept-score alignment (CSA) to adapt concept vectors using class-conditional Gaussian statistics from the source domain, linear probing adaptation (LPA) to adapt the concept-to-label layer using pseudo-labels, and a residual concept bottleneck (RCB) to add new concepts when the original concept bank is incomplete. Experiments across CIFAR10/100-C, Waterbirds, Metashift, and Camelyon17 with multiple CBM construction methods show substantial gains in many settings, sometimes bringing CBM performance close to or above non-interpretable zero-shot or linear-probe baselines. The paper also includes interpretability analyses showing that concept weights and residual concepts adapt in intuitively meaningful ways under shift.

Strengths: Timely and important problem: making FM-based classifiers both interpretable and robust under deployment-time distribution shift is highly relevant.; Clear decomposition of failure modes for CBMs under shift, separating low-level shift, concept-level shift, and incomplete concept sets.; Technically modular method: CSA, LPA, and RCB are well motivated and map naturally onto the identified failure modes.; Strong empirical breadth: evaluation spans five datasets, several shift types, multiple FM backbones, and three different CBM construction approaches.; Results are often substantial; Table 1 shows notable target-domain gains, especially on low-level shifts and several concept-level/natural-shift settings.; Useful ablations analyze the role of each component and hyperparameters, and the paper includes runtime analysis and negative results.; Interpretability analysis goes beyond accuracy, with visualizations on Waterbirds and Metashift showing adapted concept-to-class mappings and residual concepts that appear semantically meaningful.; The source-free adaptation setting is practically relevant, and the method does not require access to source data during deployment.

Weaknesses: The theoretical formalization is somewhat loose and does not lead to strong guarantees; several assumptions are introduced mainly to motivate the method rather than rigorously justify it.; CSA relies on access to source-domain class-conditional concept statistics and assumes Gaussian structure in concept space; the sensitivity of the method to misspecified statistics is not deeply analyzed.; The pseudo-labeling strategy depends heavily on the robustness of the underlying FM and linear probe; the paper itself shows failure cases when pseudo-label quality is poor.; Some empirical claims are uneven: CONDA does not consistently improve all CBM variants, and in some cases CSA alone or LPA alone outperforms the full method, suggesting the combined pipeline may not always be optimal.; Comparisons are limited mostly to unadapted CBMs and non-interpretable ZS/LP baselines; there is little direct comparison to broader TTA baselines adapted to concept-based pipelines or to simpler adaptation alternatives.; Interpretability evaluation is largely qualitative; there is no strong quantitative human-centered metric showing that adapted concepts are more faithful or more useful to users.; The method introduces several stages and hyperparameters, which may complicate deployment and tuning despite the reported modest runtime.; The notion of concept-set completeness is intuitive but not operationalized in a way that can be tested or estimated directly.

Soundness Issues: The definition of low-level shift as preserving the concept-score distribution and concept-level shift as preserving prediction distribution is idealized and may not match real data-generating processes; this weakens the formal framing.; CSA assumes source class-conditional concept distributions are Gaussian and that matching Mahalanobis-based distances with pseudo-labels is sufficient for alignment; this is heuristic and not theoretically validated.; Because pseudo-labels are produced using zero-shot and linear-probe predictions from the same FM family, adaptation may inherit or amplify their biases under shift.; The source-free setting still requires retained source-side summary statistics (means/covariances) and a source-trained linear probe for pseudo-labeling; this is reasonable, but should be framed carefully as not entirely source-information-free.; The residual concept bottleneck improves expressivity, but adding randomly initialized residual concepts and optimizing them with pseudo-labels may trade off interpretability for accuracy; the paper discusses this partially but does not fully resolve it.; Some conclusions about interpretability are based on automatic concept annotation and visual inspection rather than rigorous validation of concept faithfulness or stability.
