Summary: The paper studies test-time adaptation for concept bottleneck models (CBMs) built on top of foundation model embeddings under distribution shift. It argues that CBMs are not inherently robust after deployment, identifies three failure modes (non-robust concept bottleneck under low-level shift, non-robust classifier under concept-level shift, and incomplete concept set), and proposes CONDA, a three-stage source-free adaptation framework using only unlabeled target data: Concept-Score Alignment (CSA) to adapt concept vectors via class-conditional Gaussian alignment in concept space, Linear Probing Adaptation (LPA) to adapt the concept-to-label linear layer using pseudo-labels, and a Residual Concept Bottleneck (RCB) to add new concepts for incomplete concept banks. Experiments across CIFAR10/100-C, Waterbirds, Metashift, and Camelyon17 with multiple CBM constructions and FM backbones show substantial gains in many settings, sometimes bringing CBM performance close to or above zero-shot/linear-probe baselines, and the paper includes qualitative analyses of adapted concept explanations.

Strengths: Addresses a timely and underexplored problem: post-deployment adaptation of interpretable CBMs built on foundation models under distribution shift.; Clear decomposition of failure modes and a modular method design where CSA, LPA, and RCB are intended to target different causes of degradation.; Empirical evaluation spans multiple datasets, multiple shift types (low-level, concept-level, natural), multiple FM backbones, and multiple CBM construction methods.; Results in Table 1 show strong improvements in several practically relevant settings, especially for post-hoc CBMs on CIFAR10-C, Waterbirds, Metashift, and Camelyon17.; Ablation analyses are reasonably informative: Figure 3 and Appendix D help support the claim that different components matter under different shift types.; The paper goes beyond accuracy and includes qualitative interpretability analyses (Figures 4 and 8) and residual-concept annotation attempts.; The method is source-free at adaptation time and does not require target labels, which is a practically meaningful setting.; The appendix is fairly detailed, including algorithmic description, runtime measurements, hyperparameters, and negative results/limitations.

Weaknesses: The theoretical framing of distribution shifts is not rigorous enough to support the claims made later; several definitions are informal or unrealistic for real deployment settings.; The method depends critically on pseudo-label quality from zero-shot/linear-probe FM predictions, so gains may largely reflect the strength of those non-interpretable predictors rather than intrinsic CBM adaptation.; The source-free setting is weakened by assuming access to class-conditional Gaussian statistics of source concept scores; these statistics may be nontrivial to store/share and are effectively extra source information.; Empirical gains are inconsistent across CBM variants: for Yeh et al. (2020), CONDA often hurts performance, and on Waterbirds the post-hoc CBM baseline remains far below LP despite adaptation.; Interpretability claims are suggestive but not rigorously evaluated; there is no human study, no faithfulness metric, and no quantitative assessment that adapted concepts remain semantically stable or causally meaningful.; Comparison to broader TTA baselines is limited. The paper does not compare against applying standard TTA directly to the FM feature classifier and then distilling/explaining, or against simpler CBM adaptation baselines.; Some experimental choices appear tuned to favor the method, e.g., using CIFAR-C severity 2 because higher severities break pseudo-labeling, which narrows the robustness claim.; The residual concept mechanism increases model complexity and may dilute interpretability, but the tradeoff is only lightly explored.

Methodological Flaws: The formal definition of low-level shift as preserving the concept-score distribution exactly (Eq. 2) is too strong and not aligned with realistic corruptions; in practice even benign low-level perturbations change FM embeddings and concept scores. This makes the taxonomy more definitional than explanatory.; The definition of concept-level shift in Eq. 3 assumes prediction distributions remain unchanged across domains, but this is stated at the marginal distribution level rather than conditional on labels or semantics. Equal prediction distributions do not imply preserved task structure, so the scientific logic behind the taxonomy is weak.; Definition 1 of a 'complete' concept set is problematic: existence of some classifier g such that source and target prediction distributions match is not a meaningful or identifiable notion of concept completeness. Matching marginal prediction distributions can occur for incorrect reasons and does not guarantee sufficiency for label prediction.; CSA assumes class-conditional concept scores are Gaussian and that source-domain class means/covariances are available. There is no empirical validation of the Gaussian assumption, no sensitivity analysis to covariance estimation quality, and no justification that Mahalanobis alignment in concept space is appropriate across all CBM constructions.; The adaptation objective in CSA uses pseudo-labels generated from FM-based predictors while optimizing concept vectors to match source statistics. If pseudo-labels are wrong, CSA can reinforce incorrect alignments; this failure mode is acknowledged but not adequately mitigated.; The pseudo-labeling protocol selects the prediction with higher confidence between zero-shot and linear probing. Confidence across these two predictors may not be calibrated or comparable, so this heuristic lacks methodological justification.; The evaluation does not cleanly isolate whether improvements come from concept adaptation versus simply inheriting the FM classifier's pseudo-label biases. A stronger control would compare against directly replacing the CBM with the pseudo-label teacher or adapting a non-concept linear head under the same pseudo-labels.; The claim that CONDA yields concept-based interpretations 'better aligned with the test data' is not empirically validated with a formal metric. The qualitative Sankey-style visualizations on pages 12 and 31 are illustrative but insufficient to substantiate interpretability improvements.; The paper's source-free claim is somewhat overstated because it requires source-trained CBM parameters, a source-trained linear probe for pseudo-labeling, and source class-conditional concept statistics for CSA.; Some reported conclusions overgeneralize from selective settings. For example, the main text emphasizes broad effectiveness, but Table 1 and Appendix F show several failures and degradations, especially for certain CBM constructions and when pseudo-labels are weak.
