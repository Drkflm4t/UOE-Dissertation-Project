Summary: The paper studies test-time adaptation for concept bottleneck models (CBMs) built on top of foundation model features under distribution shift. The authors argue that CBMs are not inherently robust after deployment, even when they are interpretable and competitive on the source domain. They categorize failure modes into: non-robust concept bottlenecks under low-level shifts, non-robust concept-to-label classifiers under concept-level shifts, and incomplete concept sets. To address these, they propose CONDA, a source-free, unlabeled test-time adaptation framework with three stages: Concept-Score Alignment (CSA), Linear Probing Adaptation (LPA), and a Residual Concept Bottleneck (RCB) that adds new concepts. Experiments on CIFAR10/100-C, Waterbirds, MetaShift, and Camelyon17 with several CBM construction methods show substantial target-domain gains in many settings, sometimes bringing CBM performance close to or above non-interpretable zero-shot/linear-probing baselines. The paper also includes interpretability analyses showing that adapted concept weights and residual concepts better reflect target-domain correlations.

Strengths: Addresses an important and timely problem: making foundation-model-based prediction pipelines both interpretable and adaptable under deployment-time distribution shifts.; Novel problem formulation: test-time adaptation for CBMs with a frozen foundation model and no source data appears to be a meaningful new setting.; Clear decomposition of failure modes into concept misalignment, classifier mismatch, and concept incompleteness, which motivates the three-part method design.; Method is modular and intuitive: CSA targets low-level shifts, LPA targets concept-to-label remapping, and RCB targets missing concepts.; Strong empirical breadth: evaluated across multiple datasets, shift types, backbones, and three different CBM construction approaches.; Results are often compelling, with large gains on several benchmarks; e.g., substantial improvements on CIFAR10-C and Waterbirds for some CBM variants, and competitive performance relative to feature-based baselines.; Useful ablations analyze the role of each component and hyperparameters, and runtime analysis suggests the method is practical.; Interpretability analysis goes beyond accuracy and shows how concept weights and residual concepts change after adaptation in ways that align with dataset shifts.

Weaknesses: The theoretical formalization of shift types is somewhat idealized and not tightly connected to the actual adaptation guarantees; the paper does not provide formal guarantees that CSA/LPA/RCB recover the desired invariances.; The method depends heavily on pseudo-label quality from zero-shot/linear-probing predictors; when those are weak, adaptation can fail, as acknowledged in the limitations and negative results.; CSA assumes access to source-domain class-conditional Gaussian statistics of concept scores, which may be a nontrivial artifact to store or estimate robustly, and the sensitivity of the method to misspecified statistics is not deeply studied.; Some empirical results are mixed: CONDA degrades performance for certain CBM/dataset combinations, especially with the Yeh et al. baseline or some concept-level shifts, so the method is not uniformly beneficial.; Comparisons are somewhat narrow with respect to broader TTA baselines; the paper mainly compares unadapted CBMs and non-interpretable ZS/LP, rather than adapting strong feature-based TTA methods under matched settings.; Interpretability claims are suggestive but still qualitative; there is limited quantitative evaluation of whether adapted or residual concepts are truly more faithful, stable, or human-meaningful.; The online batch adaptation protocol may be sensitive to batch order/composition, but this is not extensively analyzed.; The residual concept branch increases model complexity and may trade off interpretability for accuracy; this is discussed but not fully resolved.

Soundness Issues: The definition of low-level shift assumes unchanged concept-score distributions, but in practice the whole motivation is that deployed CBMs often violate this assumption; thus the formal categorization is more descriptive than operational, and the bridge from definitions to optimization objectives is heuristic.; CSA models class-conditional concept scores as Gaussians and minimizes a log intra/inter Mahalanobis ratio using pseudo-labels. This is a plausible heuristic, but there is no evidence that the Gaussian assumption holds across datasets or CBM types, nor analysis of robustness to covariance estimation error.; The pseudo-labeling scheme uses an ensemble of zero-shot and linear-probing predictions trained with source supervision. This may inject non-interpretable model biases into the adaptation target, making it unclear whether improvements come from better concept adaptation or simply distillation from stronger feature-based predictors.; Because adaptation is evaluated on the same incoming test batches used for optimization, the protocol follows standard online TTA practice, but it can blur the distinction between adaptation and evaluation; robustness to different stream orders or episodic resets is not fully explored.; RCB introduces randomly initialized residual concepts optimized with cross-entropy plus similarity/coherency regularizers. While effective empirically, there is limited evidence that these learned residual vectors consistently correspond to semantically coherent concepts rather than generic discriminative directions.; The paper claims improved interpretability, but there is no human study or formal faithfulness metric validating that the adapted concepts are more trustworthy rather than merely more predictive.
