This paper studies test-time adaptation for concept bottleneck models built on top of foundation-model representations. The central claim is that concept-based pipelines are not inherently robust under distribution shift, even when the underlying foundation model features remain useful, and that one can recover both accuracy and better-aligned concept explanations by adapting the concept bank and classifier using only unlabeled target data. The proposed method, CONDA, combines three stages: concept-score alignment (CSA), linear probing adaptation (LPA), and a residual concept bottleneck (RCB) that adds new concepts at test time. Empirically, the paper evaluates across several datasets with low-level, concept-level, and natural shifts, and reports substantial gains over unadapted CBMs, in some cases bringing concept-based performance close to or above feature-based baselines.

Overall, I found the paper interesting and timely. The topic is important: concept bottlenecks are often motivated by interpretability and sometimes by presumed robustness, but the deployment-time behavior of CBMs under shift has been underexplored. The paper’s framing is compelling, especially the observation in Figure 1 on page 2 that concept-based predictions can degrade sharply under both corruption-style and semantic/contextual shifts. The attempt to connect different failure modes to different adaptation mechanisms is also a strength. At a high level, this is a meaningful contribution that could be of interest to both the interpretability and robustness/TTA communities.

The strongest aspect of the paper is the problem formulation and motivation. The authors clearly articulate that there are at least three distinct ways a deployed CBM can fail: the concept mapping itself may be non-robust to low-level perturbations, the concept-to-label classifier may fail under concept-level shifts, and the concept set may simply be incomplete. This decomposition in Section 2.3 is intuitive and useful. It gives the method a principled modular structure rather than presenting adaptation as a monolithic heuristic. I also appreciated that the paper does not overclaim that concepts are automatically robust; instead it explicitly challenges that assumption.

A second major strength is the breadth of empirical evaluation. Table 1 on page 11 covers multiple datasets, multiple shift types, multiple backbones, and three different CBM construction methods. That breadth matters because the proposed method is intended as a general adaptation framework rather than a tweak for one specific CBM instantiation. The results are mixed in places, but that actually increases credibility. In particular, the paper does not hide negative cases, and Appendix F explicitly discusses settings where adaptation can hurt when pseudo-labels are poor or when the unadapted CBM already exceeds the feature-based pseudo-labeling sources. This honesty is appreciated.

The interpretability analysis is also a positive. The Sankey-style visualizations in Figures 4 and 8 (pages 12 and 31) are helpful for showing how concept-to-class weights shift after adaptation. On Waterbirds, the emergence of bird-related residual concepts such as “feathers,” “wings,” and “beak” is intuitively appealing, and the discussion in Section 4.4 gives a plausible story for how RCB can compensate for missing source concepts. The additional analysis in Appendix E, especially the confidence/consistency plots in Figure 6 on page 29 and the accuracy-interpretability tradeoff in Figure 7 on page 30, is thoughtful and goes beyond raw accuracy reporting.

That said, I have several substantive concerns, mostly around methodological assumptions, fairness of comparisons, and the strength of the evidence for the interpretability claims.

My main concern is that the method relies heavily on pseudo-labels derived from non-interpretable feature-based predictors, specifically an ensemble of zero-shot and linear-probing predictions from the foundation model. This is described in Section 3 and on page 7. In practice, this means the adaptation signal comes from the very black-box pipeline that the paper is trying to replace with an interpretable one. That is not necessarily invalid, but it weakens the conceptual claim that the CBM is being adapted “solely” from unlabeled target data in a self-contained way. More importantly, it raises a fairness issue in the headline comparison that CONDA can “align CBM performance with non-interpretable classification.” If the adaptation target is itself derived from those non-interpretable classifiers, then matching them is less surprising. The paper should be much clearer that CONDA is effectively distilling target-domain behavior from feature-based predictors into a concept-based model at test time. Framed that way, the contribution is still interesting, but more precise.

Relatedly, the pseudo-labeling design creates a ceiling and a dependency that complicate interpretation of the results. In several datasets, the adapted CBM approaches LP or ZS performance, but this may simply reflect the quality of the pseudo-label oracle rather than the intrinsic effectiveness of concept adaptation. Appendix D partially acknowledges this and Table 5 on page 28 shows a large gap between current pseudo-labeling and perfect pseudo-labels, especially on CIFAR100-C. However, the main paper still presents the gains somewhat as evidence that the concept bottleneck itself has become robust. A more cautious interpretation is that the method can transfer robustness from the FM-based predictor into the CBM when pseudo-labels are reliable. That distinction matters.

A second concern is the theoretical framing of shift types and completeness. Section 2 introduces low-level shift as preserving the concept-score distribution and concept-level shift as changing concept scores while preserving prediction distributions. These definitions are neat, but they are also somewhat idealized and tightly coupled to the chosen concept mapping h. In practice, whether a shift is “low-level” or “concept-level” depends on the representation and concept bank, not just the data-generating process. For example, Gaussian noise may alter concept scores substantially for one CBM and not another; the paper indeed uses this as a failure mode. So the taxonomy is useful descriptively, but less convincing as a formal categorization of real-world shifts. Similarly, Definition 1 of concept completeness is quite strong and existential, but the paper does not operationalize it beyond motivating RCB. I would have liked either a more modest framing or stronger theoretical development connecting these definitions to the proposed objectives.

A third concern is the CSA objective. The method assumes access to class-conditional Gaussian statistics of source-domain concept scores, namely means and covariances for each class, even though the source data are unavailable at adaptation time. This is stated in Section 3.1 on page 8. In source-free adaptation this may be acceptable if such statistics are stored, but it is still a nontrivial assumption that should be emphasized more clearly in the problem statement and abstract. Moreover, the Gaussian assumption seems fairly ad hoc. There is no evidence that concept-score distributions are approximately Gaussian, nor that Mahalanobis alignment in concept space is the right inductive bias. Since CSA appears to matter mainly for low-level shifts (Figure 3 on page 12), it would strengthen the paper to include empirical validation of this assumption, e.g., calibration of Gaussian fit, comparison to simpler moment matching, or a nonparametric alternative.

A fourth concern is that the ablation evidence does not fully support the necessity of the full three-stage method. Figure 3 shows that under low-level shifts CSA alone can outperform the full method, and under concept-level shifts LPA alone can match or exceed the full method. This is interesting and consistent with the authors’ narrative, but it also suggests that the unified pipeline may be overengineered or at least not robustly beneficial. The paper should discuss whether one can detect the shift type online and selectively activate components. As written, the method seems to pay extra complexity for components that may be unnecessary or even mildly harmful in some regimes. This is especially relevant because the runtime analysis in Appendix B.1/B.2 and Table 3 on page 23 shows pseudo-labeling dominates cost, but the adaptation stages still add overhead.

A fifth concern is about the interpretability claims for residual concepts. The paper argues that RCB discovers meaningful new concepts and improves interpretability. However, the residual concepts are learned directly to improve pseudo-label agreement, with regularizers for diversity and coherence. This does not guarantee semantic meaningfulness. The automatic annotation procedure in Appendix B.2 uses CLIP-DISSECT-style matching against a candidate vocabulary from ConceptNet and accepts labels above a cosine threshold. This is a reasonable exploratory tool, but it is not strong evidence that the learned residual vectors correspond to stable, human-understandable concepts. In fact, Figure 7 on page 30 explicitly shows an accuracy-interpretability tradeoff as more residual concepts are added. I think the paper should tone down claims that RCB enhances interpretability, or provide stronger human evaluation or intervention-based evidence. At present, the evidence supports “some residual vectors can sometimes be annotated plausibly,” which is weaker.

I also have concerns about the fairness and clarity of some empirical comparisons. First, the backbones differ across datasets, and for CIFAR the paper uses FARE2 robust CLIP rather than standard CLIP. This is reasonable for performance, but it makes it harder to isolate the contribution of CONDA versus the robustness of the backbone. The paper does acknowledge that stronger backbones help, but the main message sometimes blurs whether gains come from adaptation or from starting with a particularly robust FM. Second, the CBM baselines are heterogeneous in both concept construction and source-domain accuracy. For example, Yeh et al. (2020) often has much higher source accuracy than the other CBMs, but the paper also notes in Appendix F that its concepts may be less interpretable because they are optimized for accuracy. This complicates the narrative that CONDA improves “concept-based interpretability” across methods. It may improve accuracy across CBM variants, but interpretability quality is not comparably established across them.

Another issue is that the paper’s main table averages CIFAR-C over 15 corruptions at severity 2, but the choice of severity 2 is motivated partly by preserving pseudo-label quality (Appendix C.1, page 24). This is understandable, yet it also means the benchmark is selected to suit the pseudo-labeling mechanism. Appendix F and Table 8 on page 32 show that at higher corruption severity the method can fail badly because the FM pseudo-labels collapse. This limitation should be more prominent in the main paper, because it materially bounds the applicability of the approach. In deployment settings with severe shift, the method may not help.

I found some aspects of the exposition slightly overstated. The paper claims to be the first TTA method for concept bottlenecks with a foundation model backbone “to our knowledge.” That may be true, but the novelty is somewhat incremental at the component level: CSA is inspired by class-aware feature alignment, LPA is pseudo-label fine-tuning with sparsity regularization, and RCB resembles adding residual capacity with coherence/diversity penalties. The novelty lies more in the decomposition and integration for CBMs than in fundamentally new adaptation machinery. I would encourage the authors to present the contribution in those terms.

There are also some technical details that deserve clarification. The notation in Section 2 is heavier than necessary and occasionally obscures the main ideas. The formalization via measurable transformations and push-forward measures is mathematically neat but not really used later in the analysis or proofs, since there are no formal guarantees. The paper might benefit from simplifying this section and reallocating space to empirical diagnostics. Also, the online adaptation protocol uses the same batch for adaptation and prediction, following common TTA practice, but this can sometimes inflate performance relative to stricter evaluation protocols. The paper should explicitly state whether labels are never used for model selection on target data and how hyperparameters were tuned. Table 4 lists dataset-specific hyperparameters, but it is not clear whether these were selected using target validation labels, source validation, or prior heuristics. This matters for assessing realism.

Despite these concerns, I think the paper has real merit. The empirical phenomenon it highlights is important: CBMs can lose much more under shift than one might expect from their source-domain performance, and adapting the concept layer and concept-to-label mapping can recover a substantial fraction of that loss. The modular design is sensible, and the experiments are broad enough to make the work noteworthy. I especially appreciate that the paper includes negative results and limitations rather than presenting a uniformly rosy picture.

To strengthen the paper, I would suggest the following concrete improvements. First, reframe the method more explicitly as source-free target-domain distillation from FM-based pseudo-labels into a concept-based predictor. This would sharpen the contribution and avoid overclaiming autonomous concept robustness. Second, add stronger baselines from the TTA literature applied directly to the equivalent linear model over FM features, and perhaps to the CBM logits, to show that the gains are not simply due to generic pseudo-label fine-tuning. Third, include an oracle study that disentangles the contributions of pseudo-label quality versus adaptation architecture more systematically, beyond Table 5. Fourth, provide more rigorous evidence for interpretability, ideally human evaluation, intervention utility, or stability of residual concepts across runs. Fifth, discuss or develop a mechanism to detect shift type and activate only CSA or LPA/RCB as needed, since the ablations suggest the full pipeline is not always optimal.

I would also encourage a more careful discussion of what counts as a “concept” in the residual branch. If the residual vectors are primarily latent directions that can sometimes be post hoc annotated, then calling the whole model a concept bottleneck may be somewhat aspirational. A distinction between source concepts with explicit semantics and residual latent concepts with approximate annotations would make the interpretability claims more honest and nuanced.

In summary, this is a good paper on an important and underexplored problem. Its main contribution is not a radically new adaptation algorithm, but a compelling formulation of deployment-time failures in FM-based CBMs and a practical framework that often recovers substantial accuracy under shift while preserving some degree of concept-level explanation. The empirical results are strong enough to make the work interesting, but the current presentation overstates interpretability and underemphasizes the dependence on black-box pseudo-labels and backbone robustness. With clearer framing and stronger validation of the interpretability and adaptation assumptions, this could become a solid contribution.

My overall assessment is positive but not unreservedly so. I see this as a borderline-to-accept paper for a strong ML venue: novel enough in problem framing and sufficiently validated empirically, but with important caveats around assumptions, fairness, and interpretability that should be addressed in revision.
