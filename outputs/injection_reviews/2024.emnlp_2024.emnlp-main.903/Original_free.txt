This paper studies continual pre-training (CPT) of language models with mixed general-domain and target-domain data, and asks whether there is a principled “best” mixture ratio. The authors define a Critical Mixture Ratio (CMR) as the largest domain-data proportion that still preserves general capability within a tolerance while improving domain loss, and they propose empirical scaling laws to predict CMR from limited runs. The paper presents experiments on four model sizes (460M, 940M, 1.6B, 3.1B), mainly on a Finance domain and with a smaller generalization study on Academic Papers. The central empirical claims are that losses during CPT obey simple power-law-like relationships with mixture ratio and training tokens, and that these fits can be used to estimate the feasible ratio set and hence the CMR.

Overall, I find the paper interesting and practically motivated. Choosing replay/domain mixture ratios in CPT is indeed an important but under-principled problem, and the attempt to formalize the trade-off between domain adaptation and general retention is valuable. The paper also contains a substantial amount of experimentation and tries to connect empirical observations to a simple optimization view. In particular, the visualizations of domain-vs-general loss trajectories across model sizes and ratios are useful; for example, the 3D trajectory plot on page 3 and its cross-sections in the appendix help convey the notion of “feasible” training trajectories. The observation that larger models tolerate larger domain proportions, and that domains closer to the original pretraining distribution admit larger CMR, is plausible and potentially useful.

That said, I have significant reservations about the rigor of the formulation, the strength of the empirical evidence, and the extent to which the proposed “scaling law” is established beyond curve fitting on a narrow setup. My overall assessment is therefore mixed: the paper has a good problem and some promising empirical observations, but the current version does not yet fully support its stronger claims.

The main strength of the paper is the problem framing. In practice, CPT almost always involves a trade-off between domain specialization and catastrophic forgetting, and the paper makes this trade-off explicit. Defining a feasible set of mixture ratios under a general-loss tolerance is a sensible operationalization. The definition of CMR as the maximum feasible ratio is also intuitive from a resource-efficiency perspective: if one can safely use more domain data without violating the retention constraint, then lower ratios are arguably under-utilizing the adaptation budget. This is a useful lens, even if some details of the formalism need tightening.

A second strength is the breadth of the experimental grid relative to many scaling-law papers in this area. The authors pretrain their own models from scratch and then run CPT across multiple ratios and model sizes, which is nontrivial. The paper includes both ratio-wise fits at fixed token budget and token-wise fits at fixed ratio. The appendix tables on pages 16–17 provide fitting metrics and coefficients, which is appreciated for reproducibility. The reported fit quality for the token-wise curves is often high in terms of MSE/R^2, at least within the observed range.

A third positive aspect is the attempt to test generalization to a second domain. The Academic Papers experiment, shown on pages 7–9, is limited but directionally useful. The finding that the 460M model has a higher predicted CMR on Academic Papers (36.7%) than on Finance (29.8%) is intuitively consistent with the claim that the academic domain is closer to the original pretraining mix. This kind of domain-distance dependence is an interesting hypothesis worth pursuing.

However, my biggest concern is that the paper’s theoretical framing is much weaker than it appears at first glance. The use of a Lagrangian in Section 3 gives an impression of principled optimization, but in practice the method does not solve the stated constrained optimization problem over model size, ratio, and tokens. Instead, it defines a heuristic condition based on the sign of a derivative along training trajectories and then empirically fits losses. The derivation of feasible ratios in Appendix B is not fully convincing. In particular, the condition that there exists some T0 such that dF/dT ≤ 0 is quite weak and does not obviously correspond to the CPT objective as stated. If the objective is to end training with acceptable general loss and improved domain loss, why is existence of a single point along the trajectory with non-positive derivative the right criterion? This seems neither necessary nor sufficient for the final-time constraint in Equation (1). The paper sometimes informally interprets this as “the curve shows a downward trend as training proceeds,” but the formal condition is only existential, not global over a suffix of training.

Relatedly, the role of the Lagrange multiplier lambda is under-specified and somewhat problematic. In the derivation, lambda is introduced as if it encodes the trade-off preference, but in practice it is not learned or selected by solving the constrained problem. Later, on page 8, the paper notes that lambda must be very large (100 to 7000) because general-loss changes are small. This makes the formalism feel post hoc rather than predictive. Since CMR is ultimately determined by the tolerance epsilon and the fitted curves, it is unclear what substantive role lambda plays beyond motivating the slope condition. I would strongly encourage the authors either to simplify the presentation and drop the optimization veneer, or to make the optimization genuinely operational.

A second major concern is the definition of “optimality.” The paper repeatedly states that CMR is the optimal mixture ratio, but this is only true under the authors’ specific definition: maximize domain-data proportion subject to a general-loss tolerance and some trajectory condition. That is not the same as optimizing downstream utility, minimizing total compute for a target domain performance, or maximizing a weighted combination of general and domain performance. In fact, if domain loss as a function of ratio is smooth and diminishing-returns-like, the largest feasible ratio need not be optimal for many reasonable objectives. The paper should be much more careful to say that CMR is optimal only for this constrained criterion, not in any broader sense.

Third, the empirical evidence for a “scaling law” is narrower than the title suggests. The experiments cover only four relatively small models up to 3.1B parameters, one main domain, one language/data mixture regime, one architecture family, and one CPT schedule. The CMR law itself is fit separately for each fixed model size as a function of training tokens; it is not a joint law over model size, tokens, and domain characteristics. Indeed, the limitations section acknowledges that the method cannot predict CMR for larger models from smaller ones. This is important: the title and framing suggest a general scaling law for CPT mixture ratios, but the actual result is closer to an empirical interpolation/extrapolation recipe within a fixed setup. I think the claims should be moderated accordingly.

Fourth, the validation of the predicted CMR is incomplete. The paper predicts CMR values such as 29.8%, 34.9%, 41.4%, and 47.8% for the four Finance models at the 20B-token CPT budget (page 7, Figure 5), but it is not clear that these predicted values are directly verified by running CPT at or near those ratios. Much of the evidence comes from fits based on a small discrete set of ratios such as 1/8, 1/4, 1/3, 1/2, 3/4, and 1. If the central contribution is accurate prediction of the critical ratio, then the paper should include targeted holdout experiments at predicted CMRs and nearby ratios to show that the predicted threshold indeed separates feasible from infeasible settings. Without such validation, the result remains largely a curve-fitting exercise.

Fifth, the reliance on validation loss alone is a limitation that affects the core claim more than the paper acknowledges. The authors cite prior work suggesting pretraining loss correlates with downstream performance, which is fair, but here the entire notion of preserving “general ability” and improving “domain-specific capability” is operationalized only through validation losses on held-out corpora. This is not obviously sufficient. General loss may recover while specific capabilities degrade, and domain loss improvements may not translate uniformly to downstream tasks. Since the paper’s motivation is practical CPT for specialized domains, at least a modest downstream evaluation suite would substantially strengthen the work. Even a small set of general benchmarks plus domain tasks for Finance and Academic Papers would help validate that CMR corresponds to a meaningful operating point.

There are also several methodological details that need clarification. The tolerance epsilon is set to 0.05 and justified empirically in Appendix C, but this choice is central because it directly determines the feasible set A and hence CMR. The paper says the value “does not affect the conclusions,” which seems too strong. A different epsilon could materially change the feasible set and the ranking of ratios, especially when general-loss changes are small. I would like to see a sensitivity analysis over epsilon. Similarly, the CPT budget is fixed at 20B tokens in the main experiments, while the CMR curves are extrapolated far beyond the observed range, e.g., to 500B tokens in Figure 5 on page 7. Such extrapolation is speculative, especially given the non-monotonic general-loss behavior and the limited observed token range. The paper should distinguish clearly between interpolation and long-range extrapolation, and avoid over-interpreting the latter.

The fitting choices also deserve more scrutiny. For mixture-ratio fitting, the paper uses a simple power law L(R)=alpha R^s + beta for both domain and general loss at Tmax. But the general-loss curve shown in Figure 3 on page 6 is nearly flat at low ratios and then rises more sharply; whether a single power law is the right inductive bias is not obvious. For token-wise fitting, the general-loss increment uses a two-term power law to capture rise-then-fall behavior. This is flexible enough to fit the observed curves, but the paper does not compare against alternative functional forms or report uncertainty intervals. Since the main conclusions depend on threshold crossings and inferred critical points, uncertainty quantification is important. Confidence bands on fitted curves and on predicted CMR would make the claims much more credible.

Another issue is that the paper’s notion of domain similarity is only loosely supported. The claim that Academic Papers are closer to the general corpus because they constitute about 10% of the pretraining data is plausible, but this is not a principled measure of distribution gap. It would be stronger to quantify similarity using perplexity transfer, embedding-based divergence, or token distribution statistics, and then correlate that with CMR across several domains. With only two domains, the claim is suggestive but far from established.

I also found parts of the exposition imprecise. There are multiple grammatical issues and some notation inconsistencies. For example, the paper sometimes uses S and sometimes N for model size in the appendix derivations. The set A is described as ratios satisfying the first constraint, and then ratios in A “that align with our CPT objective” are considered feasible, but the transition to the derivative-based definition of F is not cleanly motivated. Equation numbering and references are occasionally confusing. The statement on page 8 that “the larger the Tmax, the wider the range of feasible mixture ratios” may be true empirically in their setup, but it is presented almost as a theorem without proof. The discussion that CMR approaches 1 as Tmax approaches infinity is interesting but speculative and likely depends on optimization dynamics, overfitting, and whether replay data remains sufficient to preserve generality.

Despite these concerns, I do think the paper contains a potentially useful empirical insight: there may indeed be a predictable threshold-like regime for replay/domain mixture in CPT, and this threshold appears to depend on model size, token budget, and domain proximity. That is worth studying. But to reach the standard of a strong conference paper, the current work needs stronger validation and a more careful separation between empirical observation, heuristic modeling, and formal claims.

The most important improvements I would recommend are as follows. First, directly validate predicted CMRs with held-out experiments at the predicted ratio and nearby ratios, ideally across multiple model sizes and both domains. Second, add downstream evaluations on both general and domain tasks to show that the loss-based CMR corresponds to a meaningful practical optimum. Third, perform sensitivity analyses over epsilon, learning rate schedule, replay corpus choice, and CPT budget. Fourth, provide uncertainty estimates for fitted parameters and predicted CMRs, and compare the chosen functional forms against reasonable alternatives. Fifth, either strengthen the theory substantially or simplify it: as written, the Lagrangian/derivative derivation feels more decorative than foundational. A cleaner empirical definition of feasibility based on final losses might actually make the paper stronger.

I also think the paper would benefit from broader baselines. Since Que et al. (2024) is discussed as a related CPT mixture law, it would be useful to compare the proposed CMR predictions against D-CPT or against simple heuristics such as fixed replay ratios, equal-loss trade-off points, or validation-based early pilot sweeps. The practical value of the method depends not just on whether it fits curves, but whether it predicts better ratios with less compute than existing heuristics.

In summary, this is a promising and practically relevant paper with an interesting central idea, but the current evidence does not fully justify the strength of its claims about a general CMR scaling law. I appreciate the effort to pretrain multiple models and the clear motivation around CPT data mixing. However, the formalism is not yet rigorous enough, the empirical validation of predicted CMR is incomplete, and the evaluation is too narrow to establish broad generality. I would encourage the authors to continue this line of work, because with stronger validation and a tighter presentation it could become a valuable contribution.

My recommendation is borderline to weak reject in its current form, primarily because the paper is interesting but not yet sufficiently convincing on rigor and validation for a top-tier venue. If the authors can add direct CMR verification, downstream-task evidence, and stronger robustness analyses, I could imagine a substantially improved version being competitive.
