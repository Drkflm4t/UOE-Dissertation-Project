This paper studies a practically important but underexplored question in continual pre-training (CPT) of language models: how to choose the mixture ratio between replayed general data and domain-specific data. The central claim is that there exists a “Critical Mixture Ratio” (CMR), defined as the largest domain-data proportion that still preserves general capability within a tolerance while improving domain loss, and that this CMR follows a predictable scaling law with training tokens. The paper supports this claim with experiments on models from 460M to 3.1B parameters, primarily on a finance domain and with an additional academic-papers domain for generalization. Overall, I found the paper interesting, timely, and quite valuable. It addresses a real pain point in domain adaptation of LLMs, proposes a clear conceptual framework, and backs it with a substantial empirical study and a reasonably interpretable mathematical formalization.

The strongest aspect of the paper is that it elevates what is often treated as a heuristic engineering choice into a principled optimization target. In many CPT pipelines, the replay/domain mixture is indeed chosen ad hoc, and the paper’s framing of CPT as a trade-off between preserving general ability and acquiring domain knowledge is both intuitive and useful. The definitions of feasible mixture ratios and CMR are easy to understand, and the visualizations are effective. In particular, the domain-general loss trajectory plots on pages 3 and 5 are helpful in conveying the geometric intuition behind the feasible region and the existence of a maximal acceptable ratio. The paper’s emphasis that “more domain data is not always better” under finite compute is important and practically relevant.

A second major strength is the empirical regularity the authors uncover. The paper shows that domain loss decreases monotonically with mixture ratio while general loss is initially stable and then rises, and that these relationships can be fit well by simple power-law forms. The plots on page 6 are especially convincing in showing smooth dependence of both domain and general validation loss on mixture ratio. Likewise, the token-scaling fits on pages 7 and 9 capture the rise-then-fall behavior of general loss during CPT, which is an interesting phenomenon in itself and aligns with recent observations about transient instability or forgetting during continued training. The reported fitting quality is strong: Table 2 and Table 3 indicate very small prediction errors and high R-squared values across settings. Even if one remains cautious about extrapolation, the empirical fit quality within the studied regime is impressive.

A third strength is the practical orientation of the work. The proposed CMR scaling law is not merely descriptive; it is intended to help practitioners estimate a good mixture ratio from limited pilot runs. This is exactly the kind of result that can matter in real training pipelines, where exhaustive grid search over ratios is expensive. The paper also provides fitted coefficients in the appendix, detailed plots across model sizes and ratios, and a transparent description of the data setup and training procedure. I appreciated the effort to make the study reproducible and inspectable.

The cross-domain comparison is also a nice touch. The result that the 460M model has a higher predicted CMR for Academic Papers than for Finance, attributed to smaller distribution gap from the general corpus, is plausible and potentially insightful. The figures on pages 7 and 8 support this claim, and the discussion connects it to prior work on continual pre-training and domain similarity. This broadens the contribution beyond a single-domain empirical artifact.

That said, there are several issues that should be addressed to strengthen the paper further.

My main concern is around the exact definition of “optimality” and how much of it is a modeling choice rather than an empirically validated optimum. The paper defines CMR as the maximum feasible ratio under a tolerance constraint on general loss. This is a reasonable operational definition, but it is not the only possible notion of optimality. In practice, users may care about downstream task utility, weighted combinations of general and domain performance, or compute-normalized gains. The paper acknowledges this partially, but the wording sometimes overstates the result by calling CMR “the optimal mixture ratio” without sufficiently emphasizing that this is optimal relative to the chosen tolerance-based objective. I would encourage the authors to sharpen this distinction. Doing so would not weaken the paper; rather, it would make the contribution more precise and more broadly acceptable.

Relatedly, the tolerance parameter epsilon is central to the definition of feasibility and hence to CMR, but its treatment is somewhat underdeveloped. The appendix explains that epsilon is set to 0.05 based on empirical observation and that conclusions do not depend on it, but this is not fully demonstrated. Since CMR is defined as the largest ratio satisfying a general-loss constraint, one would expect its numerical value to depend materially on epsilon. A sensitivity analysis over several epsilon values would substantially strengthen the paper. This seems especially important if the method is to be used in practice, because different applications will tolerate different levels of general-capability degradation. I view this as an important missing experiment, though it appears straightforward to add.

A second concern is that the theoretical derivation is more of a formalization than a predictive theory in the strict sense. The Lagrangian setup is sensible as a way to encode the trade-off, but some steps in the derivation feel heuristic. For example, the feasible-ratio condition based on the existence of a point T0 where the derivative of the objective with respect to training tokens becomes non-positive is intuitive, but the connection between this condition and the end-of-training feasibility criterion could be explained more rigorously. Similarly, Equation (7)/(32) is implicit in T0 because T0 appears on both sides through the second power-law term, and the practical solution procedure is not described in enough detail. None of this invalidates the empirical findings, but the paper would benefit from being more explicit that the “scaling law” is primarily an empirical law motivated by the formalization, rather than a theorem derived from first principles.

There is also some ambiguity in notation and exposition. The paper uses F both for the Lagrangian objective and for the feasible set, which is confusing. There are a few typographical and grammatical issues throughout, and some equations and definitions could be cleaned up. For example, the definition of the mixed dataset and ratio on page 2 is slightly awkward, and the derivation in Appendix B occasionally switches notation or uses symbols in ways that require effort to parse. These are presentation issues rather than scientific flaws, but given the mathematical emphasis of the paper, clarity matters.

On the experimental side, the study is substantial but still somewhat narrow relative to the breadth of the claims. The largest model is 3.1B, which is meaningful but still far from the scale at which many CPT decisions are made in current frontier or production settings. The authors are transparent about this limitation. I do think the observed trends are likely useful, but stronger evidence on larger models would increase confidence that the same laws hold without regime changes. Similarly, the main experiments are on one domain, with only a single-model generalization study on a second domain. This is a good start, but more domains with varying similarity to the base corpus would make the “distribution gap affects CMR” claim much more compelling.

Another limitation is the reliance on validation pre-training loss as the sole primary metric. The paper cites prior work showing correlation between loss and downstream performance, which is fair, and for a scaling-law paper this choice is understandable. Still, because the paper’s motivation is practical CPT for specialized domains, at least a small downstream evaluation would make the contribution much stronger. In particular, it would be valuable to know whether the predicted CMR indeed corresponds to the best trade-off on domain tasks and general benchmarks, compared with nearby ratios such as 1/4, 1/3, or 1/2. This would help bridge the gap between loss-based optimality and application-level usefulness.

I also think the comparison to prior work could be sharpened. The paper positions itself against D-CPT law by arguing that prior work ignores the general/domain trade-off, which is a fair distinction. However, a more direct empirical comparison would be very informative. For example, if one used the prior method to choose a ratio and compared it against the proposed CMR on both domain loss and general retention, that would make the practical advantage much more concrete. As written, the novelty is clear conceptually, but the empirical superiority over alternatives is not fully established.

Despite these concerns, I view the paper positively. The core idea is strong, the problem is important, and the empirical evidence is substantial and thoughtfully presented. The paper contributes a useful conceptual vocabulary—feasible mixture ratios and CMR—for thinking about CPT, and it provides evidence that these quantities are predictable from small-scale observations. Even where the theory is not fully rigorous, it serves as a coherent scaffold for the empirical findings. I also appreciate that the authors openly discuss limitations and future directions, including scaling to larger models and incorporating model-size scaling more directly.

For improvement, I would most strongly recommend the following additions or clarifications in a revision. First, include a sensitivity analysis over epsilon and discuss how practitioners should choose it. Second, add at least a modest downstream evaluation on both general and domain tasks to validate that the loss-based CMR aligns with task-level trade-offs. Third, clarify the derivation and practical computation of T0 and CMR, including whether Equation (7) is solved numerically and how stable that procedure is. Fourth, improve notation and exposition, especially avoiding reuse of F for multiple meanings. Fifth, if possible, include a direct comparison against a baseline ratio-selection strategy, whether heuristic fixed ratios or a prior scaling-law method.

In summary, I think this is a meaningful and well-executed paper on an important practical question in continual pre-training. Its main contribution is not just another fitted curve, but a useful reframing of CPT data mixing as a constrained trade-off problem with a predictable critical point. The empirical results are strong within the studied regime, the visualizations are informative, and the practical implications are significant. While there are some limitations in scope, theoretical precision, and downstream validation, these feel like opportunities to strengthen an already solid contribution rather than fundamental weaknesses. I would be supportive of acceptance.
