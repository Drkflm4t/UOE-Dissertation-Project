Summary: This paper studies continual pre-training (CPT) of language models with mixed general and domain-specific data, aiming to replace heuristic mixture-ratio choices with a principled criterion. The authors define a Critical Mixture Ratio (CMR) as the largest domain-data proportion that still preserves general capability within a tolerance while improving domain loss. Empirically, using Llama-style models from 460M to 3.1B parameters, trained on a 220B-token general corpus and then continually pre-trained for 20B tokens on Finance or Academic Papers mixtures, they observe that domain loss decreases with higher domain ratio while general loss tends to first stay flat or rise slightly and then worsen at high ratios. They fit simple power-law style functions for loss versus mixture ratio and for loss change versus training tokens, and use these fits to predict feasible ratios and CMR. Reported findings include that predicted CMR increases with model size (e.g., about 29.8% to 47.8% across 460M to 3.1B on Finance) and is higher when the target domain is closer to the original pre-training distribution (Academic Papers vs Finance).

Strengths: Addresses a practically important but under-studied CPT design question: how to choose the replay/domain mixture ratio efficiently rather than heuristically.; Introduces a clear optimization perspective on the trade-off between preserving general ability and improving domain ability, with an intuitive definition of feasible ratios and CMR.; Provides extensive controlled experiments across four model sizes and multiple mixture ratios, with visualizations of domain/general loss trajectories (notably Figures 1–5).; The empirical trends are plausible and useful: larger models tolerate larger domain proportions, and domains closer to the pre-training distribution admit higher CMR.; Attempts to turn limited experimental measurements into predictive scaling laws, which could be practically valuable for planning CPT runs.; Includes an explicit generalization check on a second domain (Academic Papers), which strengthens the claim that the phenomenon is not unique to Finance.; Paper is generally well motivated, and the appendix provides fitting coefficients and additional plots that improve reproducibility.

Weaknesses: The central notion of 'optimality' is definition-dependent: CMR is defined as the maximum feasible ratio under a chosen tolerance, not necessarily the ratio that optimizes downstream utility, compute efficiency, or a formal Pareto objective.; Evaluation relies almost entirely on validation pre-training loss rather than downstream task performance, so the practical significance of CMR for real applications remains indirect.; The tolerance parameter epsilon=0.05 is chosen empirically and can materially affect the feasible set and thus CMR; sensitivity analysis is missing.; The theoretical derivation is fairly lightweight and mostly reformulates empirical observations with a Lagrangian; it does not provide a strong mechanistic or statistical justification for the proposed power-law forms.; The fitting procedure appears vulnerable to overfitting/extrapolation risk, especially for the two-term general-loss function and long-range extrapolation beyond observed token budgets (e.g., to 500B tokens).; Experiments are limited to relatively small models by modern LLM standards (up to 3.1B), only two domains, and a single architecture family, limiting external validity.; The paper does not compare against strong baselines for mixture-ratio selection (e.g., grid search under fixed compute, adaptive replay schedules, or prior CPT laws such as D-CPT in a matched setup).; Some mathematical steps are not fully rigorous; for example, feasibility is tied to existence of a point where a derivative condition holds, which may be weaker than the stated end-of-training objective.; The claim that CMR can be regarded as the optimal mixture ratio may be overstated given the narrow objective and absence of downstream validation.

Soundness Issues: The equivalence between the CPT objective and the derivative-based feasible-ratio condition is not fully convincing. Existence of some T0 with dF/dT <= 0 does not by itself guarantee the end-state constraints on general and domain losses at Tmax.; The use of a fixed large Lagrange multiplier range and the interpretation of slope conditions in Figures 1–2 seem heuristic rather than derived from a validated optimization procedure.; Power-law assumptions for loss versus mixture ratio and token volume are empirically fitted but not strongly justified; the paper may be capturing local smooth trends rather than true scaling laws.; Generalization evidence is weak: only one additional domain and one model size (460M) are tested for Academic Papers.; Because all conclusions depend on validation losses, the paper does not establish that predicted CMR corresponds to best downstream trade-offs in practice.; Extrapolations to much larger token budgets and discussion of asymptotic behavior (e.g., CMR approaching 1 as Tmax grows) are speculative and unsupported by experiments.
