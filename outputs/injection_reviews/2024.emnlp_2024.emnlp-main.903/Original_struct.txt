Summary: The paper studies continual pre-training (CPT) of language models with mixtures of general and domain-specific data, and proposes the notion of a Critical Mixture Ratio (CMR): the largest domain-data proportion that still preserves general-domain performance within a tolerance while improving domain loss. The authors pretrain four Llama-style models (460M, 940M, 1.6B, 3.1B) on a 200B-token general corpus, then run CPT for 20B tokens on Finance and Academic Papers mixtures. Empirically, they fit power-law-like relationships between (i) final loss and mixture ratio, and (ii) loss change and training tokens, and then derive a scaling law intended to predict CMR as a function of training budget. They report that predicted CMR increases with model size and is higher for Academic Papers than Finance, which they attribute to smaller distribution shift from the original pretraining corpus.

Strengths: Addresses a practically important but underexplored question in CPT: how to choose the replay/domain mixture ratio in a principled way rather than heuristically.; Introduces an intuitively useful concept of balancing domain adaptation against preservation of general capability, and attempts to formalize this trade-off.; Runs experiments across multiple model sizes and two domains, which is better than a single-scale anecdotal study.; Provides extensive plots/tables and fitted coefficients in the appendix, which helps reproducibility of the reported fitting procedure.; The empirical observation that larger models can tolerate higher domain-data ratios during CPT is plausible and potentially useful in practice.

Weaknesses: The central theoretical framing is weak: the proposed Lagrangian/feasibility derivation does not actually establish optimality of the maximum feasible ratio, and several mathematical steps are heuristic rather than rigorous.; The definition of CMR is heavily dependent on an arbitrary tolerance parameter ε=0.05, yet the paper does not provide sensitivity analysis showing whether conclusions are robust to this choice.; The empirical evidence for a 'scaling law' is limited: only four model sizes, one main domain for fitting, one additional domain for a very narrow generalization check, and only a single CPT budget actually trained (20B tokens) while larger-token claims rely on extrapolation.; The paper evaluates only validation losses, not downstream task performance or forgetting on representative general benchmarks, so the practical meaning of CMR for capability preservation is not convincingly established.; The claimed prediction quality is overstated because many results are interpolation over a small number of mixture ratios rather than true out-of-distribution prediction; extrapolation to much larger token budgets is unsupported.; The notion that the maximum feasible ratio is 'optimal' ignores alternative objectives, e.g., minimizing a weighted combination of general and domain performance, compute efficiency, or downstream utility; this is a definition-driven conclusion rather than a demonstrated optimum.; Experimental design may confound conclusions because the general CPT replay set is fixed at 20B tokens and the domain datasets are large, but the paper does not analyze data quality, deduplication, contamination, or whether repeated sampling affects the fitted laws.; The presentation contains multiple language/notation inconsistencies and some unclear claims, making it difficult to assess rigor.

Methodological Flaws: The optimization formulation is not actually solved. Equation (4) defines an optimization over model size, ratio, and tokens, but the paper later fixes model size and effectively defines CMR as max feasible R by fiat. This does not follow from the Lagrangian objective and does not prove that CMR is optimal for any meaningful utility function.; The derivation of feasible ratios from ∂F/∂T <= 0 is scientifically questionable. A negative derivative at some training point T0 only indicates local decrease of the chosen objective along training time, not that the final trained model satisfies the CPT objective or that the ratio is globally feasible/optimal. Existence of one point with non-positive derivative is too weak a condition.; The chain-rule derivation leading to ∂LΔgen/∂LΔdom = -1/λ assumes differentiability and ∂LΔdom/∂T ≠ 0, then interprets existence of such a point as feasibility. This is not equivalent to the original constrained objective, and the equivalence claim is not justified.; Equation (7)/(32) for T0 is implicit because T0 appears on both sides. The paper presents it as if it were a usable closed-form solution, but it is not. No numerical solution procedure, convergence analysis, or uniqueness discussion is provided.; The use of power-law forms is largely ad hoc. For mixture ratio, the general loss curve is described as nearly flat then rising, but the chosen single-term power law L(R)=αR^s+β is not obviously appropriate for such shape over the full range. For token dynamics, the two-term form is flexible enough to fit many curves, raising overfitting concerns given limited data points.; Claims of high predictive accuracy are not very convincing because the reported tiny errors for mixture-ratio prediction appear to come from predicting one held-out ratio within a narrow interpolation regime. This does not validate the broader CMR extrapolation claims.; The paper extrapolates CMR to token budgets far beyond observed training (e.g., Figure 5 to 500B equivalent) without empirical verification. Given known instability of scaling-law extrapolation under distribution shift and regime changes, these conclusions are not empirically valid.; Generalization evidence is weak: only one additional domain and only one model size (460M) are tested. This is insufficient to support claims that the CMR scaling law generalizes.; The paper uses validation loss as a proxy for general/domain capability preservation, citing correlation with downstream performance, but does not verify this in the CPT setting. Catastrophic forgetting and domain gains are ultimately task-level phenomena; relying solely on loss weakens empirical validity.; The tolerance ε is selected empirically and the paper explicitly states it does not affect conclusions, but provides no ablation to support that statement. Since feasibility and CMR are defined through ε, this claim is logically unsupported.; The statement that as Tmax→∞ the feasible ratio range approaches (0,1) and CMR approaches 1 is speculative and not established by either theory or experiments. In realistic CPT, optimization dynamics, overfitting, and irreversible forgetting can violate this intuition.; The model-size scaling claim is based on only four relatively small models up to 3.1B, all with the same architecture family and training recipe. This is too narrow to substantiate a robust scaling law across LLMs.
