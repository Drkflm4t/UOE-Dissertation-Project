Summary: The paper studies continual pre-training (CPT) of language models with mixed general and domain-specific data, aiming to identify an optimal domain-data proportion. It introduces the Critical Mixture Ratio (CMR), defined as the maximum feasible domain-data ratio that improves domain loss while keeping general loss within a tolerance. Empirically, the authors pre-train Llama-style models from 460M to 3.1B parameters, then perform CPT on Finance and Academic Papers domains with varying mixture ratios. They fit simple power-law style relationships for (i) loss versus mixture ratio and (ii) loss change versus training tokens, and combine these to predict CMR as a function of training budget. Results suggest that CMR exists, tends to increase with model size and training budget, and is larger when the target domain is closer to the original pre-training distribution.

Strengths: Addresses a practically important but under-studied question in CPT: how to choose the replay/domain mixture ratio in a principled way rather than heuristically.; Introduces a clear conceptual framing of the trade-off between preserving general capability and improving domain capability, and formalizes this via feasible mixture ratios and CMR.; Provides an intuitively useful empirical finding that CMR increases with model size and depends on domain similarity, which could be actionable for practitioners.; Uses multiple model scales (460M to 3.1B) and two domains, with substantial token budgets, making the empirical study broader than many small-scale CPT analyses.; The fitted curves appear numerically accurate on the reported metrics (very small fitting errors, high R^2 values), and the appendix provides coefficients and additional plots that improve transparency.; The paper is well motivated and connects its proposal to prior work on scaling laws and continual/domain pre-training.; The visualizations, especially the domain-general loss trajectories and CMR-vs-token plots, help communicate the proposed trade-off and prediction procedure.

Weaknesses: The notion of 'optimality' is definition-dependent: CMR is defined as the largest feasible ratio under a chosen tolerance on general loss, rather than being derived from downstream utility or a broader objective that includes compute/performance trade-offs explicitly.; Empirical validation is limited to relatively small models by current LLM standards (up to 3.1B) and only two domains, so it is unclear how robust the claimed scaling law is for larger frontier-scale models or more diverse domains.; The evaluation relies almost entirely on validation loss rather than downstream task metrics, so the practical significance of preserving general loss within the chosen tolerance is not directly demonstrated.; The tolerance parameter epsilon=0.05 is selected empirically and materially affects the feasible set and thus CMR; sensitivity analyses are limited.; Some claims about generalization are stronger than what the evidence supports, since the cross-domain validation is only on one additional domain and one model size.; Presentation and notation are sometimes imprecise or inconsistent, which makes parts of the derivation harder to verify rigorously.

Methodological Flaws: The mathematical treatment is more heuristic than rigorous. In particular, the derivation of the feasible-mixture condition from the Lagrangian objective to the slope condition dL_delta_gen/dL_delta_dom = -1/lambda relies on local derivative arguments and an assumed nonzero derivative, but this does not by itself establish feasibility of a ratio over the full training trajectory or justify the optimization interpretation claimed in the main text.; Equation (7)/(32) is implicit in T0 because T0 appears on both sides through the term T0^(s3-s2). The paper presents this as if it were an obtained solution, but it is not a closed-form solution; the numerical procedure for solving it is not clearly specified.; The optimization formulation mixes fixed and variable quantities somewhat inconsistently. For example, the constrained problem is written over S, R, T, but the practical experiments fix model size when defining feasible ratios and then fit separate laws post hoc; thus the formal optimization problem is not actually solved as stated.; The criterion for feasibility uses existence of at least one point T0 where partial F/partial T <= 0 (or equality), which is weak and may not guarantee that the final training outcome satisfies the intended CPT objective. A single local point with nonpositive derivative does not imply the trajectory remains desirable thereafter.; The empirical evidence for a power-law relationship with mixture ratio is based on fitting a small number of mixture settings. Given the low number of points, strong claims about a scaling law may be overstated, and alternative functional forms are not meaningfully compared.; The paper extrapolates CMR far beyond the observed token range (e.g., to 500B tokens) without validation. Such extrapolation is speculative, especially because the fitted general-loss curves are non-monotonic and based on limited observed ranges.; The claim that larger CMR corresponds to smaller distribution gap is plausible but not rigorously established; the paper infers this from only two domains and a rough estimate of domain prevalence in the pre-training corpus, without a quantitative similarity metric or controlled ablation.
