Summary: The paper studies continual pre-training (CPT) of language models with mixed general and domain-specific data, focusing on how to choose the mixture ratio rather than setting it heuristically. It introduces the Critical Mixture Ratio (CMR), defined as the largest domain-data proportion that still preserves general performance within a tolerance while improving domain loss. Empirically, the authors pre-train models from 460M to 3.1B parameters, then perform CPT on Finance and Academic Papers domains with varying mixture ratios. They fit simple power-law style relationships for loss as a function of mixture ratio and training tokens, and use these fits to predict CMR. Results suggest that CMR exists, tends to increase with model size, and is larger when the target domain is closer to the original pre-training distribution. The work is positioned as a practical scaling-law framework for selecting CPT data mixtures under compute constraints.

Strengths: Addresses a practically important but under-studied question in CPT: how to choose the replay/domain mixture ratio in a principled way.; Introduces a clear conceptual framing of the trade-off between preserving general capability and improving domain capability, with the notion of feasible mixture ratios and CMR.; Provides a reasonably extensive empirical study across four model sizes and two domains, rather than a single-model anecdotal analysis.; The observed trends are intuitive and potentially useful in practice: larger models tolerate higher domain-data ratios, and domains closer to the pre-training distribution admit larger CMRs.; The paper includes explicit mathematical definitions, derivations, fitted coefficients, and appendix material that improve transparency and reproducibility.; The visualizations, especially the domain-vs-general loss trajectory plots and token-scaling extrapolations, help communicate the proposed trade-off and prediction procedure.

Weaknesses: The central claims rely almost entirely on validation loss rather than downstream task performance, so the practical significance of CMR for real applications remains only indirectly supported.; The proposed notion of optimality is definition-dependent: CMR is defined as the maximum feasible ratio under a tolerance threshold, but this does not necessarily imply global optimality for all practical objectives.; The tolerance parameter epsilon is chosen empirically (0.05) and appears somewhat ad hoc; conclusions may depend materially on this choice, yet sensitivity analysis is limited.; The empirical scope is still narrow relative to the ambition of a scaling law: only two domains are studied, and the largest model is 3.1B, which is small compared with modern frontier CPT settings.; Some derivations are not fully convincing as theory; they mainly formalize empirical observations and fitted relationships rather than deriving a principled law from first principles.; The fitting forms appear somewhat hand-crafted, especially the two-term form for general-loss dynamics over tokens, raising concerns about overfitting or limited robustness outside the tested regime.; The paper does not compare strongly against alternative mixture-selection baselines beyond qualitative discussion of prior work, so it is hard to assess incremental practical benefit.; Writing quality is uneven, with grammatical issues and occasional imprecision that make some arguments harder to follow than necessary.

Soundness Issues: The paper equates the maximum feasible ratio with the 'optimal' ratio by definition, but this is a normative choice rather than a demonstrated optimum under a broadly accepted utility function.; The methodology depends on validation loss as a proxy for both general and domain capability; while correlated with downstream performance, this is not sufficient evidence that predicted CMR optimizes end-task outcomes.; The feasibility criterion depends on an externally chosen tolerance epsilon and a large Lagrange multiplier lambda; the practical interpretation and stability of these quantities are not rigorously established.; The CMR prediction law is validated only in a limited interpolation/extrapolation regime and with sparse mixture-ratio grids, so confidence in precise predicted CMR values should be moderate rather than high.; Generalization evidence is limited: only one additional domain and one model size are used for the cross-domain check, which is not enough to substantiate a broad scaling-law claim.; Several conclusions about asymptotic behavior (e.g., CMR approaching 1 with unlimited tokens) are speculative and not empirically verified.
