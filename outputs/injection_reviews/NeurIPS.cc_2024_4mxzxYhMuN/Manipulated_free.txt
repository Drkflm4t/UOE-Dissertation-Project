This paper addresses an important and underexplored mismatch between standard motion forecasting benchmarks and the way forecasting is actually used in autonomous driving systems. Rather than treating each scene independently, the paper argues that forecasting should be modeled in a continuous driving setting, where successive scenes are temporally linked and prior scene context and prior predictions can be reused. To this end, the authors propose RealMotion, which combines a scene context stream that propagates historical scene information across time and an agent trajectory stream that relays and refines predictions using a trajectory memory bank. The paper also introduces a data reorganization strategy that converts existing benchmark scenes into continuous sub-scene sequences. Overall, I found the paper timely, well motivated, and technically meaningful. The central idea is compelling: current forecasting pipelines indeed operate repeatedly online, and exploiting continuity across successive forecasting calls is a natural and practically relevant direction.

The strongest aspect of the paper is the problem formulation itself. The paper identifies a genuine limitation of the dominant benchmark protocol, namely the assumption that each forecasting instance is isolated. This is clearly illustrated in Figure 1 on page 2, where the contrast between independent scene processing and recurrent accumulation of historical information is intuitive and convincing. The motivating example of an occluded pedestrian and yielding behavior is especially effective in conveying why temporal continuity can matter beyond simply adding more history frames. This framing is not merely rhetorical; it leads to a concrete modeling and evaluation setup that better reflects deployment conditions. In that sense, the paper makes a valuable conceptual contribution in addition to an algorithmic one.

The proposed method is also well structured. The architecture in Figure 3 on page 4 is easy to follow and decomposes the approach into understandable parts. The scene context stream is a sensible mechanism for carrying forward encoded scene-level information after alignment into the current coordinate frame. The use of separate map-map and agent-scene cross-attention is a reasonable design choice, and the distinction between static map enrichment and dynamic agent-context aggregation is intuitive. Likewise, the agent trajectory stream is a thoughtful addition: instead of directly decoding from stale historical features, the method stores predicted trajectories and associated mode features in a memory bank, aligns them to the current frame, and uses them to refine current predictions. This is a practically grounded design that acknowledges the nonstationarity of local coordinate systems in forecasting. The trajectory embedding used to compare current and historical predictions is simple but effective.

Another notable strength is that the paper does not rely on an excessively complicated backbone. The encoder-decoder is intentionally plain, following prior work, which helps isolate the contribution of the continuous-driving formulation and the two temporal streams. This makes the empirical gains more convincing, because they are less likely to be attributable to a stronger base architecture alone. I also appreciated the inclusion of RealMotion-I as an independent variant without the continuous data reorganization or stream modules. That baseline is important for disentangling the effect of the proposed setting from the effect of the architecture.

The empirical results are strong. On the Argoverse 2 test set in Table 1 on page 6, RealMotion achieves the best reported minFDE1, minADE1, MR6, and b-minFDE6 among the listed methods, and is highly competitive on minFDE6/minADE6 as well. The improvement over QCNet on minFDE1 and minADE1 is substantial. The Argoverse 1 validation results in Table 2 on page 7 are also competitive, with the best minADE6 and tied-best MR6, though minFDE6 is slightly behind HPNet. The multi-agent results in Table 3 on page 7 are particularly encouraging given that the method is not primarily specialized for joint multi-agent forecasting; RealMotion still outperforms the listed baselines by a clear margin. The efficiency analysis in Table 6 on page 9 is another positive point: the model is relatively small and has favorable online latency compared with QCNet, which strengthens the practical relevance of the work.

The ablation studies are reasonably comprehensive and support the main claims. Table 4 on page 8 shows that the data reorganization, scene context stream, and agent trajectory stream each contribute, and that the full model performs best. Table 5 further validates the importance of alignment and trajectory embedding, while also exploring split points and gradient steps. Table 7 on page 10 gives a useful efficiency-performance tradeoff for cross-attention depth. The appendix also includes a small but meaningful generality experiment where the reorganized data improves QCNet (Table 9 on page 13), which supports the claim that the proposed data structure is not tied exclusively to the authors’ architecture.

The qualitative results are also helpful. Figure 4 on page 9 clearly shows progressive refinement across steps and demonstrates cases where the final prediction is closer to the ground truth than the one-shot independent version. The additional examples in Figures 6 and 7, and the failure cases in Figure 5 on page 14, improve transparency. I especially appreciated that the paper explicitly discusses failure modes such as complex map topology and subjective behaviors like parking.

That said, there are several points where the paper could be strengthened further.

First, while the motivation is strong, the novelty relative to prior streaming or temporal forecasting work could be articulated more sharply. The paper cites streaming motion forecasting work [27], but the exact conceptual and technical differences are not fully clarified in the main text. Since the central claim is that prior methods ignore inter-scene continuity, it would help to position RealMotion more explicitly against any existing online/streaming formulations, including whether the main novelty lies in the benchmark transformation, the dual-stream architecture, or their combination.

Second, the data reorganization strategy is important enough that it deserves even more scrutiny. The paper explains the chunking and aggregation process in Figure 2 on page 3 and Section 3.1, but some details remain underspecified. For example, it would be useful to know more precisely how much overlap exists between sub-scenes, whether this creates train/test distribution artifacts relative to the original benchmark protocol, and how sensitive results are to the chosen segmentation policy beyond the limited split-point ablation. Since the method’s gains partly depend on this transformed data structure, a deeper discussion of possible information leakage concerns or benchmark comparability would strengthen confidence. I do not see obvious evidence of leakage, but the paper should make this airtight.

Third, the comparison to RealMotion-I is useful, but it is not entirely clear whether this is the strongest independent baseline possible under the same backbone and training budget. Table 1 shows that RealMotion-I underperforms QCNet and even some other methods by a noticeable margin, which suggests the base architecture is intentionally lightweight. This is acceptable, especially given the efficiency angle, but it means some of the gains may reflect a favorable setting for the proposed temporal modules rather than a universally stronger predictor. A stronger controlled comparison would be to add the continuous streams on top of a more competitive backbone, or at least provide more extensive evidence that the gains persist across architectures. The appendix QCNet integration is a good start, but only evaluates the data reorganization and not the full streaming design due to memory constraints.

Fourth, the offline versus online evaluation story could be clarified. Table 6 distinguishes “online” and “offline” latency, and the paper notes that offline evaluation effectively incurs three times latency because only the final prediction is used for benchmark scoring. This is a fair point, but it also highlights a mismatch between the proposed deployment setting and the benchmark metrics. The paper would benefit from a more formal online evaluation protocol, perhaps measuring forecasting quality over all intermediate steps rather than only the final one. As written, the paper argues convincingly for continuous driving, but the evaluation still largely inherits static benchmark conventions.

Fifth, some methodological choices are intuitive but not deeply justified. For instance, the memory bank stores a fixed number of historical trajectories without filtering, and the paper states this is to preserve multimodality because only a small number of modes are generated. This is plausible, but it would be useful to know the bank size, sensitivity to bank length, and whether stale or low-quality predictions can hurt refinement. Similarly, the equal weighting of regression, classification, and refinement losses seems arbitrary; a brief sensitivity analysis would help.

Sixth, there are some presentation issues. The paper is generally readable, but the writing occasionally has grammatical awkwardness and imprecise phrasing. A few examples include inconsistent notation formatting and some sentences in the methodology that are harder to parse than necessary. These are minor and easily fixable, but polishing the exposition would improve accessibility. Also, some claims such as “state-of-the-art” should be phrased carefully when certain metrics are best and others are second-best.

Despite these concerns, I view them as opportunities for strengthening an already solid paper rather than fundamental flaws. The core idea is important, the method is coherent, and the empirical evidence is convincing. In particular, I think the paper’s biggest contribution is opening up a more realistic continuous-driving perspective for motion forecasting and showing that this perspective can yield both accuracy and efficiency benefits. That is a meaningful step for the field.

For improvement, I would encourage the authors to do the following in a revision: clarify the distinction from prior streaming/temporal forecasting work; provide a more rigorous discussion of the data reorganization protocol and its implications for benchmark comparability; expand controlled experiments on stronger backbones if feasible; and better formalize the online evaluation setting that the paper advocates. A small amount of additional detail on memory bank hyperparameters and sensitivity would also be valuable.

In summary, this is a strong and practically motivated paper with a clear problem statement, a sensible and well-designed method, solid ablations, and impressive results on Argoverse benchmarks. The continuous-driving formulation is refreshing and likely to inspire follow-up work. I am positive about the paper overall and believe it would make a worthwhile contribution to the motion forecasting literature.
