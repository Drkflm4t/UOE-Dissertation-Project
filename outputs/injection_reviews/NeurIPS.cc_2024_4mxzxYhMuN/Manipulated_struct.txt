Summary: This paper studies motion forecasting under a more realistic continuous-driving setting, arguing that standard benchmarks and methods treat each scene independently and therefore miss useful temporal context across successive forecasting steps. The proposed framework, RealMotion, introduces two recurrent scene-level streams: a scene context stream that carries forward historical scene representations via alignment and cross-attention, and an agent trajectory stream that relays prior predicted trajectories through a memory bank to refine current predictions. The authors also propose a data reorganization strategy that converts existing benchmark scenes into short continuous sub-scene sequences. Experiments on Argoverse 1 and 2, including single-agent and multi-agent settings, show strong results, with particularly strong Argoverse 2 leaderboard performance and favorable latency/parameter tradeoffs relative to several baselines.

Strengths: The paper addresses an important and practically relevant mismatch between standard motion forecasting benchmarks and real deployment: forecasting is performed continuously over time rather than on isolated scenes.; The proposed formulation is conceptually novel in this literature: leveraging cross-scene temporal context at the scene level and relaying prior predictions is a meaningful extension beyond one-shot forecasting.; The method is reasonably well structured, with two clearly motivated components (scene context stream and agent trajectory stream) that target complementary sources of temporal information.; The data reorganization strategy is useful and potentially impactful beyond this specific model, since it can be applied to existing benchmarks and even improves QCNet in the appendix.; Experimental results are strong overall, especially on Argoverse 2 single-agent and multi-agent benchmarks, and the ablations support that each major component contributes.; The efficiency discussion is a plus: RealMotion appears substantially smaller than several strong baselines while maintaining competitive online latency.; The paper includes qualitative examples, failure cases, and multiple ablations on alignment, trajectory embedding, split points, gradient steps, and attention depth, which improves transparency.

Weaknesses: A central concern is that part of the gain may come from changing the data formulation rather than purely from the model; while the paper includes an independent variant and some QCNet integration evidence, the comparison against prior methods is still not fully apples-to-apples because most baselines were not retrained under the same continuous data setup.; The proposed data reorganization may introduce overlap/correlation among sub-scenes derived from the same original sample, and the paper does not fully discuss whether this changes the effective training distribution or risks optimistic evaluation if not carefully separated.; The methodology is somewhat heuristic in places, especially the trajectory memory bank design, split-point selection, and the choice to refine predictions via stored trajectories rather than jointly model latent temporal state more formally.; The paper claims practical continuous-driving relevance, but evaluation is still conducted on reorganized offline benchmarks rather than a true streaming benchmark or closed-loop system, so the real-world benefit remains only partially validated.; Some comparisons are slightly hard to interpret: for example, RealMotion is best on several metrics but not uniformly all metrics, and Argoverse 1 results are strong but not clearly dominant on minFDE6 versus the best prior method.; The paper would benefit from clearer discussion of training/inference protocol fairness, especially because offline evaluation uses three segments while online latency is reported differently.; There is limited analysis of robustness under long horizons, drift accumulation, or memory-bank failure modes, which are especially relevant for recurrent/streaming forecasting.

Soundness Issues: The overall idea is plausible and empirically supported, but the causal attribution of gains is not fully isolated: benchmark reorganization, extra supervision across segments, and recurrent temporal modeling are intertwined.; It is not entirely clear whether the reorganized training/evaluation setup preserves strict comparability with standard benchmark protocols; more detail is needed on how sub-scenes are generated for train/val/test and whether any information leakage or duplicated context could occur across segments.; The memory-bank refinement mechanism relies on previous predictions, which may propagate errors over time; while qualitative examples suggest benefits, the paper lacks a deeper quantitative analysis of error accumulation or recovery.; The claim of 'state-of-the-art' is mostly supported on Argoverse 2 single-agent and multi-agent tables shown, but evidence is weaker for universal superiority across datasets/settings, especially since Argoverse 1 does not clearly dominate all metrics.; The method appears sound engineering-wise, but some design choices (equal loss weights, FIFO memory without filtering, specific split points) seem empirically chosen rather than theoretically justified.
