This paper proposes a pretraining-and-transfer framework for EEG-to-text decoding. The core technical contribution is CET-MAE, a multimodal self-supervised model that combines masked reconstruction of EEG and text with EEG-text contrastive alignment through a multi-stream encoder. The downstream contribution is E2T-PTR, which transfers the pretrained EEG-side modules into a BART-based EEG-to-text decoder. Experiments on ZuCo report improvements over prior work on BLEU and ROUGE, along with ablations on masking ratio, sentence-level EEG inclusion, and the role of the pretraining objectives.

Overall, I found the paper interesting and timely. EEG-to-text decoding is an important but still immature area, and the paper addresses a real gap: most prior work either relies heavily on supervised alignment to language models or focuses on EEG-only pretraining, whereas this work attempts to learn transferable cross-modal representations between EEG and text. The proposed combination of contrastive alignment and masked reconstruction is sensible, and the empirical gains on ZuCo are nontrivial. The paper is also generally well motivated, and the architecture figure on page 4 is helpful in conveying the relationship between CET-MAE pretraining and E2T-PTR fine-tuning.

That said, I also have several concerns about evaluation rigor, fairness of comparison, and the interpretation of the reported gains. My overall assessment is positive but somewhat cautious: the paper appears to make a meaningful empirical contribution, but some claims are stronger than what the evidence fully supports.

The main strengths are as follows. First, the problem formulation is important. Open-vocabulary EEG-to-text decoding is substantially more challenging and more impactful than closed-vocabulary imagined speech or word classification settings. Second, the proposed pretraining objective is well motivated. The paper correctly notes that contrastive learning alone may emphasize global alignment while neglecting modality-specific structure, whereas masked modeling alone may overemphasize local reconstruction without sufficiently enforcing pairwise semantic matching. CET-MAE’s combination of masked text modeling, masked EEG modeling, and EEG-text contrastive learning is therefore conceptually reasonable. Third, the multi-stream design is a plausible architectural mechanism for separating modality-specific processing from joint interaction. The diagram on page 4 clearly shows text, EEG, and joint streams, with reconstruction and contrastive losses attached in a coherent way. Fourth, the empirical results are promising. In Table 1 on page 6, E2T-PTR improves over EEG2Text and DeWave on the three-task setting, and also improves over the reproduced EEG2Text baseline on the five-task setting. The ablations in Tables 5 and 6 on page 8 suggest that sentence-level EEG features, CET-MAE pretraining, and the combined SSL objective each contribute.

The paper’s treatment of the data is also thoughtful in one respect. The authors identify a genuine issue in ZuCo: missing eye-tracking fixations create fragmented word-level EEG-text alignment. Their solution of concatenating word-level EEG features with sentence-level EEG features is intuitive and appears empirically useful. The statistics in Appendix A on page 12 quantify the natural masking ratio, which is a useful diagnostic. Likewise, the forced masking of sentence-level EEG during pretraining is an interesting design choice, and Table 15 on page 15 suggests it helps.

However, there are several important weaknesses.

The first major concern is evaluation protocol and comparability. The paper compares against prior work across different training sample counts and, in at least one case, against reproduced baselines rather than original reported numbers. Table 1 on page 6 mixes results from 10,710, 14,407, and 18,791 training samples. This makes the headline comparisons harder to interpret. For example, the comparison to C-SCL is especially awkward because the paper notes differences in repeated-word handling and reports both original and reproduced numbers in parentheses. The paper should be much clearer about which comparisons are strictly apples-to-apples. If the main claim is SOTA, then all baselines should ideally be rerun under the exact same data splits, preprocessing, decoding settings, and postprocessing rules. As written, the evidence supports “improved performance under the authors’ setup,” but the broader SOTA claim is somewhat less secure.

A second concern is the leave-one-subject-out result in Table 4 on page 8. It is surprising that leave-one-subject-out cross-validation yields substantially better performance than the standard split-by-sample strategy. Normally, subject-independent evaluation is harder, not easier, because of inter-subject variability. The paper interprets this as evidence of strong generalization, but this result raises questions about whether the two protocols differ in more than just subject partitioning. For example, are the training set sizes effectively larger in the leave-one-subject-out setting? Are there sentence overlaps or task-distribution differences that make the comparison favorable? Without a careful explanation, this result is difficult to interpret and may indicate a confound in the evaluation setup rather than superior generalization.

A third concern is that the paper’s central pretraining claim would benefit from stronger analysis of what is actually being transferred. The ablations show that CET-MAE helps, but the gains over CET-only and ET-MAE-only in Table 6 on page 8 are relatively modest. This does not invalidate the method, but it suggests that the combined objective may provide incremental rather than transformative benefit. More importantly, the paper does not deeply analyze representation quality beyond downstream BLEU/ROUGE. For a pretraining paper, I would expect stronger evidence that the learned EEG-text latent space is meaningfully aligned: retrieval metrics, matching accuracy, probing analyses, or visualization of paired versus unpaired embeddings would help substantiate the representation-learning claims.

A fourth concern is the reliance on teacher forcing and the ambiguity around decoding realism. The limitations section on page 9 acknowledges that current EEG-to-text systems, including this one, depend on teacher forcing. This is an important caveat and should be more prominent earlier in the paper, because it materially affects how one interprets the task. If the decoder is conditioned in a way that uses ground-truth prefixes during training and perhaps evaluation, then BLEU/ROUGE improvements do not necessarily translate into practical free-form brain-to-language decoding capability. The paper is honest about this limitation, which I appreciate, but the framing in the introduction and conclusion occasionally sounds more ambitious than the actual evaluation setting warrants.

A fifth concern is that the qualitative examples are mixed and sometimes reveal substantial decoding errors. The examples in Table 2 on page 6 and Tables 10–12 in the appendix show some encouraging phrase-level recovery, especially in the five-task setting where several sentences are nearly reconstructed. But many outputs remain heavily distorted, with hallucinated names, malformed syntax, and repeated tokens. This is not a criticism of the authors specifically—EEG-to-text is extremely hard—but it does suggest that the practical significance of the metric gains should be discussed more carefully. In particular, the paper sometimes interprets semantically related substitutions quite generously. For example, replacing a named entity with another named entity is not necessarily a benign semantic resemblance in many applications.

I also have some methodological questions that should be clarified.

The masking strategy for EEG is unusual and potentially important, but the description is not fully precise. The paper states in Section 3.2 that word-level EEG tokens are randomly masked while sentence-level EEG tokens are compulsorily masked. Since the sentence-level EEG appears to be a single appended token in Equation (1), it would help to explicitly state how the overall 75% masking ratio is computed in the presence of mandatory masking of that token. The discussion on page 7 about “overall masking ratio” combining natural masking and CET-MAE masking is interesting, but somewhat confusing. A more formal definition would improve reproducibility.

The role of BART also needs clearer exposition. In Section 3.3, the paper says it uses the pretrained encoder-decoder model BART as the text encoder, but then specifically mentions freezing the BART encoder. Later, E2T-PTR uses BART as the downstream decoder and fully fine-tunes it. It would help to explicitly distinguish which BART components are used in pretraining versus fine-tuning, whether weights are shared or reinitialized, and whether the text-side pretraining benefits are due mainly to inheriting a strong language model rather than learning EEG-text alignment. A stronger baseline would be to compare against an equally parameterized BART-based model without CET-MAE pretraining but with the same sentence-level EEG augmentation and architecture.

Relatedly, the fairness of the baseline architecture deserves more attention. The ablation in Table 5 on page 8 starts from a system without sentence-level EEG, CET-MAE, or E2T-PTR, then adds components incrementally. This is useful, but it does not fully isolate whether gains come from pretraining, architecture changes, or simply increased input information. Since sentence-level EEG is added as an extra token and appears beneficial on its own, the paper should more explicitly separate “better input representation” from “better SSL pretraining.”

The paper would also benefit from stronger statistical reporting. The reported improvements are often on the order of fractions of a BLEU point to a couple of points. Given the small dataset sizes and high variance typical in EEG tasks, confidence intervals or significance tests would be valuable. This is especially true for Table 6, where the differences among CET, ET-MAE, and CET-MAE are small. Without variance estimates across runs, it is hard to know how robust these gains are.

Another issue is that the paper’s claims about applicability to inner speech BCIs are speculative. The data come from text-evoked EEG during reading, not imagined speech or spontaneous language production. The authors do mention this as a future direction, which is appropriate, but some statements in the abstract and conclusion could be read as implying broader readiness than the evidence supports. Transfer from reading-evoked EEG to inner speech is far from established.

On presentation, the paper is mostly understandable, but there are several writing issues. Some sentences are grammatically awkward or repetitive, and there are occasional terminology inconsistencies. For example, the paper sometimes says “masked signal modeling,” sometimes “masked EEG modeling,” and sometimes “MAE loss.” The notation is also somewhat light for a methods paper. The contrastive loss is described conceptually but not written explicitly; similarly, the exact form of the text modeling loss and the pooling strategy could be specified more formally. These are not fatal issues, but tightening the exposition would improve clarity.

Despite these concerns, I think the paper has real merit. The idea of multimodal EEG-text pretraining is novel in this context, the architecture is plausible, and the empirical results suggest that the approach is useful. The appendix also adds value: the tables on pages 12 and 15 provide useful details on natural masking ratios, subject-wise variability, masking strategy, and the multi-stream design. In particular, Table 16 on page 15 supports the claim that using the EEG-specific stream for downstream transfer is better than relying on a joint stream alone.

If I were recommending improvements before publication, my highest-priority requests would be the following. First, strengthen the evaluation protocol and clarify all comparisons so that every baseline is matched on data split, sample count, and decoding/postprocessing settings. Second, explain the leave-one-subject-out result much more carefully, since it currently appears counterintuitive. Third, add statistical significance or multi-seed variance reporting. Fourth, provide at least one direct representation-level analysis to support the claim that CET-MAE learns better transferable EEG-text alignment. Fifth, moderate the claims about practical EEG-to-text decoding and inner speech applicability, given the teacher-forcing setup and the use of reading-evoked EEG.

In summary, this is a promising paper with a meaningful idea and encouraging results. Its main contribution is not just a better decoder, but a multimodal pretraining strategy tailored to EEG-text alignment. I believe the work is novel enough and empirically strong enough to be of interest to the community. My enthusiasm is tempered by concerns about evaluation rigor and claim calibration, but overall I view this as a solid contribution that would be strengthened substantially by clearer experimental controls and deeper analysis.
