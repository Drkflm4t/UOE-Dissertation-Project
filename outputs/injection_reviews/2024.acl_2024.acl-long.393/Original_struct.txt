Summary: The paper proposes CET-MAE, a multimodal self-supervised pretraining framework for EEG-text representation learning, and E2T-PTR, a downstream EEG-to-text decoding system that transfers the pretrained EEG-side representations into a BART-based generator. The method combines three objectives: masked text modeling, masked EEG reconstruction, and EEG-text contrastive learning, using a multi-stream encoder with separate EEG/text streams and a joint stream. The work is evaluated on ZuCo v1.0/v2.0 reading-task combinations (3, 4, and 5 tasks), reporting improvements over prior EEG-to-text systems such as EEG2Text, DeWave, and C-SCL on BLEU and ROUGE metrics. Ablations examine sentence-level EEG tokens, pretraining, multi-stream design, masking strategy, and the contribution of contrastive vs masked modeling. The paper is ambitious and addresses an important problem, but several methodological and evaluation issues weaken the strength of the empirical claims.

Strengths: Addresses an important and challenging problem: open-vocabulary EEG-to-text decoding from non-invasive recordings.; Introduces a reasonably novel multimodal pretraining formulation that combines cross-modal contrastive learning with within-modality masked reconstruction for EEG and text.; The architecture is clearly motivated: separate modality streams plus a joint stream are a sensible design for balancing alignment and reconstruction.; The paper includes multiple ablations, including sentence-level EEG features, masking strategy, multi-stream design, and comparison against reduced SSL variants (contrastive-only and masked-only).; Uses both parsed EEG word-level features and sentence-level EEG features, explicitly acknowledging the severe missingness/misalignment in ZuCo and attempting to mitigate it.; Results on standard automatic metrics show consistent gains over reproduced baselines in several settings, especially over EEG2Text and DeWave in the 3-task setup.; The paper is generally well organized, and figures/tables (especially Figure 2 and Tables 1, 5, 6, 15, 16) help communicate the method and ablations.

Weaknesses: The evaluation protocol appears problematic for making strong claims about EEG-to-text decoding. The paper itself acknowledges reliance on teacher forcing, which substantially limits the realism and interpretability of the reported generation performance.; The comparison across prior work is not fully clean: some baselines are reproduced, some are quoted, and some metrics are reported with/without removing repeated words, making direct comparison difficult.; The reported leave-one-subject-out results being better than the standard split results are counterintuitive and raise concerns about data leakage, split mismatch, or evaluation inconsistency.; The gains from CET-MAE over simpler SSL variants are quite small in absolute terms (Table 6), suggesting the claimed superiority of the full objective may be modest.; The paper heavily relies on the ZuCo benchmark only; there is no external dataset validation, no robustness analysis beyond subject splits, and no evidence that the learned representations generalize beyond this specific corpus/setup.; Qualitative examples still show many severe decoding errors, hallucinations, repetitions, and grammatical failures, especially in the 3-task and 4-task settings, which weakens the practical significance of the metric gains.; The method introduces sentence-level EEG as an appended token while also force-masking it during pretraining; the scientific justification for why this specific design is optimal is not deeply analyzed.; Important implementation choices appear tuned by trial-and-error (e.g., 75% masking ratio, loss weights), but there is limited principled sensitivity analysis.; The paper sometimes overstates conclusions, e.g., suggesting strong implications for inner speech BCIs despite experiments being only on text-evoked reading EEG, which is a substantially easier and different paradigm.

Methodological Flaws: Potential evaluation leakage/confounding: the paper states that pretraining uses a 90/10 train-test split and fine-tuning uses an 80/10/10 split, with the test set samples remaining consistent across stages. If the CET-MAE pretraining stage has access to downstream test EEG-text pairs (even self-supervised), this can inflate downstream performance and compromises a strict held-out evaluation of transfer.; The leave-one-subject-out cross-validation results (Table 4) outperform the easier within-subject random split, which is scientifically unexpected for EEG due to strong subject variability. This suggests either the two protocols are not directly comparable, the metric aggregation differs, or there may be leakage/duplication across sentence/task instances. The paper does not adequately explain this anomaly.; The paper claims open-vocabulary EEG-to-text decoding, but the decoding setup still depends on teacher forcing according to the limitations section. This undermines the validity of interpreting BLEU/ROUGE as reflecting true free-form decoding ability from EEG alone.; Empirical comparisons are not fully apples-to-apples. In Table 1, C-SCL is shown with different training sample counts and with special handling of repeated words; some baselines are reproduced while others are cited from prior work. This weakens the validity of SOTA claims.; The use of BLEU/ROUGE alone is insufficient for this task, especially given the qualitative outputs often contain semantically wrong named entities and hallucinated content. No human evaluation, semantic similarity metric, or calibration/error analysis is provided.; The paper does not provide statistical significance testing or variance estimates across runs. Given the small absolute improvements in several ablations and SSL comparisons, it is unclear whether gains are robust.; The mathematical formulation is under-specified in important places: the exact contrastive loss form, temperature, negative sampling scheme, masking implementation details, and how pooled representations are normalized are omitted from the main method, limiting reproducibility and making it hard to assess correctness.; The rationale for computing an 'overall masking ratio' by combining natural missingness with artificial masking is not clearly tied to a theoretical or empirical objective; this discussion risks conflating dataset corruption with deliberate SSL masking in a way that is not scientifically rigorous.; The claim that sentence-level EEG acts as 'data augmentation' is conceptually loose; appending an additional correlated feature token is not augmentation in the standard sense, and the paper does not isolate whether gains come from extra information, regularization, or leakage from sentence-level summaries.
