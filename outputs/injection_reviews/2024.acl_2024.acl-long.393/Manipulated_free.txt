This paper tackles EEG-to-text decoding through a pretraining-and-transfer approach that combines multimodal self-supervision with an LLM-based decoder. The core proposal is CET-MAE, a contrastive EEG-text masked autoencoder that jointly uses masked reconstruction for both modalities and contrastive alignment between EEG and text, followed by E2T-PTR, a downstream EEG-to-text framework that transfers the pretrained EEG-side representations into a BART-based generator. The paper is timely and addresses an important problem: open-vocabulary language decoding from non-invasive EEG remains extremely challenging, and the field is still limited by small datasets, noisy alignment, and weak cross-modal representation learning. Overall, I found the paper interesting, technically meaningful, and empirically fairly strong. The work is a solid step toward more principled EEG-language pretraining.

The main strength of the paper is the formulation itself. The authors identify a real gap in prior work: existing EEG pretraining efforts are mostly unimodal, while EEG-to-text systems often rely on direct supervised alignment to a language model without a dedicated multimodal pretraining stage. CET-MAE is a sensible response to this gap. The combination of contrastive learning and masked modeling is well motivated by analogous developments in vision-language and audio-visual learning, and the adaptation to EEG-text is nontrivial because of the severe noise, sparsity, and imperfect alignment in text-evoked EEG. I also appreciated the explicit handling of both word-level and sentence-level EEG features, motivated by the natural masking/missingness induced by eye-fixation alignment in ZuCo. The discussion around natural masking ratio and the concatenation of sentence-level EEG as an additional token is one of the more practically insightful aspects of the paper.

The architecture is clearly presented. Figure 2 on page 4 is especially helpful: it distinguishes the text stream, EEG stream, and joint stream in CET-MAE, and then shows how the pretrained EEG encoder, projection layer, and EEG-stream transformer are transferred into E2T-PTR. This multi-stream design is conceptually appealing. The modality-specific streams support contrastive alignment while preserving modality-specific structure, and the joint stream supports reconstruction and cross-modal interaction. The masking strategy is also interesting: random masking for word-level EEG and compulsory masking for sentence-level EEG. This is a somewhat unusual but intuitively reasonable design choice, since the sentence-level token is intended to force recovery of global semantics.

Empirically, the paper presents a reasonably comprehensive set of experiments on ZuCo. The main results in Table 1 on page 6 show consistent gains over EEG2Text and DeWave under the three-task setting, and gains over the reproduced EEG2Text baseline under the five-task setting. The improvements are not merely on one metric; they appear across BLEU-1 through BLEU-4 and ROUGE-1. For example, under three reading tasks, E2T-PTR improves BLEU-4 from 8.22 for DeWave to 8.99 and ROUGE-1 F1 from 30.69 to 32.61. Under five tasks, the gains over EEG2Text are also consistent. In a domain where progress is typically incremental and data are scarce, these improvements are meaningful.

The ablations are another positive aspect. Table 5 on page 8 shows that each of the three main ingredients contributes: sentence-level EEG features, CET-MAE pretraining, and the full E2T-PTR transfer framework. Table 6 further compares CET-MAE against contrastive-only and masked-autoencoding-only variants, supporting the claim that the hybrid SSL objective is better than either component alone. Table 15 on page 15 also provides evidence that the forced masking strategy for sentence-level EEG is beneficial relative to random masking. Table 16 supports the multi-stream design by showing that transferring the EEG-specific stream works better than using a joint stream alone. These analyses make the paper more convincing than a simple benchmark-improvement submission.

I also appreciated the qualitative examples. The examples in Table 2 on page 6 and the extended examples in Tables 10–12 on pages 13–14 show that the model often captures partial semantics, named entities, and grammatical scaffolding better as training data scale increases. The five-task examples are particularly striking: some outputs are nearly exact reproductions of the target sentence, which is impressive given the difficulty of the task. At the same time, the examples honestly reveal the remaining limitations, such as hallucinated names, duplicated words, and syntactic corruption. This transparency is welcome.

That said, there are several issues that should be addressed or at least clarified.

My biggest concern is evaluation protocol and comparability. The paper states that it follows the preprocessing and splits of Wang and Ji (2022), but there are multiple training regimes across three, four, and five reading tasks, and some baselines are reproduced while others are quoted from prior work. In Table 1, C-SCL is reported with a different number of training samples than E2T-PTR, and the paper notes that repeated generated words are handled differently in some cases. This makes direct comparison somewhat messy. The authors do try to mitigate this by reporting reproduced baselines and parenthetical scores, but the presentation still leaves ambiguity about whether all methods are being compared under exactly the same data split, preprocessing, decoding settings, and postprocessing. For a benchmark paper in a small-data regime, this matters a lot. I would strongly encourage the authors to standardize the comparison table more carefully and explicitly separate “reported from prior paper” versus “reproduced under our setup.”

A second concern is the leave-one-subject-out result in Table 4 on page 8. The paper claims that leave-one-subject-out cross-validation exceeds the standard split performance, and interprets this as evidence of strong generalization. This is counterintuitive: LOSO is usually harder, not easier. The likely explanation is that the effective training data in each fold are much larger than in the 8:1:1 split, but then the comparison is not apples-to-apples. As written, the discussion risks overstating subject generalization. The authors should clarify exactly how the folds are constructed, whether sentences overlap across subjects, and whether the model may benefit from seeing the same textual content from other subjects during training. If so, LOSO here measures cross-subject transfer under shared sentence content, not full subject-independent generalization to unseen content. This distinction is important.

A third issue is the role of teacher forcing and what exactly is meant by “open-vocabulary EEG-to-text decoding.” The limitation section on page 9 acknowledges dependence on teacher forcing, which is good, but the paper should be more precise earlier. If decoding relies on teacher-forced training with paired text and standard seq2seq fine-tuning, then the challenge is not fully comparable to unconstrained autoregressive decoding from EEG alone. This does not invalidate the contribution, but the framing should be more careful. In particular, some readers may infer stronger decoding capability than is currently demonstrated.

There are also some methodological details that need clarification. The paper says the text encoder uses BART, but Section 3.2 mentions masking text tokens “followed by processing with BERT,” which seems inconsistent. I assume the authors mean BART-style token embeddings or simply masked text processing using the BART encoder, but this should be corrected. Similarly, the exact dimensionalities and tokenization of the EEG sequence could be described more cleanly. The paper says each EEG token is 840-dimensional and that sentence-level EEG is appended as the last token, but it would help to specify the typical sequence lengths, how missing word-level EEG positions are represented before masking, and whether sentence-level EEG is always present. The loss weighting coefficients are given, but there is little discussion of sensitivity beyond saying they were tuned to balance gradients.

Another concern is the strength of the empirical gains relative to model scale and training complexity. CET-MAE introduces several components, and E2T-PTR fully fine-tunes BART. The gains over simpler SSL variants in Table 6 are real but modest. This is not fatal, but it means the paper’s strongest claim should be practical effectiveness rather than a dramatic conceptual breakthrough. I would have liked to see either parameter-matched comparisons or some analysis of computational cost versus gain. Even a brief discussion of pretraining time and fine-tuning overhead would help readers assess the tradeoff.

The qualitative interpretation occasionally overreaches. For example, the paper highlights semantically similar substitutions such as “appointed” versus “elected,” but in some examples the substitutions are not clearly supported by EEG evidence and may simply reflect language-model priors. This is a general challenge in EEG-to-text work: strong decoders can produce plausible text even when the neural signal is weak. The paper would benefit from a more cautious discussion of how much of the output is attributable to EEG information versus textual prior. One possible way to strengthen this in future versions would be to include control experiments with shuffled EEG-text pairs, degraded EEG, or a text-only prior baseline conditioned on metadata or prompt templates.

I also think the paper could do more to analyze what is being learned in CET-MAE. Since the central claim is about transferable EEG-text representations, some representation-level evidence would be valuable: retrieval accuracy between EEG and text embeddings, clustering by sentence semantics, probing of subject invariance, or visualization of alignment before and after pretraining. Right now, the evidence is mostly downstream generation quality. That is useful, but somewhat indirect for validating the representation-learning story.

On writing and presentation, the paper is generally understandable, but there are many grammatical issues, awkward phrasings, and a few inconsistencies. Examples include “bridging EEG and text has been ignored,” “Our goal L is minimizing is the summation,” and the BERT/BART inconsistency noted above. Some tables are also hard to interpret quickly, especially Table 1 with mixed reproduced and reported numbers. These are fixable and do not undermine the technical content, but the camera-ready version should be edited carefully.

Despite these concerns, I view the paper positively. The problem is important, the proposed method is well motivated, the architecture is coherent, and the empirical study is broader than average for this area. The paper’s strongest contributions are the first explicit EEG-text multimodal pretraining formulation of this kind, the practical handling of missingness via sentence-level EEG augmentation and masking, and the demonstration that such pretraining can improve downstream EEG-to-text generation. The work is not definitive yet, and some claims around generalization and evaluation should be tightened, but it is a meaningful contribution that should be of interest to researchers in multimodal learning, neural decoding, and brain-computer interfaces.

My recommendation is positive. I would support acceptance, provided the authors clarify the evaluation protocol, clean up the methodological inconsistencies, and moderate a few interpretive claims. The paper opens a promising direction for EEG-language foundation modeling and provides enough technical and empirical substance to merit publication.
