Summary: The paper proposes a two-stage framework for EEG-to-text decoding. First, it introduces CET-MAE, a multimodal self-supervised pretraining method that combines masked text modeling, masked EEG reconstruction, and EEG-text contrastive learning through modality-specific EEG/text streams plus a joint stream. Second, it proposes E2T-PTR, which transfers the pretrained EEG-side representations from CET-MAE into a BART-based decoder for open-vocabulary text generation from EEG. Experiments on the ZuCo 1.0/2.0 reading datasets under 3-, 4-, and 5-task settings show consistent improvements over prior EEG-to-text baselines on BLEU and ROUGE metrics, with ablations on sentence-level EEG features, masking strategy, and SSL objective design.

Strengths: Addresses an important and challenging problem: open-vocabulary EEG-to-text decoding for non-invasive BCIs.; The proposed CET-MAE is a reasonably novel multimodal pretraining design for EEG-text alignment, combining cross-modal contrastive learning with within-modality reconstruction in a unified architecture.; The paper is technically well motivated: it explicitly targets both semantic alignment across modalities and reconstruction-based learning within modalities.; The use of sentence-level EEG features to mitigate missing word-level fixation-aligned EEG is a practical and well-motivated design choice given the ZuCo data characteristics.; Experimental results show consistent gains over strong prior baselines such as EEG2Text and DeWave in the 3-task setting, and improvements over reproduced EEG2Text in the 5-task setting.; The paper includes several useful ablations: effect of sentence-level EEG, CET-MAE pretraining, E2T-PTR transfer, masking ratios, forced vs random masking, and comparison against contrastive-only / masked-only SSL variants.; The appendix provides substantial implementation detail, dataset statistics, subject-level analyses, and qualitative decoding examples.; The work is clearly positioned relative to prior multimodal SSL and EEG-to-text literature, and the architecture figure is informative.

Weaknesses: The empirical gains, while consistent, are modest in several settings, especially in the 4-task comparison where ROUGE-1 F1 is slightly below the reproduced C-SCL result and BLEU-3/4 are not uniformly better.; The evaluation relies heavily on n-gram overlap metrics (BLEU/ROUGE), which may be insufficient for assessing semantic fidelity in noisy open-ended generation.; Some qualitative examples still contain severe grammatical and factual errors, indicating that practical decoding quality remains limited despite metric improvements.; The paper compares against reproduced baselines in some settings, which introduces uncertainty about fairness unless reproduction details are fully validated.; The role of eye-tracking information remains somewhat entangled with the EEG representation pipeline because word-level alignment depends on fixation-derived segmentation in ZuCo.; The presentation occasionally overstates conclusions, e.g., suggesting strong generalization from leave-one-subject-out results without fully clarifying whether preprocessing, hyperparameter tuning, and model selection were strictly isolated from test-subject information.

Methodological Flaws: A major evaluation concern is the leave-one-subject-out result in Table 4 being better than the standard split result. Cross-subject generalization is typically harder than random within-subject splitting, so this counterintuitive outcome raises questions about split construction, sample overlap, subject/task distribution differences, or possible leakage. The paper does not sufficiently explain this.; The paper acknowledges dependence on 'implicit use of teacher forcing' but does not clearly formalize how decoding is performed at test time versus training time. If ground-truth prefixes or related supervision are used during evaluation, this could materially inflate generation performance and should be clarified precisely.; The claim of 'state-of-the-art' is only partially supported because comparisons are not uniform across all task settings and some baselines are reproduced rather than taken from identical published protocols.; The masking-ratio analysis is based on trial-and-error and limited settings; there is no statistical significance testing, confidence interval reporting, or multiple-seed analysis, making it hard to assess robustness of the reported gains.; The paper motivates sentence-level EEG concatenation partly as data augmentation and semantic completion, but does not isolate whether gains come from added information, increased token count, or leakage of sentence-level context that may make decoding easier in a way not comparable to prior setups.; The qualitative examples reveal that many outputs remain semantically incorrect despite acceptable BLEU/ROUGE, suggesting that the empirical validity of the claimed semantic decoding improvements is weaker than the headline metrics imply.
