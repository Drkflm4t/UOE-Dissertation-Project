This paper studies the approximation quality of Bayesian coresets through general upper and lower bounds on KL divergence, with the explicit goal of moving beyond the restrictive assumptions that dominate prior theory. The central message is clear and important: while Bayesian coresets are empirically useful in a broad range of nonclassical posterior settings, the theory has lagged behind, and this paper substantially closes that gap. Overall, I found the paper technically strong, well motivated, and genuinely insightful. It makes a meaningful theoretical contribution to scalable Bayesian inference, and it does so in a way that also clarifies practical algorithmic consequences.

The main strengths are the breadth and sharpness of the theoretical results. On the lower-bound side, the paper develops general asymptotic KL lower bounds under assumptions that are much milder than those used in earlier coreset analyses. In particular, the results do not rely on exponential-family structure, strong log-concavity, or posterior normality in the usual global sense. Theorems 3.3 and 3.5 are the core contributions here: they reduce posterior approximation difficulty to properties of weighted sums of score-like quantities and local curvature, which is both elegant and useful. I especially appreciated that the lower bounds are not merely abstract impossibility statements; they are leveraged to derive concrete consequences for widely used construction strategies. The conclusion that importance-weighted coresets require coreset size proportional to dataset size to maintain bounded KL, and that even optimal post-hoc scaling only improves this to a logarithmic lower bound, is a strong and practically relevant negative result. Likewise, the dimension-dependent lower bound showing that any construction needs more than d points in locally d-parameter settings is conceptually clean and likely to be broadly cited.

The upper-bound side is also compelling. Lemma 5.1 gives a very general KL upper bound in terms of exponential moments under the true posterior, and Theorem 5.3 turns this into a usable criterion via the proposed multivariate subexponentiality condition. This is a nice abstraction: it is weaker and more flexible than the assumptions in prior work, yet still strong enough to yield interpretable guarantees. Proposition 5.4 is particularly valuable because it shows that this condition is not exotic; under local finiteness of the moment generating function, one obtains subexponentiality with covariance structure as the controlling matrix. This makes the framework feel principled rather than ad hoc. The application to subsample-optimize methods in Corollary 6.1 is then a satisfying payoff: the paper explains why these methods can succeed with polylogarithmic coreset size in a broad class of models, substantially extending prior positive theory.

A major virtue of the paper is that it does not stop at theorem statements but uses them to explain empirical phenomena that practitioners have observed for years. The analysis of importance-weighted constructions is a good example. These methods are simple and computationally attractive, but often disappointing in practice; the paper gives a convincing theoretical explanation for why. Conversely, the positive analysis of subsample-optimize methods aligns with recent empirical success and helps distinguish which algorithmic ingredients matter. This kind of theory-to-practice bridge is exactly what one hopes for in a mature theoretical ML paper.

The experimental section is modest in scale but well chosen and effective for validation. The two synthetic models are not generic toy examples; they are deliberately selected to violate assumptions common in the literature: the multimodal, unidentifiable Cauchy location model with heavy-tailed prior, and the unidentifiable logistic regression model with heavy-tailed prior and persistent posterior heavy tails. The posterior visualizations on page 6 are useful in conveying the pathologies of these settings, and Figures 2 and 3 provide direct empirical support for the theoretical claims. In particular, the scaling trends in Figure 2 match the predicted lower-bound behavior for importance-weighted methods, while Figure 3 shows that subsample-optimize constructions can indeed maintain very small KL with logarithmic coreset size. For a theory paper, this level of empirical validation is appropriate and convincing.

I also found the exposition generally strong. The paper is mathematically dense, but the high-level narrative is coherent: Section 3 develops lower bounds, Section 4 turns them into algorithmic consequences, Section 5 develops upper bounds, and Section 6 applies them to modern coreset constructions. The basic KL lower and upper lemmas are useful organizing devices. The proofs in the appendix appear careful and substantial, and the assumptions are stated explicitly. The paper also does a good job of clarifying what is and is not required by the theory. For example, the discussion around local concentration sets B and the avoidance of direct control over coreset-tail behavior is insightful.

That said, there are a few limitations and points where the paper could be improved. First, while the subexponentiality framework is elegant, its practical verifiability remains somewhat indirect. The paper acknowledges in the conclusion that both the subexponentiality constant in Proposition 5.4 and the alignment probability in Corollary 6.1 currently require case-by-case analysis. This is a real limitation because it leaves some gap between the general theorem and turnkey applicability. I do not view this as a serious flaw—the paper is already making a substantial advance—but it would strengthen the work if the authors could add more guidance, perhaps in the main text or appendix, on how one might verify these conditions in common model classes beyond the two examples considered.

Second, the empirical section is intentionally narrow and focused on validation rather than benchmarking. That is acceptable for a theory-first paper, but some readers may still want a slightly broader empirical picture. For example, it could be useful to include one additional experiment on a more standard identifiable model, not because the theory needs it, but to show continuity with classical settings and to help calibrate the practical magnitude of the bounds. Similarly, some discussion of how tight the upper bounds are in practice, relative to observed KL, would be informative.

Third, some assumptions in the lower-bound applications, especially the alignment and moment conditions used in the corollaries, are technically reasonable but may be hard for non-specialists to parse. The paper would benefit from a short intuition box or paragraph summarizing which assumptions are essentially standard asymptotic regularity conditions, which are algorithm-specific, and which are mainly proof artifacts. This would improve accessibility without changing the technical content.

I have a minor concern about the optimization oracle implicit in Algorithm 3. The algorithm is stated as minimizing KL over weights supported on a sampled subset, but in practice the experiments use nonnegative least squares on discretized log-likelihoods via posterior samples, as noted in the caption of Figure 3 on page 10. This is perfectly reasonable experimentally, but the relationship between the idealized optimization problem in the theorem and the practical surrogate used in experiments could be spelled out a bit more clearly. A brief discussion of whether the theory is intended to justify the idealized algorithm only, or also approximate implementations of it, would help.

Despite these relatively minor issues, my overall assessment is very positive. The paper addresses an important open problem in Bayesian coreset theory, introduces genuinely new techniques, and derives results that are both mathematically nontrivial and practically illuminating. The negative results for importance-weighted methods are especially valuable because they clarify a long-standing empirical puzzle, while the positive results for subsample-optimize methods provide a broad theoretical foundation for a promising class of algorithms. The work is original, rigorous, and likely to have lasting impact on the literature on scalable Bayesian inference.

In summary, I believe this is a strong paper. Its main contribution is not just another bound under slightly altered assumptions, but a substantial expansion of the theoretical scope of Bayesian coreset analysis, together with concrete algorithmic implications. I would be supportive of acceptance.
