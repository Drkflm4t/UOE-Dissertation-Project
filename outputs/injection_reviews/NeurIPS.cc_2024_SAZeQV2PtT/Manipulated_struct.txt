Summary: The paper studies Bayesian coresets for scalable posterior inference and provides general asymptotic lower and upper bounds on the KL divergence between the full posterior and a coreset posterior. The main theoretical contributions are: (1) broadly applicable lower bounds requiring only mild local regularity and posterior concentration assumptions, including extensions to settings where the likelihood depends on parameters through a lower-dimensional map; and (2) an upper bound based on a generalized subexponentiality condition on centered log-likelihood contributions. These results are then used to derive concrete implications for common coreset construction strategies: importance-weighted subsampling requires coreset size growing proportionally with dataset size to keep KL bounded, even after optimal post-hoc scaling, while subsample-optimize methods can achieve bounded error with polylogarithmic coreset size under suitable assumptions. The theory is supported by experiments on two challenging synthetic models with multimodality, unidentifiability, and heavy tails, where the empirical trends in Figures 2 and 3 align with the predicted rates.

Strengths: Addresses an important gap in the Bayesian coreset literature by moving beyond prior analyses limited to exponential families or strongly log-concave/smooth settings.; Provides both lower and upper KL bounds, which is more informative than papers that only establish one-sided guarantees.; The lower-bound framework appears technically general and insightful, especially in reducing posterior approximation difficulty to properties of weighted sums of score vectors near a concentration point.; Derives sharp and practically meaningful consequences for specific algorithms, notably the negative result for importance-weighted coresets and the positive result for subsample-optimize methods.; The assumptions are clearly stated theorem-by-theorem, and full proofs are included in the appendix.; The empirical section is well chosen: the synthetic examples intentionally violate standard regularity assumptions and therefore illustrate the claimed breadth of applicability.; Figures 2 and 3 provide convincing qualitative support for the theoretical predictions, including the predicted scaling behavior with dataset size.; The paper is generally well written and well positioned relative to prior work, with clear motivation and discussion of desiderata for useful coreset methods.

Weaknesses: Most results are asymptotic, so the paper gives limited guidance on finite-sample constants or when the asymptotic regime becomes practically relevant.; Some upper-bound assumptions, especially subexponentiality constants and the alignment condition in Corollary 6.1, may be difficult to verify in new models without substantial case-by-case work.; The empirical validation is limited to two synthetic examples; while appropriate for illustrating theory, it leaves open how predictive the bounds are in more realistic applied Bayesian models.; The optimization step in Algorithm 3 is treated somewhat abstractly; practical computational costs and sensitivity to approximation choices in the optimization objective are not deeply analyzed.; The notation and proofs are dense, which may make the paper difficult to digest for readers outside the immediate theory community.; The lower bounds focus on KL divergence; additional discussion of implications for posterior expectations or downstream decision quality would strengthen practical interpretation.

Soundness Issues: I did not identify an obvious fatal flaw from the provided manuscript, and the theorem statements appear to include explicit assumptions with complete appendix proofs.; That said, several key conclusions rely on posterior concentration and local quadratic approximations around a point of interest; if these conditions fail or are hard to justify in a given model, the practical applicability of the lower bounds may narrow.; The positive result for subsample-optimize methods depends on a nontrivial alignment probability condition (Eq. 6) whose verification is deferred; this makes the result somewhat less constructive than the negative results.; The experiments validate scaling trends rather than the full scope of assumptions, so empirical evidence for the broadest generality claims remains limited.
