This paper studies the approximation quality of Bayesian coresets through general upper and lower bounds on the KL divergence between the full posterior and a coreset posterior. The main contribution is a fairly broad theoretical framework that goes beyond prior analyses restricted to exponential families or strongly log-concave/smooth posteriors. The paper derives lower bounds under mild local asymptotic assumptions and upper bounds under a generalized subexponentiality condition on the log-likelihood contributions. It then uses these results to explain why importance-weighted coresets can perform poorly and why subsample-optimize methods can succeed with polylogarithmic coreset size. The paper also includes empirical illustrations on two deliberately challenging models: a multimodal, unidentifiable Cauchy location model and an unidentifiable heavy-tailed logistic regression model.

Overall, I found the paper strong. The problem is important, the results are nontrivial, and the paper makes a meaningful conceptual advance in the theory of Bayesian coresets. In particular, the lower bounds seem especially valuable: they provide a principled explanation for a long-standing empirical observation that naive importance-weighted subsampling often fails badly for posterior approximation. The upper-bound side is also useful, though somewhat more assumption-heavy and less immediately sharp in practice. The work is mathematically sophisticated, and the proofs in the appendix appear careful and substantial.

The main strength of the paper is its scope relative to prior theory. The introduction clearly motivates that many scalable Bayesian inference guarantees rely on asymptotic normality, unimodality, or strong log-concavity, whereas coreset methods are often empirically useful precisely in settings where those assumptions fail. The paper addresses this gap directly. The lower-bound machinery in Section 3 is elegant: Lemma 3.1 reduces KL lower bounding to a localized overlap quantity over a set B, and Theorems 3.3 and 3.5 then translate posterior approximation difficulty into statements about weighted sums of score vectors and local curvature. This reduction is conceptually clean and likely useful beyond the specific corollaries proved here.

A second major strength is that the theory leads to concrete algorithmic insights rather than remaining purely abstract. Corollaries 4.1 and 4.2 show that importance-weighted coresets require coreset size scaling essentially linearly with N to maintain bounded KL, even after optimal post-hoc scaling. This is a strong and practically relevant negative result. Corollary 4.3 gives a dimension-dependent impossibility result for any coreset construction with fewer than d points under suitable conditions. On the positive side, Corollary 6.1 gives a broad justification for subsample-optimize methods, showing bounded KL with M of polylogarithmic order under alignment and subexponentiality assumptions. This balance of negative and positive results makes the paper much more compelling.

The empirical section, while limited in scope, is well chosen. The two models in Eqs. (2) and (3) are not toy examples in the sense of being overly favorable to the theory; rather, they are intentionally difficult cases with multimodality, unidentifiability, and heavy tails. The plots on page 8 (Figure 2) support the predicted scaling laws for importance-weighted coresets and optimally scaled variants, and the plots on page 10 (Figure 3) show that subsample-optimize coresets can indeed achieve very small KL with logarithmic-size coresets in these settings. These experiments are aligned with the paper’s claims and help validate that the asymptotic theory is not vacuous.

That said, I do have several concerns and suggestions.

My main reservation is about the practical interpretability of the upper-bound assumptions. The lower bounds are relatively satisfying because they rely on assumptions that resemble standard local asymptotic conditions. By contrast, the upper bounds hinge on Definition 5.2, namely (h, A)-subexponentiality of the centered log-likelihood vector under the posterior. While Proposition 5.4 shows this holds locally whenever the posterior moment generating function exists in a neighborhood of zero, the resulting constant beta is existential and model-dependent. Similarly, Corollary 6.1 depends on the alignment probability condition in Eq. (6), which is intuitive but not easy to verify generically. The paper acknowledges this limitation in the conclusion, but I think the presentation could do more to help readers understand when these assumptions are likely to hold with favorable constants and when they may be too weak to be predictive. As written, the upper-bound theory is broad but somewhat difficult to operationalize.

Relatedly, Corollary 6.1 is interesting but feels less definitive than the lower-bound results. The statement depends on three ingredients: subexponentiality with increasing probability, polynomial growth of Var_pi(ell), and the alignment condition. The last condition is especially central, yet it is not directly tied to a concrete sampling scheme beyond the generic probabilities p_n. Since the practical success of Algorithm 3 depends heavily on how the subsample is chosen, it would strengthen the paper if the authors could discuss more explicitly what classes of sampling distributions are expected to satisfy Eq. (6), even heuristically. For example, are uniform probabilities typically enough in redundant-data regimes, or is leverage-style sampling needed? The current theorem is useful as a framework, but its algorithmic implications remain somewhat indirect.

A second concern is that the paper defines coreset quality using the minimum or maximum of forward and reverse KL in different sections. In Section 3, KL(w) is defined as the minimum of forward and reverse KL for lower bounds, while in Section 5 it is defined as the maximum for upper bounds. This is mathematically reasonable for the respective purposes, but it creates some notational friction and could confuse readers. I would strongly recommend using distinct notation, or at least emphasizing the switch more prominently. As it stands, a reader skimming the paper could easily miss that KL(w) changes meaning.

A third issue is that some of the assumptions in the lower-bound applications are stronger than the headline message may suggest. For example, Corollary 4.3 requires assumptions (A7) and (A8), including a linear independence/moment condition on gradients of selected points. This is fine, but the interpretation “any construction algorithm requires M > d” should be read locally and under these conditions, not as a universal information-theoretic lower bound for all models and all notions of coreset quality. The paper mostly states this carefully, but the prose around the contributions could be slightly more qualified to avoid overgeneralization.

I also think the experimental section could be expanded modestly. The current experiments are convincing as sanity checks for the asymptotic claims, but they are both synthetic and low-dimensional. Since one of the paper’s motivations is scalable Bayesian inference for large datasets, it would be helpful to include at least one more realistic example, even if still moderate in dimension, to show that the qualitative conclusions persist outside carefully constructed models. Alternatively, if the goal is purely theoretical validation, the paper could say this more explicitly. Right now the experiments are good but narrow.

On the technical side, the paper is generally well written, but some sections are dense enough that additional intuition would help. In particular, Theorems 3.3 and 3.5 are important but not easy to parse on first reading. A short informal interpretation immediately after each theorem would improve accessibility. For instance, after Theorem 3.3, the paper could explicitly say that bounded KL requires the coreset score average gw/w to match the full-data score average g/N to within order N^{-1/2}, up to curvature and localization terms. Likewise, after Theorem 5.3, it would help to emphasize more plainly that the KL is controlled by posterior variance of the log-likelihood residual when the subexponential condition holds. The appendix proofs are detailed, but the main text could do more to guide the reader.

I have a few more minor comments. First, the desiderata in Section 2 include coreset construction time o(N) or at most O(N) with a small constant, but some of the analyzed methods, especially those requiring nonuniform probabilities or posterior-based optimization surrogates, may not obviously satisfy this in practice. A brief discussion distinguishing statistical approximation guarantees from end-to-end computational savings would be useful. Second, the paper focuses exclusively on KL divergence; some discussion of whether similar techniques might extend to integral probability metrics or task-specific posterior expectation error would broaden the impact. Third, the figures are informative, but the captions could say a bit more about how KL was computed numerically in the heavy-tailed examples.

Despite these concerns, my overall assessment is positive. The paper makes a substantial theoretical contribution to the study of Bayesian coresets. The lower bounds are novel, insightful, and likely to influence how researchers think about coreset construction. The upper bounds are less sharp in a practical sense, but they still provide a meaningful unifying framework that covers settings beyond prior analyses. The empirical results, especially Figures 2 and 3 on pages 8 and 10, support the main qualitative claims. I would be inclined to accept this paper.

To strengthen the final version, I would encourage the authors to do three things. First, clarify the practical meaning of the upper-bound assumptions, especially Eq. (6), perhaps with examples of sampling schemes or model classes where they are expected to hold. Second, clean up the KL notation so that lower- and upper-bound quantities are not denoted identically. Third, add a bit more intuition in the main text around the central theorems and, if space permits, one additional experiment or discussion point connecting the asymptotic theory to a more realistic application setting.

In summary, this is a rigorous and valuable paper. Its main contribution is not a new coreset algorithm but a general theory that explains both the limitations of importance-weighted constructions and the promise of subsample-optimize approaches in broad Bayesian models, including multimodal and heavy-tailed settings. That is an important contribution, and the paper advances the state of understanding in this area.
