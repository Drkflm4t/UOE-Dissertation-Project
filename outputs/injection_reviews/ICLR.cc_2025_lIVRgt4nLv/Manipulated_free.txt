This paper presents Agent S, an open framework for desktop GUI automation with multimodal language models, centered around three main ideas: experience-augmented hierarchical planning, continual memory construction/update, and an Agent-Computer Interface (ACI) that combines screenshots with an OCR-augmented accessibility tree and a bounded action space. The paper evaluates on OSWorld and WindowsAgentArena, reports a substantial improvement over prior baselines, and includes ablations and qualitative/error analyses. Overall, I found the paper timely, well-motivated, and practically relevant. It addresses an important emerging problem—general computer-use agents—and proposes a coherent systems design that appears to improve performance meaningfully. At the same time, some aspects of the empirical methodology and claims would benefit from tighter validation and clearer reporting.

The strongest aspect of the paper is that it tackles a genuinely difficult and important setting: open-ended desktop control across heterogeneous applications, long horizons, and dynamic interfaces. The framing in the introduction is compelling, and the paper clearly identifies three bottlenecks for GUI agents: domain knowledge, long-horizon planning, and grounding/action execution. The proposed decomposition into manager/worker/evaluator modules is intuitive and well aligned with these challenges. The overview figure on page 4 is especially helpful: it clearly shows how the Manager uses web knowledge and narrative memory to produce subtasks, how Workers retrieve episodic experience for execution, and how the Evaluator closes the loop by summarizing trajectories back into memory. This architecture is not merely a loose collection of tricks; it is presented as a reasonably integrated framework.

A second major strength is the ACI design. The paper makes a persuasive case that raw coordinate prediction or unconstrained code generation are both poor interfaces for MLLM-based GUI agents. The proposed dual-input setup—visual screenshot for semantic understanding plus an image-augmented accessibility tree for grounding—is sensible and practically motivated. The OCR augmentation of the accessibility tree, described on pages 6–7, is a nice design choice: rather than only projecting tree information into the image, the system also injects OCR-derived text blocks back into the tree when missing. Likewise, the bounded action space of primitives such as click, type, hotkey, drag-and-drop, and switch-applications seems like a good compromise between expressivity and controllability. This part of the paper is one of its clearest conceptual contributions, and the ablation in Figure 6 supports the claim that ACI materially helps.

The third major strength is the use of experience at multiple levels. The distinction between narrative memory for full-task abstractions and episodic memory for subtask-level grounded experience is well motivated. The manager retrieves high-level guidance, while workers retrieve more concrete execution traces. This is a more structured use of memory than many prior retrieval-augmented agent systems. I also appreciated the self-evaluator idea: summarizing trajectories into textual “rewards” is an interesting way to compress experience into reusable guidance. Even if one may debate whether this should be viewed as analogous to hierarchical RL, the mechanism itself is plausible and practically useful.

Empirically, the headline results are strong. On page 8, Table 1 reports that Agent S with GPT-4o improves OSWorld overall success from 11.21% to 20.58%, with especially large gains in Daily and Professional tasks. The category breakdown in Figure 2 on page 2 and Table 1 suggests the gains are not isolated to one niche domain. The WindowsAgentArena transfer result on page 10 is also encouraging: without explicit adaptation, Agent S improves over the comparable NAVI setup from 13.3% to 18.2%. Given how brittle GUI agents often are to environment changes, this cross-OS generalization is meaningful. The qualitative examples in the appendix are also useful; for instance, the examples on pages 18–20 show the agent handling terminal/git tasks, LibreOffice settings changes, VS Code find-and-replace, Writer formatting, and file recovery from trash. These examples help ground the claims in concrete behaviors.

The ablation section is another positive. Table 2 and Figures 6–7 provide evidence that web knowledge, narrative memory, episodic memory, hierarchical planning, self-supervised exploration, continual memory update, and the self-evaluator all contribute. In particular, the comparison between “Baseline + Retrieval” and “Agent S (ACI-only) + Retrieval” in Figure 6 suggests that retrieval alone is not enough; the interface and planning structure matter. The error analysis is also appreciated. Table 3 on page 10 and the appendix discussion on pages 21–23 show that the authors looked beyond aggregate success rate and tried to categorize failures into planning, grounding, and execution errors. The pie chart on page 21, attributing many execution errors to upstream planning/grounding issues, is informative and points toward future work.

That said, I do have several concerns, mostly around experimental rigor and clarity rather than the overall direction.

My main concern is that the absolute performance remains low, despite the relative gains. A 20.58% success rate on OSWorld is a meaningful improvement over prior baselines, but it still means roughly four out of five tasks fail. The paper is honest about this, and the error analysis helps, but some of the framing occasionally reads stronger than the evidence supports. For example, phrases like “uses computers like a human” are rhetorically appealing but substantially overstate current capability. The system is better described as a promising step toward more capable GUI agents rather than human-level computer use. I would encourage the authors to moderate some of this language.

A second concern is fairness and completeness of the comparisons. The paper compares against reported baselines from OSWorld and WindowsAgentArena under “same input setting” or “similar configuration,” but the exact degree of comparability is not always fully transparent. Agent S uses online web search, self-constructed memories from exploration tasks, OCR augmentation, and a custom ACI. These are legitimate components, but they also introduce additional resources and engineering beyond a plain backbone model. It would help to more explicitly quantify what extra information and compute Agent S receives relative to each baseline. For example, how much of the gain comes from online search versus memory versus interface changes versus simply more prompting structure and more inference calls? The ablations partially address this, but a clearer accounting of test-time budget and external resource usage would strengthen the empirical claims.

Relatedly, the memory construction procedure raises some questions about evaluation protocol. On pages 5–6, the paper states that initial memory is bootstrapped through self-supervised exploration on synthetic tasks, including environment-aware tasks generated from initial environments of OSWorld and WindowsAgentArena. This is interesting, but it also creates a potential concern about benchmark-specific adaptation. Even if no ground-truth labels are used, generating tasks from benchmark environments may still provide a form of exposure to the test distribution. The paper should be much more explicit about whether any exploration tasks are derived from the same applications, websites, or initial states as the evaluation tasks, and whether there is any risk of leakage or overfitting to benchmark idiosyncrasies. This is especially important because the method’s central claim is learning from experience. If the memory is partly built from benchmark-adjacent environments, the distinction between zero-shot generalization and benchmark-specific preparation becomes blurry.

A third concern is the limited scale of the ablation study. The main benchmark has 369 OSWorld tasks, but the ablations are conducted on a 65-task subset, testsub, because of inference cost. This is understandable, but it weakens confidence in some of the finer-grained conclusions. The subset is said to be more balanced than the official small set, which is good, but the sampling procedure is not described in enough detail to assess representativeness. Since many of the paper’s claims hinge on the contribution of individual modules, it would be preferable either to run at least the most important ablations on the full benchmark or to provide confidence intervals / repeated runs on the subset. As presented, some differences may be sensitive to sampling noise.

A fourth issue is that the paper does not report efficiency metrics, despite the architecture being fairly elaborate. The conclusion acknowledges that step count and wall-clock time are unaddressed, and I appreciate that honesty. Still, for an agentic system involving manager planning, web retrieval, memory retrieval, worker execution, reflection, and self-evaluation, cost and latency are central practical considerations. A method that improves success rate by using substantially more model calls or much longer trajectories may still be valuable, but readers need that information to judge the tradeoff. Even simple statistics such as average steps per task, average number of LLM/MLLM calls, average wall-clock time, and token usage would materially improve the paper.

I also found some methodological details underspecified. The retrieval setup uses text-embedding-3-small, but the paper does not say much about memory size, retrieval top-k, prompt lengths, or how often retrieved experiences are stale or contradictory. The self-evaluator is central to the continual learning story, yet its reliability is not independently validated. Since the evaluator determines what gets stored as successful or failed experience “without any human feedback or ground truth information,” errors in self-assessment could compound over time. The ablation “w/o Self-Evaluator” replaces summaries with full trajectories, but that does not isolate evaluator correctness from summarization format. A more direct analysis of evaluator precision/recall on a labeled subset would be useful.

There are also some presentation issues. The paper is generally readable, but several claims could be sharpened. The analogy to hierarchical reinforcement learning on page 5 is suggestive, though somewhat loose; no reward optimization or policy learning is actually performed in the standard RL sense. I would recommend presenting this more cautiously as an inspiration rather than a close formal correspondence. Some notation is also inconsistent or lightly defined in places, and there are minor writing issues throughout. These are not major obstacles, but polishing would help.

One empirical detail that stood out is the uneven per-domain behavior. Table 8 on page 16 shows strong gains in OS, Writer, VLC, Thunderbird, VS Code, and GIMP, but Calc performance appears worse than the baseline (2.13 vs 4.26). Similarly, Table 4 and Table 9 for WindowsAgentArena show zeros in some domains/apps. This does not invalidate the overall contribution, but it suggests the method may be particularly effective in some interface regimes and weak in others. The paper would benefit from a more explicit discussion of where the architecture helps most and why. For example, does hierarchical planning mainly help knowledge-intensive tasks, while ACI helps dense UI grounding tasks? Does web retrieval help browser and office tasks more than system utilities? The current analysis hints at this but does not fully unpack it.

Despite these concerns, I think the paper makes a meaningful contribution. The problem is important, the system design is thoughtful, and the empirical gains are substantial enough to merit attention. In particular, I view the ACI formulation and the multi-level experience retrieval/planning design as the most valuable takeaways. Even if some components are engineering-heavy and the absolute success rates remain modest, this is the kind of systems paper that can move the field forward by showing what combinations of planning, retrieval, memory, and interface abstraction actually matter in realistic desktop environments.

To strengthen the paper further, I would suggest the following concrete revisions. First, clarify the memory bootstrapping protocol and explicitly rule out benchmark leakage as much as possible. Second, report efficiency/cost metrics and, if feasible, normalize comparisons by inference budget. Third, expand ablation reporting, ideally with confidence intervals or repeated runs, and if possible run a subset of key ablations on the full OSWorld test set. Fourth, provide a more direct analysis of the self-evaluator’s quality and the long-term effects of continual memory updates. Fifth, moderate the strongest anthropomorphic claims and better characterize the current limitations suggested by the 20% success rate and the failure taxonomy.

In summary, this is a strong and relevant paper on GUI agents. It proposes a coherent framework with clear architectural ideas, demonstrates sizable gains on a challenging benchmark, and includes useful ablations and failure analysis. The main weaknesses are around evaluation protocol clarity, efficiency reporting, and the still-limited absolute performance. Nevertheless, I believe the paper is a worthwhile contribution and likely to be of broad interest to researchers working on multimodal agents, computer-use assistants, and interactive AI systems. My overall assessment is positive.
