Summary: The paper presents Agent S, an open framework for autonomous GUI-based computer control using multimodal LLMs. The core idea combines three components: (1) experience-augmented hierarchical planning that retrieves external web knowledge plus internal narrative/episodic memories for task decomposition and execution, (2) continual memory construction and update through self-supervised exploration and self-evaluation, and (3) an Agent-Computer Interface (ACI) that uses screenshots plus an OCR-augmented accessibility tree and a bounded set of high-level GUI actions for grounding and control. On OSWorld, Agent S improves over the reported GPT-4o baseline from 11.21% to 20.58% overall success rate, with especially large gains in Daily and Professional tasks; it also shows improvement on WindowsAgentArena from 13.3% to 18.2% without explicit adaptation. The paper includes ablations for retrieval sources, ACI, hierarchical planning, and memory update, as well as an error analysis showing execution and grounding errors remain dominant failure modes.

Strengths: Addresses an important and timely problem: general-purpose desktop GUI automation with multimodal agents.; The framework is modular and clearly structured, with a sensible decomposition into manager/worker/evaluator plus memory and ACI components; the overview figure on page 4 is especially helpful.; Strong empirical improvement on OSWorld over the reported baseline, including a new state-of-the-art overall result in the paper's setting.; Good breadth of evaluation: full OSWorld results, cross-category breakdowns, ablations, qualitative examples, error analysis, and transfer to WindowsAgentArena.; The ACI design is practically motivated: combining screenshot reasoning with accessibility-tree grounding and a bounded action space is a convincing engineering choice.; Experience augmentation is explored at multiple levels (web knowledge, narrative memory, episodic memory), and the ablations suggest each contributes.; The paper is unusually transparent about failure modes; the tables and examples on pages 9-10 and 21-23 make clear that grounding and execution remain major bottlenecks.; Open-source code availability increases reproducibility and practical impact.

Weaknesses: Despite the relative gains, absolute success rates remain low (20.58% on OSWorld), which limits claims about robust human-like computer use.; The contribution is largely a systems integration/engineering advance; some components are incremental combinations of existing ideas such as retrieval augmentation, hierarchical planning, reflection, and constrained action spaces.; Evaluation comparisons are somewhat narrow: the main baseline is the OSWorld agent under matched inputs, but there is limited comparison against a broader set of recent GUI-agent methods under unified settings.; Ablations are conducted on a 65-task subset rather than the full benchmark, which weakens confidence in some component-level conclusions.; The memory construction process relies on synthetic/random exploration tasks and self-evaluation, but the quality, diversity, and possible bias/noise of stored memories are not deeply characterized.; The self-evaluator is central to continual learning, yet its accuracy is not independently validated; erroneous summaries could reinforce mistakes.; Some methodological details that matter for reproducibility and fairness are underspecified or lightly discussed, such as retrieval corpus specifics, prompt sensitivity, compute/runtime costs, and variance across runs.; Generalization evidence to WindowsAgentArena is promising but still limited; gains are uneven across domains and some categories remain at 0%.; The paper does not evaluate efficiency metrics such as number of steps, latency, or token/computation cost, which are especially important for practical GUI agents.

Soundness Issues: The empirical claims are generally supported by the reported benchmark results and ablations, but several conclusions should be interpreted cautiously because ablations use a relatively small sampled subset.; The paper frames the memory update process as learning from experience, but this is retrieval-based adaptation rather than learning in a stronger parametric sense; some claims could be phrased more precisely.; Because the self-evaluator provides success/failure summaries without external supervision, there is a risk of noisy or incorrect memory entries; the paper does not quantify how often this occurs or how robust the system is to such noise.; No statistical significance estimates, confidence intervals, or multi-seed variance are reported, making it hard to assess robustness of the observed gains.; Cross-benchmark comparison on WindowsAgentArena is only partially controlled, and the paper itself notes limitations in comparing against the strongest closed-source system; thus generalization claims should remain modest.; The use of online web search may introduce variability over time and raises reproducibility concerns unless retrieval snapshots or fixed caches are provided.
