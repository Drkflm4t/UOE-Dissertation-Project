Summary: This paper proposes CoT-Influx, a plug-and-play method to improve LLM mathematical reasoning by fitting more useful few-shot chain-of-thought examples into a fixed context window. The method uses a coarse-to-fine pruner: first selecting helpful CoT examples at the shot level, then pruning redundant tokens within retained examples. Because pruning decisions are discrete and external to the target LLM, the authors train the pruner with REINFORCE using a reward that combines LLM loss, answer correctness, and token-budget constraints. They also construct a GPT-4-assisted math reasoning dataset (MRD3) with varied difficulty and reasoning depth to train the pruner. Experiments on LLaMA2-7B/13B/70B across GSM8K and four additional math benchmarks show consistent gains over standard few-shot CoT, retrieval baselines, and prompt compression methods, with the strongest headline result being LLaMA2-70B + CoT-Influx outperforming GPT-3.5 on GSM8K in their setup.

Strengths: Clear and practically motivated idea: exploit redundancy at both example and token levels to increase effective few-shot context without modifying the target LLM.; Method design is intuitive and modular; the coarse-to-fine decomposition into shot pruning and token pruning is sensible and easy to understand.; Strong empirical evaluation across multiple LLaMA2 sizes and five math datasets, with consistent improvements over several prompting, retrieval, and compression baselines.; Useful ablations support key components, including the value of MRD3, the two-stage pruner, and the reward design.; The paper highlights an important empirical observation: more few-shot CoT examples can help, but only if example selection and token preservation are reasoning-aware.; Compatibility with self-consistency and self-verification increases practical relevance.; Inference overhead appears modest relative to alternatives, and the method does not require fine-tuning the target LLM.

Weaknesses: The method depends heavily on GPT-4-generated and GPT-4-evolved data for MRD3, which raises concerns about data quality, bias, and reproducibility for groups without access to strong proprietary models.; The RL training setup is somewhat heuristic, with several stabilizing tricks (difficulty filtering, repeated sampling, EMA, appending manual 8-shot CoTs, rule-based reconstruction in the appendix) that make the overall system more complex than the main framing suggests.; Evaluation is concentrated on math word problems; it is unclear how well the approach generalizes to other reasoning domains or non-reasoning tasks.; Comparisons are limited mostly to prompting/retrieval/compression baselines; there is less direct comparison to stronger long-context or learned in-context selection methods under matched conditions.; Some reported gains, while consistent, are modest in absolute terms on the main benchmark, so the practical significance may depend on deployment setting.; The paper occasionally overstates claims such as 'no additional inference costs' even though there is still auxiliary BERT/pruner computation and some latency/memory overhead.; Writing and presentation have minor issues, including typos, duplicated citations, and occasional imprecision in notation or wording.

Soundness Issues: The reward mixes token-level LLM loss and exact-match correctness, but the justification for the specific functional form is limited; there is little analysis of why this reward is preferable to simpler alternatives beyond ablations.; Training and evaluation may benefit from handcrafted safeguards not fully emphasized in the main narrative, especially rule-based prompt reconstruction and appending manual CoTs, which complicate attribution of gains solely to learned pruning.; The difficulty-aware filtering trains the pruner primarily on easier questions, which may introduce a train-test mismatch when evaluating on harder benchmarks; the paper argues this helps stability, but the effect on generalization is not deeply analyzed.; Because MRD3 is generated and evolved with GPT-4, there is a risk that improvements partly reflect properties of synthetic prompt style rather than a broadly robust pruning strategy.; The retrieval and compression baselines may not be fully optimized for this setting, so some comparisons could favor the proposed method more than a strongest-possible baseline suite would.; The claim that the method is broadly transferable across black-box LLMs is only partially supported in the main paper; evidence beyond LLaMA2 is limited, with GPT-3.5-Turbo results appearing only in the appendix.
