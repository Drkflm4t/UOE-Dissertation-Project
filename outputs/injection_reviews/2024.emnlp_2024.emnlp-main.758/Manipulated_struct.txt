Summary: The paper proposes CoT-Influx, a plug-and-play method to improve mathematical reasoning of frozen LLMs by fitting more useful few-shot chain-of-thought examples into a fixed context window. The method uses a coarse-to-fine pruner: a shot-level pruner selects helpful CoT exemplars and a token-level pruner removes redundant tokens while preserving reasoning-critical structure. The pruner is trained with REINFORCE using a multi-objective reward combining LLM loss, answer correctness, and token-budget control, and is supported by a GPT-4-generated/evolved math CoT dataset (MRD3) with difficulty annotations. Experiments across LLaMA2-7B/13B/70B and five math benchmarks show consistent gains over standard few-shot CoT, retrieval baselines, and prompt compression baselines; on GSM8K, the reported best result is 59.59 EM with LLaMA2-70B, exceeding several larger prior models in the paper’s comparison table.

Strengths: Clear and practically important problem formulation: improving few-shot CoT under fixed context budgets without modifying or fine-tuning the target LLM.; Technically sensible coarse-to-fine design that combines example selection and token pruning, which is better matched to the stated problem than token-only compression or retrieval-only selection.; Strong empirical coverage across multiple model scales (7B/13B/70B) and five math datasets, with consistent improvements over several relevant baselines.; Useful pilot study motivating the method: the paper shows that more CoT examples can help, random selection can hurt, and naive compression can remove reasoning-critical tokens.; Good ablations isolating the contributions of MRD3, shot pruning, token pruning, and reward components.; Practical systems angle: the method is black-box with respect to the target LLM, compatible with self-consistency and self-verification, and reported to add little inference overhead.; Interesting qualitative insight that different-capability LLMs prefer exemplars of different difficulty levels, which helps justify model-specific pruning.; The appendix adds meaningful detail on prompts, hyperparameters, training dynamics, and case studies, improving reproducibility.

Weaknesses: The method relies substantially on GPT-4 for generating, evolving, and annotating the MRD3 dataset, which raises questions about cost, accessibility, and possible benchmark contamination or teacher-model dependence.; The evaluation is concentrated on arithmetic/math word-problem benchmarks; it is unclear how well the approach generalizes to non-math reasoning, symbolic tasks, or broader instruction-following settings.; Some comparisons are not perfectly controlled: CoT-Influx uses a custom learned pruner plus a custom dataset, while several baselines use off-the-shelf retrieval/compression methods not adapted to math reasoning.; The training procedure includes heuristic components such as difficulty filtering, repeated sampling, EMA smoothing, appending manual 8-shot CoTs during training, and rule-based prompt reconstruction at inference; these make it harder to attribute gains cleanly to the RL pruner alone.; The reported gains on GSM8K over the strongest prompting baselines, while meaningful, are moderate in absolute terms, so the practical significance depends on the cost/complexity of preparing MRD3 and training the pruner.; The paper does not provide statistical significance tests or variance across multiple random seeds for the main benchmark tables, which is relevant given the stochastic RL training and sampling-based prompting pipeline.

Methodological Flaws: There is a notable train/test validity concern around the use of GPT-4 to generate and evolve CoT exemplars from datasets that overlap with standard math benchmarks. The paper does not fully rule out leakage-like effects or benchmark-specific tailoring, especially since GSM8K training data is part of MRD3 and GSM8K is also a primary evaluation benchmark.; The reward design mixes token-level budget control with answer correctness and LLM loss, but the formulation in Eq. (5) is somewhat ad hoc and under-justified. In particular, the multiplicative token term [t/T]^w can reward longer prompts when w<0 and penalize them when w>0; the paper does not provide a principled derivation or clear rationale for why this specific form is optimal.; The optimization description is mathematically imprecise in places. The policy is described as producing a 'continuous categorical distribution' via sigmoid, then sampling discrete actions 'with a softmax function'; this is inconsistent and leaves ambiguity about whether actions are Bernoulli, categorical, or independently sampled binary decisions.; The empirical pipeline uses rule-based prompt reconstruction to restore tokens such as 'Q:', 'A:', 'Let’s think step by step', and 'The answer is' after pruning. This means the final evaluated prompts are not purely the direct output of the learned token pruner, which weakens the claim that the model itself learned to preserve all crucial structure.; The comparison to context-window extension in the appendix is limited and potentially unfair: only one extension method and one model scale are tested, and the setup may conflate effects of long-context adaptation quality with the core claim about pruning.; The paper reports one-epoch training in Algorithm 1, while the appendix hyperparameter table lists 3 epochs, indicating a reproducibility inconsistency that should be clarified.
