This paper proposes GraphMETRO, a graph out-of-distribution generalization method aimed at handling complex, heterogeneous, and instance-dependent distribution shifts. The central idea is to model unknown target shifts as mixtures of predefined stochastic graph transformations, and then learn a mixture-of-experts architecture in which a gating network predicts which shift components are active while expert networks produce “referentially invariant” representations aligned to a shared reference model. The method is evaluated on both real-world datasets from the GOOD benchmark and synthetic shift settings, with additional analyses on invariance and shift discovery.

Overall, I found the paper interesting, ambitious, and quite compelling. The problem setting is important: graph OOD work often assumes relatively simple or homogeneous shifts, whereas real graph data can exhibit multiple simultaneous and instance-specific changes. The paper’s main contribution is to explicitly embrace this complexity rather than forcing all shifts into a single invariant predictor. The proposed decomposition into shift components, together with a gating mechanism and aligned experts, is a thoughtful and original design. Empirically, the method appears strong, especially on the real-world GOOD datasets, where the gains on WebKB are particularly striking. In my view, this is a meaningful contribution to graph OOD generalization and a paper that should be taken seriously.

The strongest aspect of the paper is the framing. The authors identify a real limitation in prior graph OOD methods: many either rely on fixed augmentation families, assume a small number of environments, or seek a single invariant representation that may be too rigid under heterogeneous shifts. The motivating examples in the introduction and Figure 1 are effective, and the high-level decomposition shown in Figure 2 on page 2 makes the intuition clear. The idea that different target instances may be affected by different combinations of shift factors is both realistic and underexplored in graph learning. This gives the paper a strong conceptual foundation.

Methodologically, the paper is also quite elegant. The use of a gating model to infer instance-specific shift mixtures and expert models to mitigate individual shift components is natural, but the key addition is the alignment mechanism through the reference model. Without such alignment, aggregating expert outputs would indeed be unstable or semantically incoherent. The notion of “referential invariant representation” is a useful way to formalize this. The training objective in Equation 3 is simple enough to implement yet captures the intended behavior: the gating model is supervised using synthetic mixtures of transformations, while the experts and classifier are trained to both predict labels and align transformed representations to the reference representation. This is a nice design, and the ablations later support that the alignment term is not cosmetic but essential.

The empirical results on real-world datasets are impressive. Table 1 on page 7 shows consistent improvements over a broad set of baselines, including ERM, domain generalization methods, and graph-specific OOD methods. The WebKB result is especially notable: 41.11 ± 7.47 versus the strongest baseline at 24.61 ± 4.86. Even allowing for the relatively high variance, this is a large margin. The gains on Twitch and the graph classification datasets are smaller but still favorable. I also appreciate that the method is evaluated on both node- and graph-level tasks, which supports the claim of generality better than many graph OOD papers that focus on only one setting.

The synthetic experiments are also useful, not because they are the main evidence, but because they help unpack what the method is doing. Figure 3 on page 8 and the numerical tables in Appendix E show that GraphMETRO is often stronger than both ERM and ERM with augmentation under single and composed shifts. This is important because the method is not merely doing standard augmentation; it is learning to decompose and mitigate shifts. The invariance matrix in Figure 4(a) on page 9 is a particularly nice diagnostic: it gives evidence that experts specialize in the intended way. Likewise, the shift-mixture visualizations in Figure 4(b) are a valuable interpretability angle and make the paper more insightful than a purely predictive benchmark paper.

I also want to credit the paper for being fairly complete experimentally. The appendices include architecture details, hyperparameters, transform libraries, and ablations on shared-vs-separate experts, alignment removal, and number of transform functions. The ablation in Table 5 on page 21 is especially convincing: removing alignment causes a substantial drop, which validates a central design choice. The discussion in Appendix F is also refreshingly honest about the dependence on transform design and gating quality.

That said, there are several issues that should be addressed or at least clarified.

My main concern is the gap between the motivating claim—handling natural, unknown, complex real-world shifts—and the actual supervision used to train the gating model and experts. The method depends critically on a predefined library of stochastic transform functions, and the gating model is trained to recognize mixtures of these synthetic transforms. This means the success of the approach hinges on whether the chosen transforms span the relevant shift directions in the target domain. The paper acknowledges this limitation in Appendix F, but it remains central. In some sense, GraphMETRO is only as good as its shift basis. This does not invalidate the contribution, but the paper should be more explicit in the main text that the method is not shift-agnostic; it is shift-basis-dependent. The strong real-world results suggest the chosen transforms are useful, but it would help to better articulate why these particular transforms are expected to capture the GOOD benchmark shifts.

Relatedly, the choice of transform functions seems somewhat heuristic. The paper uses common graph augmentations such as random subgraph sampling, edge dropping, node dropping, feature noise, and edge addition. These are reasonable, but the connection between each transform and each real-world dataset’s actual shift mechanism is not always tight. For example, for WebKB and Twitch, the interpretation in Figure 4(b) is interesting, but still somewhat speculative. The paper itself notes that quantitatively validating these discovered shifts remains challenging. I think this is acceptable for now, but the interpretability claims should be phrased carefully: the method discovers mixtures over the chosen transform basis, not necessarily the true causal shift factors.

A second concern is computational cost and scalability. The paper states on page 7 that the forward process requires O(K) encoder passes and training complexity scales as O(K^2 |Ds|) because of extrapolation. This is nontrivial, especially if one wants a richer transform library or larger graphs. The appendix includes a shared-encoder variant, but it performs worse. Since MoE is often motivated by efficiency, it is worth emphasizing that this is not a sparse conditional-computation MoE in the usual sense; it is closer to an ensemble of experts whose outputs are all aggregated. I would have liked to see wall-clock training time, GPU memory, or parameter count comparisons against baselines. This is especially important because some of the gains may partly come from increased model capacity due to multiple encoders. The synthetic experiments hint at this possibility when in-distribution performance sometimes improves. A stronger capacity-controlled comparison would make the empirical claims even more convincing.

A third issue is the theoretical appendix. I do not think the paper needs theory to be valuable, but the current theoretical section is not fully convincing. In particular, Theorem 1 appears to rely on idealized gating behavior and effectively assumes away contributions from non-selected experts. Equation (10) is a strong simplification that does not obviously follow from the soft weighting in Equation 2. Theorem 2 then builds on this approximation. The generalization bound in Theorem 3 is also quite informal and seems to make several leaps, including assumptions about the target distribution being representable by the training transform family and about the training objective covering a “wider range” of transformations. I would therefore not treat the theory as a rigorous guarantee. My suggestion would be either to substantially tighten the assumptions and proofs or to present this section more explicitly as intuition rather than formal theory. This is a fixable presentation issue, but it is worth noting.

There are also some experimental questions. First, the comparison set is broad, but not always perfectly matched across tasks, and some baselines are unavailable on some datasets. That is understandable, but it makes the strongest claims somewhat dependent on the benchmark setup. Second, the number of runs for GraphMETRO is five, while some baseline numbers are inherited from GOOD; it would be useful to know whether all methods were rerun under identical conditions for the newly added Twitter dataset and whether variance across seeds is comparable. Third, the p-values in Table 1 are appreciated, but the paper does not fully specify the statistical test used. This is a minor reproducibility detail that should be easy to add.

I also think the paper could do a better job disentangling the contributions of decomposition, gating, and extra capacity. For example, a useful baseline would be a multi-expert model without gating but with uniform averaging, or a model with the same number of parameters trained on all augmentations jointly. The ablation “without L1” partially addresses the gating supervision, but not the broader question of whether the gains come from conditional specialization versus simply having multiple encoders. Similarly, a top-1 expert selection variant versus soft aggregation could be informative.

Despite these concerns, I view them as opportunities to strengthen an already strong paper rather than fundamental flaws. The core idea is novel and well motivated, the method is technically coherent, and the empirical results are genuinely impressive on challenging real-world graph OOD benchmarks. The paper also offers useful interpretability and diagnostic analyses beyond raw accuracy tables. In a space where many methods either assume too much structure or rely on narrow augmentation schemes, this work pushes toward a more realistic and flexible treatment of graph distribution shifts.

To improve the paper further, I would recommend the following. First, move some of the limitation discussion from Appendix F into the main paper, especially the dependence on transform design and gating quality. Second, either strengthen or soften the theoretical claims. Third, add explicit compute and parameter comparisons, and if possible include one or two capacity-matched ablations to isolate the benefit of the MoE decomposition from model size. Fourth, clarify the statistical testing protocol and, if space permits, include a brief discussion of how transform families were selected for each dataset. Finally, it would be helpful to discuss whether the method could be extended to learn or adapt the transform basis rather than fixing it manually.

In summary, this is a strong paper with a fresh perspective on graph OOD generalization. Its central idea—modeling unknown graph shifts as mixtures of aligned expert-handled components—is both intuitive and technically interesting, and the empirical evidence is strong enough to make the contribution credible. While some assumptions are important and the theory is less solid than the experiments, I believe the paper advances the field in a meaningful way and would be a positive addition to the conference.

My recommendation is acceptance.
