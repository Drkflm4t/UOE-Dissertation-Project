Summary: This paper introduces X-Topic, a new multilingual multi-label tweet/X topic classification dataset covering four languages (English, Spanish, Japanese, Greek) and 19 social-media-oriented topics. The dataset contains 4,000 annotated tweets (1,000 per language), each labeled by five annotators, with labels assigned when at least two annotators agree. The paper also provides a broad empirical study comparing zero-shot/few-shot LLMs and fine-tuned multilingual encoders under monolingual, cross-lingual, and multilingual settings. Results show that domain-specific multilingual encoders trained on tweet data outperform generic multilingual models, multilingual training helps substantially over monolingual training, and GPT-4o is competitive in few-shot settings but generally does not surpass the best fine-tuned models.

Strengths: Timely and useful contribution: a multilingual social-media topic classification benchmark is genuinely valuable, especially given the scarcity of labeled multilingual X/Twitter datasets.; Clear practical focus: the 19-topic taxonomy is social-media-oriented rather than borrowed from news classification, making the benchmark more realistic for platform content analysis.; Good language diversity: the inclusion of English, Spanish, Japanese, and Greek provides both high-resource and less commonly studied settings, with typological diversity.; Strong experimental breadth: the paper evaluates zero-shot, few-shot, monolingual, cross-lingual, and multilingual settings, which gives the benchmark substantial analytical value.; Useful comparison across model families: the study contrasts generic multilingual encoders, tweet-specialized encoders, and proprietary LLMs, yielding actionable findings for practitioners.; The empirical takeaway that multilingual training and in-domain pretraining help substantially is well supported by the reported results.; Annotation setup is reasonably careful: five annotators per tweet, multilingual native-speaker oversight, and reporting of multiple agreement metrics are positives.; The paper includes descriptive dataset analysis, topic overlap analysis, per-language distributions, and error analysis, which improves transparency.; Resources appear to be released publicly, including dataset and best models, increasing reproducibility and impact.

Weaknesses: The dataset is relatively small for a benchmark intended to support multilingual supervised learning: only 1,000 tweets per language, with substantial label imbalance.; The sampling strategy may bias the dataset toward popular or high-visibility content because tweets are weighted by retweet count and author follower count; this may reduce representativeness of ordinary platform content.; Several preprocessing choices may remove important real-world phenomena from social media text, especially removing URLs and tweets with many mentions/emojis, potentially making the benchmark cleaner but less realistic.; The label assignment rule of accepting a topic when at least two of five annotators agree may introduce noisy positives, especially given the modest agreement scores.; Inter-annotator agreement is fairly low (Alpha around 0.21-0.26), which is understandable for multi-label social media annotation but still limits confidence in label quality.; The comparison between cross-lingual TweetTopic training and target-language training is confounded by training set size (11k vs 800) and temporal/domain differences, making some conclusions about cross-lingual superiority less clean.; The zero/few-shot evaluation of proprietary models is somewhat under-specified for reproducibility, especially regarding decoding parameters, prompt formatting details per language, and API version stability.; The paper does not deeply analyze fairness or cultural/topic-definition mismatch across languages, despite using a single taxonomy across distinct linguistic and cultural contexts.; Some claims are stronger than warranted; for example, cross-lingual models outperforming monolingual ones is likely driven in large part by much larger English training data rather than purely cross-lingual transfer.

Soundness Issues: The methodology is generally sound, but some conclusions are partially confounded. In particular, the claim that cross-lingual models outperform monolingual ones is not a clean comparison because TweetTopic provides far more training data than the target-language subsets.; The popularity-weighted sampling assumption ('higher quality content is usually more popular') is debatable and may systematically skew the benchmark toward certain user groups, styles, or topics.; Using a 2-of-5 agreement threshold for positive labels may increase recall of plausible labels but also risks inflating ambiguous or weakly supported labels; the paper acknowledges this only briefly.; The benchmark is realistic in topic distribution, but the aggressive filtering and URL removal may reduce ecological validity for real X/Twitter classification settings.; The evaluation metric choice (macro/micro F1) is appropriate, but the paper could better justify thresholding/calibration details for multi-label prediction, which can materially affect F1.; Statistical significance is mentioned in Table 3, but the testing procedure is not clearly described in the main text, limiting assessment of robustness.
