Summary: This paper introduces X-Topic, a new multilingual multi-label topic classification benchmark for X/Twitter posts covering four languages (English, Spanish, Japanese, Greek), 19 social-media-relevant topics, and 4,000 annotated tweets total. The dataset is constructed from a large raw crawl with preprocessing, privacy masking, and 5-annotator labeling per tweet. The paper also provides an extensive empirical study across zero-shot, few-shot, cross-lingual, monolingual, and multilingual settings using both fine-tuned multilingual encoders and prompted LLMs. Results show that domain-specific multilingual encoders trained on tweet data outperform generic multilingual models, multilingual training substantially improves over monolingual training, and strong prompted LLMs such as GPT-4o are competitive but generally do not surpass the best fine-tuned supervised models. The work is valuable as both a dataset/resource paper and an empirical benchmark for multilingual social media topic classification.

Strengths: Timely and useful contribution: multilingual labeled social media topic datasets are scarce, especially beyond English, and the paper fills a clear resource gap with four linguistically diverse languages.; The topic taxonomy is practically meaningful for social media analysis, with 19 categories that go beyond narrow event-specific or sentiment-only labeling.; The paper is strong as a benchmark study: it evaluates a broad range of realistic settings (zero-shot, few-shot, cross-lingual, monolingual, multilingual), which makes the resource more useful to future researchers.; Empirical comparisons are comprehensive, including both generic and social-media-specialized multilingual encoders, as well as prompted LLMs of different scales.; The analysis yields several interesting findings: multilingual supervision helps substantially; tweet-domain pretraining matters; and few-shot LLMs, while strong, are not uniformly better than fine-tuned smaller encoders.; The paper reports multiple agreement metrics and provides descriptive dataset analysis, topic overlap analysis, per-language distributions, and error analysis, which improves transparency.; The authors make the dataset and trained models publicly available, increasing reproducibility and practical impact.; The inclusion of Greek alongside higher-resource languages is a welcome addition and broadens the benchmark's relevance beyond the most commonly studied languages.; The paper includes ethical considerations around privacy masking and annotator compensation, which is important for social media datasets.

Weaknesses: The dataset is relatively small for supervised learning (1,000 tweets per language), which limits the strength of conclusions about scaling behavior and may make results sensitive to split variance.; The annotation agreement is fairly low (Krippendorff's alpha around 0.21-0.26), indicating substantial ambiguity/noise in the labels.; The label aggregation rule of assigning a topic when at least two of five annotators agree is permissive and may increase false-positive labels relative to majority-based aggregation.; The sampling strategy is weighted by retweet count and follower count, which may bias the dataset toward popular users/content and away from a representative distribution of ordinary posts.; The comparison between cross-lingual training on TweetTopic and monolingual target-language training is confounded by training set size differences (11,267 vs. 800 examples), which the paper acknowledges but does not fully control for.; The zero/few-shot LLM evaluation may be sensitive to prompt design, decoding choices, and API/model-version instability; the paper uses a simple prompt and does not deeply explore prompt robustness.; Some claims about 'realistic distribution' are only partially supported because several preprocessing filters remove URLs, high-emoji/high-mention tweets, duplicates, and incomplete posts, which may alter the natural platform distribution.

Methodological Flaws: There is a notable scientific-logic issue in interpreting cross-lingual superiority over monolingual models: the paper attributes this to cross-lingual capability, but the training data sizes are dramatically different, so the comparison does not isolate language transfer from simple data volume effects.; The label aggregation protocol is insufficiently justified. Using a threshold of 2/5 annotators for assigning a label in a multi-label setting can admit labels with minority support and may inflate label noise; the paper argues this makes the task more realistic, but does not provide validation that this aggregation improves benchmark quality.; The weighted sampling assumption that more popular tweets are 'higher quality' is not scientifically established and may introduce systematic topical and demographic bias into the benchmark.; The annotation quality discussion somewhat overstates adequacy: while alpha > 0 is better than random, values around 0.21-0.26 are low, and the paper does not analyze disagreement by topic/language or provide adjudication/error auditing to assess whether low agreement reflects ambiguity, annotator inconsistency, or taxonomy mismatch.; The empirical evaluation relies on 5-fold CV over only 1,000 examples per language, but the paper does not report variance/standard deviation in the main table, making it hard to assess robustness of some differences beyond the marked significance annotations.; The LLM comparisons are not fully controlled for reproducibility because proprietary models (ChatGPT/GPT-4o) can change over time and the paper does not provide enough detail on decoding parameters or repeated runs to estimate variance.; The paper does not compare against simpler multilingual baselines such as machine-translation-based transfer or label-calibrated thresholding strategies, which would strengthen claims about the relative merits of the proposed benchmark settings.
