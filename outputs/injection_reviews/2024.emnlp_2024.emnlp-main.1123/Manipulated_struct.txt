Summary: This paper introduces X-Topic, a new multilingual multi-label topic classification dataset for X/Twitter posts in four languages: English, Spanish, Japanese, and Greek. The dataset contains 4,000 annotated tweets total (1,000 per language), labeled with 19 social-media-oriented topics, with five annotators per tweet and labels retained when at least two annotators agreed. The paper also provides descriptive analysis of topic distributions and annotation agreement, and benchmarks a range of multilingual encoders and prompted LLMs under zero-shot, few-shot, monolingual, cross-lingual, and multilingual settings. Results show that domain-adapted multilingual encoders outperform generic ones, multilingual training helps substantially over monolingual training, and fine-tuned encoders generally outperform few-shot LLMs on this task.

Strengths: Addresses a real resource gap by releasing a multilingual, multi-label social media topic classification benchmark spanning four typologically diverse languages.; Uses a practically relevant 19-topic taxonomy tailored to social media rather than repurposing news-domain labels.; Provides a fairly comprehensive experimental study across multiple settings: zero-shot, few-shot, monolingual, cross-lingual, and multilingual.; Compares both generic multilingual encoders and social-media-adapted models, yielding useful empirical insights about domain adaptation.; Includes descriptive dataset analysis, annotation agreement statistics, topic overlap analysis, and error analysis rather than only reporting headline scores.; The cross-lingual and multilingual findings are interesting and potentially useful for low-resource deployment scenarios.; The paper discusses ethics, annotator compensation, privacy masking, and plans to release annotations, which improves transparency and usability.

Weaknesses: The dataset is relatively small for a 19-label multi-label benchmark: only 1,000 tweets per language, which limits statistical power and may partly explain unstable per-topic performance.; Annotation agreement is fairly low (Krippendorff's alpha around 0.21-0.26), raising concerns about label reliability and topic boundary clarity.; The label assignment rule of keeping labels with agreement from at least two of five annotators may introduce noisy positives, especially for ambiguous multi-label cases.; The sampling strategy is weighted by retweet count and follower count, which may bias the dataset toward popular or high-visibility content rather than representative language use.; The comparison between cross-lingual training on TweetTopic and target-language training is confounded by training set size differences, which the paper acknowledges but does not fully control for.; The evaluation relies mainly on 5-fold cross-validation over a small dataset; there is no held-out external test set or temporal generalization experiment despite the social-media setting.; Some methodological details could be clearer, such as exact hyperparameter search ranges, prompt specifics for proprietary LLMs, and statistical testing procedures.; The paper's claims about 'quality' of tweets based on popularity proxies are plausible but not well validated and may encode social or demographic biases.

Soundness Issues: The methodology is generally reasonable, but the annotation protocol creates potential label noise: accepting labels with only 2/5 agreement is a permissive threshold for a 19-way multi-label task.; Cross-lingual superiority over monolingual models is not fully attributable to transfer because TweetTopic training uses far more data than target-language training; a size-matched control would strengthen the claim.; The preprocessing and sampling pipeline may alter the natural distribution of X posts in ways that affect downstream conclusions, especially removal of URLs and popularity-weighted sampling.; Low agreement scores suggest some topics may be insufficiently separable; stronger validation of the taxonomy or adjudication analysis would improve confidence in the benchmark.; Use of proprietary models like GPT-4o is acceptable for benchmarking, but reproducibility is limited unless prompts, decoding settings, and API versions are fully specified.
