This paper introduces X-Topic, a multilingual multi-label tweet topic classification benchmark for X/Twitter covering English, Spanish, Japanese, and Greek, with 4,000 annotated tweets total and 19 topic labels. The paper also provides a fairly extensive empirical study across zero-shot, few-shot, monolingual, cross-lingual, and multilingual settings, comparing general multilingual encoders, Twitter-adapted encoders, and prompted LLMs. Overall, I find the paper timely and potentially useful. The lack of multilingual, topic-oriented, social-media benchmarks is real, and the paper’s emphasis on realistic topic distributions rather than keyword-filtered collection is a meaningful design choice. The experimental section is broad and yields several practically relevant findings, especially the strong advantage of multilingual supervised training and the benefit of Twitter-domain pretraining. I also appreciate that the dataset and best models are released.

The main strengths are threefold. First, the resource itself fills a genuine gap. Most prior topic classification datasets are either English-only, outside social media, or focused on narrow domains or weak labels. A manually annotated multilingual benchmark with a 19-topic taxonomy tailored to social media is valuable. Second, the paper does more than just release data: it systematically studies multiple transfer settings that practitioners actually care about, including cross-lingual transfer from English-only supervision and few-shot prompting with frontier LLMs. Third, the analysis is generally thoughtful. The descriptive statistics, agreement measures, topic overlap discussion, and per-topic error analysis all help readers understand where the task is difficult. The figures and tables are informative; for example, the topic-count chart on page 4 clearly shows substantial class imbalance and cross-language variation, while Table 3 on page 7 gives a comprehensive comparison across settings and models.

That said, I also have several concerns, some of which are important for a top-tier venue. The biggest issue is that the dataset is quite small relative to the claims and breadth of the evaluation. Each language has only 1,000 annotated tweets, and the monolingual training split per fold is only 720 examples. This is enough for a benchmark, but it limits the strength of conclusions about multilingual topic classification more broadly. In particular, some of the paper’s claims about cross-lingual superiority are confounded by training set size: the English TweetTopic training set has 11,267 examples, whereas the target-language monolingual setting has only 800 labeled examples available outside the test fold. The paper does acknowledge this, but the framing sometimes overstates the cross-lingual result as evidence of transfer rather than mostly evidence of more supervision. A fairer comparison would subsample TweetTopic to match the target-language training size, or provide learning curves showing performance as a function of training examples. Without that, the conclusion that cross-lingual models “consistently outperform” monolingual ones is not very informative scientifically.

Relatedly, the multilingual-vs-monolingual comparison is also partly a data-scaling comparison rather than a pure multilinguality comparison. The multilingual setting simply has more labeled data. The observed gains are useful in practice, but the paper should be more careful in attributing them to multilingual learning rather than to increased sample size. Again, matched-size controls or ablations would strengthen the argument substantially. For example, one could compare multilingual training on N total examples against monolingual training on the same N examples via oversampling or English augmentation, or vary the number of added languages incrementally in a controlled way.

A second major concern is the annotation protocol and label aggregation rule. The paper uses five annotators per tweet and assigns a label if at least two annotators selected it. This is intentionally permissive and may indeed create a more challenging dataset, but it also risks introducing noisy positives, especially for semantically adjacent labels such as Arts & Culture, Other Hobbies, Learning & Educational, and News & Social Concern. The agreement statistics in Table 2 on page 4 are modest, with Krippendorff’s alpha between 0.21 and 0.26, lower than the original TweetTopic. The paper argues these are comparable to other multi-label annotation tasks, which is fair, but more evidence is needed that the resulting labels are reliable enough for benchmarking. In particular, because the aggregation threshold is only two of five annotators, it would be helpful to report label-frequency by agreement level, or benchmark performance under stricter gold definitions such as 3/5 agreement. Releasing all annotations is good, but the paper itself should analyze the sensitivity of results to this design choice. As it stands, some of the difficulty of the task may reflect annotation ambiguity rather than genuine modeling difficulty.

A third concern is the preprocessing and sampling pipeline, which may bias the dataset in ways that are under-discussed. The authors remove tweets with URLs, tweets with more than four emojis or mentions, and then sample 1,000 tweets per language weighted by retweet count and follower count. This likely improves readability and annotation quality, but it also shifts the benchmark away from the natural distribution of X content. In particular, removing URLs may disproportionately exclude news, politics, marketing, and information-sharing posts; weighting by popularity may overrepresent celebrity, mainstream, or high-engagement discourse; and filtering “abusing words” via heuristics may suppress important social phenomena. The paper claims the collection is “closer to the real distribution” because it avoids keyword filtering, but the subsequent filtering and popularity-weighted sampling complicate that claim. I would like to see a more explicit discussion of how these choices affect topic distribution and external validity. Ideally, the paper would compare pre- and post-filter topic tendencies, or at least characterize how much content is removed by each step beyond the counts in Table 6 on page 16.

The language coverage is useful but still limited. English, Spanish, Japanese, and Greek provide some typological diversity, especially Japanese versus the Indo-European languages, but the benchmark remains small-scale in both number of languages and number of examples. This is not fatal, but it does limit the generality of the conclusions. The paper sometimes suggests broader implications for multilingual topic classification on social media; these should be tempered. It would also help to explain more clearly why Greek was chosen beyond being “less frequently studied,” and whether annotator expertise and topic taxonomy transfer equally well across all four cultural contexts.

On the modeling side, the experimental breadth is commendable, but some methodological details are missing or could be clearer. The paper says hyperparameters were tuned with Ray Tune, but the search space, selection criterion, and whether tuning was done separately per language/setting/model are not described in the main text. Since the dataset is small, tuning choices can materially affect results. Similarly, the exact prompting setup for LLMs is underspecified. The prompt shown in Appendix B.3 is minimal, but it is unclear whether label descriptions were included, whether outputs were constrained, how multi-label predictions were parsed, whether temperature was fixed, and whether prompts were translated or identical across languages. These details matter a lot for zero/few-shot comparisons, especially when concluding that fine-tuned encoders outperform few-shot LLMs. The comparison is interesting, but it should be made more reproducible and more careful.

I also found the statistical significance reporting somewhat opaque. Table 3 indicates underlined scores are significantly different from the second-best score, but the paper does not clearly specify in the main text which test was used, over what units, and whether multiple-comparison correction was applied. Given the small test sets per fold and many model/setting comparisons, significance claims should be documented carefully.

The error analysis is a positive aspect, but it could go further. Table 4 on page 8 identifies topics with high false negative rates, and Tables 9 and 10 on page 17 provide per-topic precision/recall/F1 for xlmt-large and gpt-4o. These show, for example, that Other Hobbies is consistently difficult and that Arts & Culture has low recall for xlmt-large in several languages. This is useful. However, the paper could better connect these failures to dataset design and taxonomy structure. Some labels appear inherently broad or overlapping, especially Other Hobbies, Diaries & Daily Life, Arts & Culture, and Learning & Educational. The overlap heatmap on page 15 already hints at this. A stronger analysis would quantify whether errors concentrate among semantically adjacent labels and perhaps compare against a hierarchical or merged-label evaluation. That would help distinguish model weakness from taxonomy ambiguity.

There are also a few presentation and writing issues. The paper is generally readable, but there are numerous minor grammatical errors and typos throughout: for example “limited explored area,” “surprasses,” “tha arise,” “The this trend can bee seen,” and inconsistent naming such as xlm_t/xlmt and chat-gpt/OpenAI model references. Some terminology is also slightly confusing: the paper uses “zero-shot and unsupervised systems” together, though prompted LLM classification is not really unsupervised in the usual sense. These are fixable, but the manuscript would benefit from careful proofreading.

One aspect I appreciated is the inclusion of ethics and annotator compensation details. The paper states that annotators were paid about £8/hour and that user mentions were masked. Still, there are some unresolved ethical and legal questions around redistribution of tweet text, even under a CC BY-NC 4.0 license, depending on platform terms and jurisdiction. The paper should clarify whether the release contains raw text, IDs only, or some transformed representation, and how this aligns with X/Twitter data-sharing policies. Since the benchmark’s utility depends heavily on accessibility, this is worth stating explicitly.

In terms of empirical findings, the paper’s main takeaways seem plausible and useful. The results in Table 3 show that Twitter-adapted encoders outperform generic XLM-R variants, especially in low-resource settings, which is unsurprising but important to document. The best overall supervised model, xlmt-large, reaches around 57.6 macro-F1 when trained on all available data including TweetTopic, while gpt-4o few-shot is competitive in some cases and strongest in Greek. The chart on page 16 further illustrates that adding both TweetTopic and multilingual X-Topic data tends to help consistently. These are good benchmark observations. My reservation is less about the results themselves and more about the causal interpretation of why they occur.

To strengthen the paper, I would most strongly recommend the following revisions. First, add controlled comparisons that disentangle transfer from training-set size: subsample TweetTopic to match target-language data, and provide learning curves for monolingual, cross-lingual, and multilingual settings. Second, analyze annotation uncertainty more deeply: report agreement-conditioned label quality, perhaps compare 2-of-5 versus 3-of-5 gold labels, and discuss how evaluation changes. Third, provide fuller methodological details for hyperparameter tuning, prompting, decoding, and significance testing. Fourth, discuss dataset bias introduced by filtering and popularity-weighted sampling more explicitly, ideally with some quantitative characterization. Fifth, consider adding a taxonomy-focused analysis of confusion among overlapping labels, or at least a confusion matrix aggregated over semantically related topics.

My overall assessment is positive but somewhat mixed. The dataset is useful, the experiments are broad, and the paper addresses an underexplored problem with practical relevance. At the same time, the scientific claims would be stronger with better-controlled comparisons and deeper analysis of annotation and sampling choices. I would view this as a solid resource paper with meaningful empirical value, but not yet a fully convincing study of multilingual transfer mechanisms. If the venue values benchmark creation and practical multilingual evaluation, this paper has a good case. If the bar is higher for methodological rigor and causal interpretation, the current version falls a bit short.

In summary, I see clear merit in the contribution and expect the dataset to be useful to the community. My main recommendation is to tighten the claims, add controls for data size, and analyze annotation uncertainty more carefully. With those improvements, the paper would be substantially stronger.
