Summary: The paper studies reward overoptimization in RLHF and argues that it arises from distributional shift and uncertainty in learned preference rewards. It proposes a theoretical maximin RLHF objective that selects a policy against an adversarially chosen reward model minimizing a combination of preference-model MLE loss and a reward penalty. The authors then derive an equivalent implementation-friendly objective, Regularized Preference Optimization (RPO), which combines a DPO-style preference optimization term with an explicit imitation/SFT loss. The paper provides finite-sample guarantees under a partial coverage assumption, proves an equivalence between the maximin and minimax formulations under regularity conditions, and presents experiments on Zephyr beta/gemma style 7B models showing improved training behavior and modest but consistent gains over DPO on in-distribution evaluations and benchmarks such as MT-Bench and AlpacaEval 2.0.

Strengths: Addresses an important and timely problem in RLHF: reward overoptimization caused by imperfect preference models and distribution shift.; Provides a clear conceptual bridge between theory and practice: the adversarially regularized theoretical objective leads to a simple practical objective of DPO plus SFT.; The practical algorithm is easy to implement as a plug-in modification to existing DPO pipelines, which increases potential impact.; Theoretical analysis is nontrivial and goes beyond linear models by allowing general function approximation via covering-number style complexity control.; Finite-sample guarantees under partial coverage are clearly stated, and the dependence on coverage aligns with offline RLHF intuition.; The equivalence result between maximin and minimax objectives helps justify why the practical objective corresponds to the theoretical one.; The paper gives useful intuition, including the simple three-action example and the training-dynamics plots showing reduced collapse in log-probability on chosen responses.; Empirical results are reasonably broad for a theory-practice paper: two model families, in-distribution pairwise evaluation, MT-Bench, AlpacaEval 2.0, and additional math/reasoning/coding results in the appendix.

Weaknesses: The theoretical assumptions needed for the minimax equivalence and sample-complexity results are fairly strong and somewhat abstract, especially compactness/convex-like conditions on the reward class and the partial coverage requirement.; The practical experiments are relatively limited in scale: only ~7B-class models and a small number of training setups, so it is unclear how robust the gains are for larger modern LLMs or more diverse datasets.; Empirical improvements over DPO, while generally consistent, are modest on some benchmark metrics and may be sensitive to hyperparameter tuning of the added SFT coefficient.; The main empirical evaluations rely heavily on GPT-4-as-judge, which introduces evaluator bias and variance; human evaluation is absent.; The connection between the exact theoretical baseline policy assumptions and the practical choice of chosen-response SFT regularization is plausible but not fully airtight empirically.; The paper does not compare extensively against other recent methods that also add SFT-like or conservative regularization to preference optimization (e.g., ORPO/Smaug-like variants) beyond discussion in related work.; The claim that DPO's beta mainly changes gradient scale rather than direction is intuitive in the presented derivation, but the broader optimization dynamics in deep networks may be more nuanced than this characterization suggests.

Soundness Issues: The sample-complexity theorem is logically coherent within the stated assumptions, but its practical relevance depends strongly on the partial coverage coefficient, which may be hard to estimate or verify in realistic LLM settings.; The minimax-to-maximin equivalence relies on regularity assumptions on the reward class that are not obviously satisfied by neural reward parameterizations used in practice; thus the formal bridge from theory to implementation is suggestive rather than fully realistic.; The empirical evidence supports that adding SFT regularization can help, but it does not isolate whether the gains come specifically from the adversarial-regularization interpretation versus generic stabilization/auxiliary supervision.; In-distribution evaluation uses only 150 filtered GPT-4-annotated examples, which is a fairly small sample for strong empirical claims.; Generalization claims to new prompt distributions are theoretically conditioned on bounded density ratio and empirically illustrated only via PCA overlap plots, which are heuristic rather than rigorous evidence of coverage.
