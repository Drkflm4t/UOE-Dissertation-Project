Summary: The paper studies reward overoptimization in RLHF and argues that it arises from distributional shift and uncertainty in learned preference rewards. It proposes a theoretical maximin RLHF objective that selects a policy against an adversarially chosen reward model minimizing a combination of preference-model MLE loss and a reward penalty. The authors then derive an equivalent implementation-friendly formulation, Regularized Preference Optimization (RPO), which adds an imitation/SFT loss to the standard DPO objective. The paper provides finite-sample guarantees under a partial coverage assumption, proves an equivalence between maximin and minimax formulations under regularity assumptions, extends the analysis to shifted prompt distributions, and reports empirical gains over DPO on Zephyr beta/gemma settings, MT-Bench, AlpacaEval 2.0, and additional GSM8K/ARC/MBPP evaluations.

Strengths: Addresses an important and timely problem in RLHF: reward overoptimization caused by imperfect preference models and distribution shift.; Provides a clear conceptual contribution by interpreting SFT loss as an implicit adversarial regularizer, connecting theory and common practice in a novel way.; Introduces a principled theoretical objective with finite-sample suboptimality guarantees under partial coverage and general function approximation rather than only linear models.; Derives an implementation-friendly practical objective (RPO) that is simple, modular, and easy to add on top of DPO.; The equivalence argument between the theoretical maximin objective and the practical minimax/reparameterized objective is interesting and helps justify the practical algorithm.; Empirical results are reasonably broad: two model families/datasets, in-distribution pairwise evaluation, MT-Bench, AlpacaEval 2.0, and additional math/reasoning/coding benchmarks.; The paper is generally well organized, with intuitive figures (especially page 3 Figure 1) that clarify the failure mode of DPO and the role of the SFT regularizer.; The method appears computationally lightweight and practically relevant, since it reuses quantities already present in DPO training.

Weaknesses: The theoretical guarantees are established for Algorithm 1 / minimax-based formulations under assumptions on the reward class, but the practical neural RPO training used for LLMs is only indirectly connected to these guarantees; optimization and approximation gaps for deep models are not analyzed.; The empirical evaluation relies heavily on GPT-4-based judging for in-distribution and benchmark comparisons, which may introduce evaluator bias and variance.; Comparisons are primarily against DPO and reference/released models; stronger baselines specifically designed to mitigate overoptimization (e.g., ORPO/Smaug-like or uncertainty-aware methods discussed in related work) are not extensively compared in the main results.; The gains over DPO on some benchmark metrics are modest, so the practical significance, while positive, is not always dramatic.; Hyperparameter sensitivity, especially to the regularization weight η and the choice of baseline policy/distribution for the SFT term, is not deeply explored.; The generalization-to-new-prompts discussion uses PCA visual overlap as qualitative support, which is suggestive but not a rigorous validation of the density-ratio assumption.

Methodological Flaws: There is a notation inconsistency between Sections 3 and 5.2 regarding the baseline policy inside ϕ(π,r): Equation (3.2) uses π_base, while Equation (5.2) appears to define ϕ with a0 sampled from π_ref rather than π_base. Since Theorem 5.6 and the equivalence arguments rely on this definition, this should be clarified; as written, it creates ambiguity in the formal statement.; The proof of Lemma B.2 appears to contain an inequality issue. Since for distributions one has Hellinger distance bounded above by total variation (typically H^2 <= TV), the manuscript on pages 24 and 26 uses the reverse direction in places (e.g., D_Hellinger^2 >= D_TV^2 and D_Hellinger^2 <= D_TV). These cannot both be correct, and the step used in Theorem 5.3's proof may therefore need correction.; The empirical claim that decreasing log-probability on chosen responses is evidence of overoptimization is plausible but not fully identified causally; alternative explanations such as optimization dynamics or regularization effects are not ruled out.; The in-distribution evaluation uses only 150 filtered examples after removing undesired GPT-4 annotations, which may introduce selection effects and limits statistical strength.; The theoretical assumptions needed for minimax equivalence (compact topological reward class, convex-like property) are fairly abstract and may not map cleanly to the neural reward/policy classes used in practice, limiting the literal scope of the theorem.
