Summary: The paper studies reward overoptimization in offline RLHF and argues that it arises from distribution shift and uncertainty in learned preference rewards. It proposes a theoretical maximin objective that selects a policy against an adversarial reward model minimizing the sum of Bradley-Terry MLE loss and a reward penalty term. Under partial coverage and general function approximation, the authors derive an O~(1/sqrt(N)) suboptimality guarantee relative to any comparator policy. They then show an equivalence between this maximin objective and a minimax formulation whose closed-form inner solution yields a practical objective, Regularized Preference Optimization (RPO), consisting of DPO plus an imitation/SFT loss. Empirically, on Zephyr beta and gemma style setups, RPO improves over DPO on chosen-response log-probability during training, GPT-4 judged in-distribution pairwise comparisons, MT-Bench, AlpacaEval 2.0, and some math/reasoning/coding benchmarks.

Strengths: Addresses an important and timely problem in RLHF: reward overoptimization under imperfect preference modeling.; Provides a clear conceptual bridge between pessimistic/offline RLHF theory and a practical LLM alignment objective.; The practical algorithm is simple and easy to implement: RPO is just DPO plus an SFT/imitation term, making the contribution potentially impactful for practitioners.; Theoretical analysis is relatively general in allowing nonparametric/general reward classes rather than only linear models.; The paper gives a useful interpretation of SFT loss as an implicit adversarial regularizer, which is an interesting conceptual contribution.; Empirical results are reasonably broad across two model families/datasets and multiple benchmarks, with consistent gains over DPO.; The paper is generally well organized, and the figures/tables effectively communicate the motivation and empirical trends.

Weaknesses: The practical method is only indirectly justified by theory: the theoretical guarantees are for exact optimization over idealized policy/reward classes, while the experiments use large neural policies with approximate optimization and heuristic hyperparameter selection.; The empirical evaluation relies heavily on GPT-4 based judging for in-distribution comparisons and benchmark scoring, which introduces evaluator bias and variance; human evaluation is absent.; The experimental comparisons are narrow: the main baseline is vanilla DPO, while several closely related methods that also combine preference optimization with supervised regularization are discussed but not empirically compared.; The claimed mitigation of overoptimization is supported mainly by higher log-probability on chosen responses and downstream benchmark gains, but there is limited direct evidence that the method specifically reduces reward hacking rather than simply acting as a generic regularizer.; Hyperparameter sensitivity is underexplored. The coefficient η is selected on a test split of the training dataset, and there is little analysis of robustness to η or β.; The generalization argument to new prompt distributions is weakly validated: PCA overlap plots are only suggestive and do not substantiate the density-ratio assumption used in theory.; Some notation and exposition are occasionally inconsistent or imprecise, which makes parts of the theory harder to trust fully.

Methodological Flaws: There is a notable notation inconsistency between Sections 3/5 regarding the baseline policy in the definition of ϕ. Equation (5.2) uses a0~πref in the expectation, whereas the earlier objective and surrounding discussion use a0~πbase. Since the baseline policy is central to both the theory and the practical SFT term, this discrepancy is not merely cosmetic and could affect the formal equivalence statements and proofs.; The gradient explanation in Section 4 appears mathematically incorrect or at least imprecise: the paper defines r̂θ(x,a)=β·log(πθ(a|x))/log(πref(a|x)), whereas the DPO reparameterization should involve a log-ratio β(log πθ(a|x) - log πref(a|x)) = β log(πθ/πref). Writing a ratio of logarithms is dimensionally and mathematically wrong.; The proof of Lemma B.2 appears flawed. For the sigmoid derivative on [-R,R], the lower bound should depend on the full range of z1-z2. Since reward differences lie in [-R,R] only if rewards are in a narrower interval, but with r(x,a) in [0,R], differences lie in [-R,R] and the derivative lower bound over that interval is not obviously the stated κ=1/(1+exp(R))^2 under all preceding substitutions. More importantly, the manuscript sometimes treats reward differences as if bounded by R rather than 2R, which may invalidate constants in the concentration and Lipschitz arguments.; Relatedly, in the Hellinger/TV argument and subsequent use of Lemma B.2, the paper applies the sigmoid bound to quantities of the form r(x,a1)-r(x,a0). Since each reward lies in [0,R], these differences range over [-R,R], but elsewhere the approximation error and covering arguments seem to use sup-norm bounds on rewards directly without carefully propagating the factor of 2 for differences. The constants in Theorem 5.3 may therefore be incorrect.; The empirical protocol selects η by comparing validation loss on the 'test split' of the training dataset (page 29), while the main in-distribution evaluation also uses held-out prompts from the same dataset family. This is not a fatal flaw, but it weakens the cleanliness of the evaluation protocol and risks mild tuning leakage.; The claim that RPO mitigates overoptimization is not isolated causally. Adding SFT loss can improve stability and benchmark performance for many reasons unrelated to adversarial regularization; the experiments do not include ablations that disentangle baseline-policy choice, coefficient scaling, token-normalized vs unnormalized SFT, or comparisons to generic regularizers with matched strength.; The theoretical equivalence and guarantees assume exact inner maximization/minimization and compact/convex-like reward classes, but the practical algorithm optimizes a parameterized policy directly and may not correspond to any realizable reward class satisfying Assumption 5.5. Thus the formal guarantees do not transfer cleanly to the actual experimental setting.
