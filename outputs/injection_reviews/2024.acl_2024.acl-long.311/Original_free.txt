Summary

This paper presents a broad empirical study of parameter-efficient preference alignment for LLMs. The authors examine three axes: alignment dataset (HH-RLHF vs. BeaverTails), alignment method (SFT vs. DPO), and base model type (pre-trained vs. instruction-tuned), using LoRA/QLoRA across LLaMA-1, Vicuna-v1.3, Mistral-7B, and Mistral-7B-Instruct. The study spans 300+ experiments and evaluates on helpfulness and harmlessness benchmarks including MMLU, BBH, AlpacaEval, RealToxicityPrompts, and Red-Instruct/DangerousQA. The main claims are that higher-quality preference data helps, SFT can outperform DPO for pre-trained models while DPO is better for instruction-tuned models, mixing distinct preferences often hurts, and model merging can mitigate some trade-offs.

Overall, I find the paper timely and potentially useful to practitioners. The scope is ambitious, and the paper surfaces several practically relevant observations. However, the current version has important methodological and presentation weaknesses that make some conclusions feel stronger than the evidence supports. In particular, there are concerns around fairness of hyperparameter choices across methods, evaluation validity for alignment claims, interpretation of toxicity metrics, and the extent to which the findings generalize beyond the specific datasets/models used. I would lean borderline positive if the venue values empirical synthesis and practical guidance, but I think the paper would benefit from stronger controls and clearer statistical reporting.

Strengths

1. Relevant and practical problem
The paper addresses a question many researchers actually face: given limited compute and PEFT constraints, how should one choose between SFT and DPO, which preference data to use, and whether to start from a base or instruction-tuned model? This is a valuable contribution area.

2. Broad experimental coverage
The study is unusually broad for an alignment paper in a PEFT setting. The combination of:
- 4 model variants,
- 2 datasets,
- 2 alignment methods,
- 2 PEFT methods,
- multiple seeds,
- multiple evaluation benchmarks,
is a real strength.

3. Useful practitioner-oriented findings
Several findings are actionable:
- higher-quality/more informative data tends to help;
- DPO appears more effective on instruction-tuned models;
- SFT may be safer/more stable on pre-trained models;
- naively mixing preferences can degrade performance;
- merging separately aligned models may help.

Even if some of these are not fully universal, they are useful hypotheses and heuristics.

4. Inclusion of both harmlessness and helpfulness
Many alignment papers focus narrowly on one dimension. Studying trade-offs across helpfulness and harmlessness is valuable.

5. PEFT focus
The emphasis on LoRA/QLoRA makes the work more accessible and practically relevant than studies assuming full fine-tuning.

Weaknesses and concerns

1. Some conclusions are overstated relative to the evidence
The paper often phrases findings as general guidelines, but the evidence is limited to:
- 7B-scale models only,
- two preference datasets,
- two preference dimensions,
- one DPO formulation,
- one fixed training budget,
- a narrow set of hyperparameter choices selected per method.

This is enough for an informative empirical study, but not enough to support broad claims like “pre-trained models generally align better with SFT, whereas instruction-tuned models align better with DPO” without stronger caveats. The results may be highly contingent on the exact datasets, prompt formatting, training steps, and hyperparameters.

2. Fairness of SFT vs. DPO comparison is questionable
The training setup differs substantially between SFT and DPO:
- different LoRA ranks,
- different alphas,
- different learning rates,
- different dropout,
- different target modules,
- different schedulers.

These choices are listed in Table 6 / Appendix B-C, but they make direct method comparisons difficult to interpret. If DPO underperforms SFT on some settings, is that due to the method or due to the chosen PEFT configuration? Likewise, if DPO excels on instruction-tuned models, how robust is that to matched capacity and optimization budget?

This is probably the biggest methodological issue in the paper. A stronger comparison would either:
- match trainable parameter budget and optimization budget across methods, or
- include sensitivity analyses showing the conclusions hold under multiple comparable settings.

3. Fixed training budget may bias conclusions
All models are trained for 700 steps. Given the large differences in dataset size (e.g., BeaverTails helpful split is much larger than HH-RLHF; Appendix C), model initialization, and method dynamics, a fixed step budget may not correspond to comparable convergence. Some of the observed “overfitting” or “degradation” may simply reflect under/over-training under a non-normalized budget.

Relatedly, the quantity analysis on page 5/Figure 3 is interesting, but it still uses a fixed training regime that may confound sample count with effective number of epochs or passes over data.

4. Evaluation of harmlessness is not fully convincing
The harmlessness evaluation relies on:
- RealToxicityPrompts scored by a toxicity reward model,
- Red-Instruct DangerousQA with 100-ASR.

There are several issues:
- RealToxicityPrompts is known to be imperfect and sensitive to decoding and classifier artifacts.
- The paper uses a separate toxicity model from Hugging Face; more detail is needed on calibration and whether higher score means less toxic or more toxic. The sign conventions are confusing in the tables, especially with negative values.
- “100-ASR” on Red-Instruct needs clearer definition. Is lower better? The figures imply lower is better, but this should be explicit everywhere.
- Harmlessness is broader than toxicity/refusal on dangerous prompts. The paper sometimes interprets these metrics as “faithfulness to harmlessness,” which is too strong.

5. Helpfulness evaluation is also somewhat narrow
MMLU and BBH are knowledge/reasoning benchmarks, not direct preference-following or user-helpfulness measures. AlpacaEval is closer, but still imperfect and GPT-4-based. The paper sometimes equates gains on MMLU/BBH with “helpfulness,” which is not fully justified. These are useful downstream proxies, but not direct helpfulness evaluations.

6. Dataset construction for BeaverTails helpfulness may introduce confounds
Appendix C explains that the BeaverTails helpful split is created by taking the “better” response as chosen and the second response as rejected. This may not isolate helpfulness cleanly, especially if the “better” label already entangles safety, style, and other preferences. Similarly, the harmless split is constructed by selecting safe vs. unsafe responses. These derived splits may not be directly comparable to HH-RLHF’s explicit helpful/harmless splits.

This matters because several central claims compare HH-RLHF and BeaverTails as “lower-quality vs. higher-quality” datasets. But the datasets differ in many ways beyond quality/informativeness:
- single-turn vs. multi-turn,
- size,
- label construction,
- prompt distribution,
- response style,
- safety/helpfulness entanglement.

So the causal interpretation “quality causes improvement” is plausible but not cleanly established.

7. Statistical reporting is insufficient
The paper states that 5 seeds are used for all experiments, but the main figures and tables do not show:
- standard deviations,
- confidence intervals,
- significance tests.

Given that many reported differences are small (e.g., MMLU/BBH changes of a few points or less), uncertainty reporting is essential. Without it, it is hard to know which trends are robust and which are noise.

8. Some interpretations are speculative
For example:
- the claim that pre-trained models are “not strong inherent reward models” and therefore DPO degrades;
- the claim that harmlessness is “more objective” than helpfulness;
- the claim that BeaverTails’ safe responses being informative explains certain gains.

These are reasonable hypotheses, but they are not directly tested. The paper should label them more clearly as conjectures.

9. Model merging section is interesting but underdeveloped
Section 6.2 and Table 3 suggest DARE/TIES-style merging can outperform individual aligned models on average. This is promising, but the section feels preliminary:
- only a small set of merge methods is tested;
- merge coefficients/details are not fully discussed;
- no variance is reported;
- the mechanism is not analyzed;
- some merged models improve one metric while hurting another.

Given the paper’s title and framing, this section feels somewhat bolted on rather than fully integrated.

10. Presentation issues
There are several clarity issues:
- Some figures are hard to read and interpret.
- Figure 1 radar plots are visually dense and not ideal for precise comparison.
- The paper occasionally uses “faithful” in a vague way.
- There are grammatical issues and awkward phrasing throughout.
- Table numbering is confusing in places due to embedded figure/table artifacts in the PDF extraction.
- The paper sometimes says “Table 1” when referring to a figure or another table.

Detailed comments by section

Abstract / Introduction
The motivation is strong and well framed. The paper clearly identifies a gap: lack of systematic PEFT-focused analysis of alignment trade-offs. The contribution claims are mostly fair, though the abstract slightly overstates the generality of the findings.

Experimental setup
This section is mostly clear, but several details should be added:
- decoding parameters for evaluation,
- exact DPO beta,
- whether reference models are frozen and how they are chosen,
- prompt formatting consistency across models and tasks,
- whether all evaluations use greedy decoding or sampling,
- whether AlpacaEval uses the same generation settings across all models.

These details matter for alignment evaluation.

Effects of alignment dataset
The comparison between HH-RLHF and BeaverTails is useful. The charts on page 4 and page 5 are suggestive, especially the trend that BeaverTails often helps more. However, because the datasets differ along many axes, the paper should be more careful in attributing gains specifically to “quality” or “informativeness.”

The quantity analysis in Figure 3 on page 5 is one of the more interesting parts of the paper. The observation that instruction-tuned models may regress under additional SFT while benefiting from DPO is plausible and useful. Still, I would like to see results normalized by epochs/tokens and accompanied by uncertainty bars.

The data mixture result in Table 2 on page 6 is also interesting. But the mixture strategy appears naive. If the conclusion is “mixing preferences hurts,” that should be softened to “naive mixing of these two datasets/preferences under this setup hurts.” More sophisticated balancing or conditional prompting might behave differently.

Effects of alignment method
Figure 4 on page 6 supports the claim that DPO is often better for instruction-tuned models and more effective for harmlessness. This is one of the paper’s strongest empirical messages. Still, because the SFT and DPO setups are not tightly matched, the conclusion should be framed as an empirical observation under the chosen training recipes, not a clean causal comparison of methods.

Effects of base model
Figure 5 on page 7 is useful and aligns with common intuition: SFT helps pre-trained models more, while DPO is more suitable after instruction tuning. This section is practically valuable.

However, the paper should discuss whether Vicuna and Mistral-Instruct are fair “instruction-tuned counterparts” to LLaMA-1 and Mistral-7B. Vicuna is not simply LLaMA-1 plus generic instruction tuning; it has its own training data and recipe. Thus, differences may reflect more than just “instruction-tuned vs. pre-trained.”

Model merging
Table 3 on page 8 is intriguing. DARE appears promising, especially compared to Slerp. But this section needs more rigor to support the claim that merged models “effectively mitigate performance trade-offs.” It would help to show Pareto-style comparisons or averaged normalized scores across helpfulness and harmlessness.

QLoRA vs. LoRA
Figure 6 on page 9 suggests similar trends across QLoRA and LoRA, which is reassuring. This is a nice practical confirmation.

Suggestions for improvement

1. Improve fairness of method comparisons
At minimum, add ablations where SFT and DPO are compared under more closely matched PEFT capacity and optimization settings. Ideally:
- same target modules where possible,
- matched trainable parameter count,
- matched rank budget,
- sensitivity to learning rate and training steps.

2. Add uncertainty estimates everywhere
Since the paper uses 5 seeds, report mean ± std or confidence intervals in all main tables/figures. Mark which differences are statistically meaningful.

3. Clarify evaluation metrics and sign conventions
Especially for RealToxicity and Red-Instruct:
- define whether higher/lower is better in every table,
- explain negative values,
- justify the toxicity reward model choice.

4. Soften causal language around dataset quality
Reframe claims from “higher-quality data causes better alignment” to “the more informative BeaverTails setup tends to outperform HH-RLHF in our experiments,” unless stronger controls are added.

5. Better isolate dataset factors
If possible, add controlled subsampling:
- match HH-RLHF and BeaverTails by sample count,
- compare single-turn subsets only,
- compare prompt-overlap-controlled subsets,
- or analyze response length/informativeness explicitly.

6. Strengthen the quantity analysis
Normalize by effective epochs or tokens seen, not just number of samples and fixed steps. This would make the “sweet spot” claim more convincing.

7. Expand discussion of limitations
The limitations section is appreciated, but it should also mention:
- evaluation proxy limitations,
- confounding due to non-matched hyperparameters,
- derived BeaverTails split construction,
- possible benchmark contamination or instruction-tuning overlap.

8. Improve figure readability
Several plots, especially radar charts, should be replaced or supplemented with clearer bar plots or line plots with error bars.

9. Better define “faithfulness to preference”
This term is used repeatedly but somewhat loosely. It would help to define it operationally.

10. Consider a more cautious title or framing
“A Deep Dive” is acceptable, but the paper should emphasize that the findings are empirical trends under a specific PEFT regime, not universal laws of alignment.

Questions for the authors

1. What exact DPO beta was used, and was it tuned?
2. Were generation settings identical across all models and evaluations?
3. How were the 700 training steps chosen, and do the main conclusions hold at convergence or under early stopping?
4. Can you provide variance/error bars for all main results?
5. How sensitive are the SFT-vs-DPO conclusions to matched LoRA rank/target modules?
6. For BeaverTails helpfulness, how often does the “better” label reflect safety rather than helpfulness per se?
7. Did you try balancing or curriculum strategies for the mixed-preference experiments?
8. In model merging, what coefficients and selection criteria were used?

Recommendation

Borderline accept / weak accept if the venue values broad empirical studies and practical guidance; otherwise weak reject due to methodological confounds.

Confidence

Moderate.

Final assessment

I like the paper’s goal and think the empirical synthesis is useful. The experiments are extensive, and several findings are practically important. The strongest contribution is not a new algorithm but a set of grounded observations about when SFT or DPO is likely to work better in PEFT settings. However, the paper currently overgeneralizes from a somewhat confounded experimental setup, and the lack of uncertainty reporting weakens confidence in several claims. With stronger controls, clearer statistical reporting, and more careful framing, this could become a very solid empirical reference paper.
