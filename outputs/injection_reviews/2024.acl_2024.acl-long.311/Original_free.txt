This paper presents a broad empirical study of parameter-efficient preference alignment for LLMs, focusing on three axes: alignment dataset, alignment method, and base model type. Concretely, it compares HH-RLHF versus BeaverTails, SFT versus DPO, and pre-trained versus instruction-tuned 7B models (LLaMA-1/Vicuna-v1.3 and Mistral-7b/Mistral-7b-Instruct), under LoRA and QLoRA. The paper reports more than 300 experiments and evaluates on helpfulness-oriented benchmarks (MMLU, BBH, AlpacaEval) and harmlessness-oriented benchmarks (RealToxicity and Red-Instruct/DangerousQA). The main claims are that higher-quality preference data helps, SFT can outperform DPO for pre-trained models while DPO is better for instruction-tuned models, mixing distinct preferences often hurts, and model merging can mitigate some trade-offs.

Overall, I found the paper timely and potentially useful. The topic is important: many researchers can now run PEFT alignment experiments, but practical guidance on when to use SFT versus DPO, what data to use, and how base model choice affects outcomes is still fragmented. The paper’s main strength is its scope and its attempt to synthesize actionable empirical guidance rather than proposing yet another alignment algorithm. The comparisons across datasets, methods, and model initialization states are valuable, and several observations are interesting, especially the asymmetry between pre-trained and instruction-tuned models and the degradation from preference mixing. The paper is also generally well motivated, and Table 1 on page 2 does a good job positioning the work relative to prior claims in the literature.

That said, I also have substantial concerns about the rigor of the experimental design and the strength of some conclusions. In its current form, I view the paper as a useful exploratory empirical study, but not yet fully convincing as a definitive analysis of “trade-offs” in PEFT preference alignment. The main issues are confounding factors in the comparisons, limited statistical treatment despite multiple seeds, unclear fairness of hyperparameter choices across methods, and some over-interpretation of results that may be explained by dataset construction or evaluation mismatch rather than deeper alignment principles.

The strongest aspect of the paper is the breadth of the empirical matrix. The authors vary multiple practically relevant factors and evaluate across both helpfulness and harmlessness. The inclusion of both pre-trained and instruction-tuned models is especially valuable, because much of the DPO literature indeed assumes an already instruction-tuned starting point. The results in Figure 4 on page 6 and Figure 5 on page 7 support a nuanced message: DPO is not uniformly superior, and its effectiveness depends strongly on the starting model. This is a meaningful corrective to simplistic narratives in the literature. I also appreciated the inclusion of model merging experiments in Table 3 on page 8; even though that section is relatively small, it points to a practical mitigation strategy for conflicting preferences.

Another positive is that the paper does not merely compare final checkpoints but also examines trends with sample count and training progression. Figure 2 on page 4 and Figure 3 on page 5 are useful in showing that some effects emerge early and that more data is not always better, especially for instruction-tuned models under SFT. This kind of analysis is more informative than a single end-point comparison.

However, the paper’s central comparisons are not always as controlled as they need to be. The biggest issue is that several conclusions about “dataset quality” may be confounded by differences beyond quality. HH-RLHF and BeaverTails differ not only in informativeness, but also in size, prompt distribution, response style, annotation protocol, and even turn structure. The appendix on page 12 explicitly notes that HH-RLHF samples are multi-turn while BeaverTails samples are single-turn, and that BeaverTails does not natively provide the same helpful/harmless splits but instead requires custom split construction. This makes it difficult to attribute observed gains purely to “higher quality” or “more informative” data. For example, if BeaverTails-aligned models do better on AlpacaEval or toxicity metrics, that could reflect differences in prompt formatting, response verbosity, or task overlap rather than intrinsic data quality. The paper often states causal conclusions too strongly here.

Relatedly, the BeaverTails split construction raises methodological questions. On page 12, the authors explain that for helpfulness they define the “better” response as chosen and the other as rejected, while for harmlessness they select samples where one response is safe and the other unsafe. This means the helpfulness and harmlessness subsets are not directly analogous to HH-RLHF’s curated splits. It would be important to know how much overlap exists between these constructed subsets, whether prompt distributions differ substantially, and whether the helpfulness split inadvertently includes safety preferences because BeaverTails is fundamentally a safety-oriented dataset. This is especially relevant because the paper repeatedly notes that “harmless data performs better than helpful data,” which may simply reflect the dataset’s construction bias rather than a general phenomenon.

A second major concern is fairness of the SFT versus DPO comparison. The training setup on page 3 and the hyperparameter table in the appendix on page 13 show that SFT and DPO use different ranks, alphas, learning rates, dropout, target modules, gradient clipping, and schedulers. Some of this is standard practice, but it means the comparison is not isolating the alignment objective alone. In particular, SFT uses rank 64 while DPO uses rank 16, and the target modules differ substantially. If DPO underperforms on some pre-trained models, it is hard to know whether this is due to the objective, the lower adapter capacity, or the chosen optimization setup. The authors mention a grid search, but the paper does not provide enough detail on the search budget, validation criterion, or whether the same effort was spent across all models and datasets. Since several headline conclusions hinge on SFT versus DPO, this needs to be much more carefully justified.

The fixed training budget of 700 steps for all settings is another source of concern. Because datasets differ greatly in size, and because SFT and DPO have different optimization dynamics, a uniform step count may not correspond to a fair comparison. The appendix states that BeaverTails helpful has roughly 297k samples while harmless has about 46k, and HH-RLHF splits are around 42–44k. Training all settings for 700 steps with batch size 16 means very different fractions of an epoch are seen across conditions. Some of the observed “degradation” or “overfitting” may simply reflect undertraining or mismatched exposure. This is particularly important for the sample-efficiency claims in Section 4.2.

The statistical presentation is also weaker than expected for a paper emphasizing empirical guidance. The authors state that they run 5 seeds for all experiments, but the main figures and tables do not appear to report standard deviations, confidence intervals, or significance tests. Given that many reported differences are modest, especially on MMLU and BBH, it is difficult to assess robustness. For example, several differences in Table 2 on page 6 are on the order of 0.003–0.01 absolute. Without uncertainty estimates, it is hard to know which conclusions are stable. This is especially problematic because the paper often generalizes from small margins to broad recommendations.

I also have concerns about the evaluation methodology for harmlessness. RealToxicity is scored using a separate toxicity reward model from Hugging Face, as described on page 3. This introduces evaluator dependence and potential mismatch with the alignment data. The paper should discuss the calibration and limitations of this toxicity model more carefully. In addition, Red-Instruct is reported using “100-ASR,” but the exact prompting and decoding setup are not sufficiently detailed in the main paper. Since harmlessness results are central to the claim that DPO is more faithful to explicit preferences, more methodological transparency is needed. It would also help to include refusal rates, helpful-safe trade-off metrics, or human evaluation of safety/helpfulness balance, since toxicity alone can reward evasive or degenerate behavior.

Some interpretations also seem speculative relative to the evidence. For instance, the paper hypothesizes on page 4 and page 7 that pre-trained models fail under DPO because they are not strong enough “reward models.” This is plausible, but the paper does not directly test it. Alternative explanations include poor prompt formatting for base models, insufficient instruction-following priors, unstable optimization under the chosen DPO hyperparameters, or mismatch between chosen/rejected response distributions and the base model’s likelihood geometry. The paper should frame such explanations more cautiously unless supported by additional diagnostics.

The model merging section is interesting but underdeveloped. Table 3 on page 8 suggests DARE can outperform individual aligned models on average, while Slerp often hurts. But the setup is not fully specified: interpolation coefficients, sparsity settings, whether merges are done in adapter space or merged full weights, and how hyperparameters were selected are not clearly described. Also, the claim that merged models “effectively mitigate performance trade-offs” feels too broad given the limited number of merge methods and tasks. This section is promising, but currently more like a pilot experiment than a fully substantiated contribution.

There are also some presentation issues. The paper is generally readable, but several figures are hard to interpret precisely. The radar plots in Figure 1 on page 4 are visually appealing but not ideal for quantitative comparison, especially when metrics have different scales and semantics. Some claims in the text would be easier to verify with simple bar plots and error bars. Figure 3 on page 5 reports performance “in % relative to the performance when using 1600 samples,” which obscures absolute performance and can exaggerate small changes. More direct plots with absolute values and uncertainty would improve clarity. There are also occasional wording issues and overstatements, such as “DPO performs better than SFT” in the key takeaways, which is not uniformly true across the paper’s own results.

In terms of novelty, I would characterize the contribution as empirical synthesis rather than methodological innovation. That is perfectly acceptable if the empirical evidence is especially rigorous and broadly useful. At present, the paper is valuable but not yet at the level of a definitive benchmark study because of the confounds above. Still, I do think the paper has merit: the experimental scope is substantial, the questions are relevant, and several findings are practically important even if they should be stated more cautiously.

The paper would be significantly strengthened by the following revisions. First, add uncertainty estimates everywhere: mean and standard deviation over seeds in all tables and error bars in all plots. Second, make the SFT versus DPO comparison more controlled, ideally by matching adapter capacity and target modules where possible, or at least adding ablations showing that the main conclusions are not artifacts of these choices. Third, better isolate “dataset quality” from other dataset differences. A stronger version would subsample BeaverTails to match HH-RLHF size, compare single-turn subsets only, or perform controlled filtering within one dataset to vary informativeness while holding other factors fixed. Fourth, provide more detail and analysis for the BeaverTails split construction, including overlap statistics, prompt distribution summaries, and examples. Fifth, report epoch-equivalent training exposure or tune training duration per setting rather than fixing 700 steps globally. Sixth, add more diagnostics for DPO failure on pre-trained models, such as reward margin evolution, chosen/rejected log-prob gaps, or instruction-following sanity checks before and after alignment. Seventh, temper causal language and present some conclusions as hypotheses rather than established mechanisms.

A few additional experiments would also be especially helpful. One would be a two-stage pipeline comparison: pre-trained model + SFT instruction tuning + DPO versus direct DPO, since the paper’s recommendations strongly suggest this. Another would be evaluating whether the observed trends hold when using the same prompt template across all models, since formatting can matter a lot for base versus chat models. It would also be useful to include one stronger modern base/chat pair beyond LLaMA-1/Vicuna, since LLaMA-1 is relatively old and may make some conclusions less representative of current practice. Finally, a small human evaluation on safety/helpfulness trade-offs would greatly strengthen the harmlessness claims.

My overall assessment is moderately positive. I believe the paper addresses an important practical question and contains useful empirical observations, especially the distinction between pre-trained and instruction-tuned starting points for DPO versus SFT. However, the current evidence does not fully support some of the stronger general claims, mainly because of confounded dataset comparisons, non-uniform training setups, and lack of statistical reporting. I would encourage acceptance only if the venue values broad empirical studies and the authors can address the rigor concerns; otherwise, I would lean borderline.

In summary, this is a worthwhile and relevant paper with solid experimental ambition and several practically meaningful findings, but it needs stronger controls, clearer statistical evidence, and more cautious interpretation to fully justify its conclusions.
