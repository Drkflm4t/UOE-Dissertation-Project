This paper presents a broad empirical study of parameter-efficient preference alignment for LLMs, centered on three axes: alignment dataset, alignment method, and base model type. Concretely, it compares HH-RLHF vs. BeaverTails, SFT vs. DPO, and pre-trained vs. instruction-tuned 7B models (LLaMA-1/Vicuna-v1.3 and Mistral-7b/Mistral-7b-Instruct), under both LoRA and QLoRA. The paper’s main value is not a new algorithm, but a large-scale, carefully structured experimental analysis intended to extract practical guidance for researchers performing PEFT-based alignment. In that role, I found the paper useful, timely, and generally well executed.

The strongest aspect of the paper is its scope combined with a clear organizing framework. The authors do not merely compare two methods on one benchmark; instead, they systematically vary dataset quality/content, sample quantity, alignment objective, model initialization, and PEFT implementation. The resulting study spans over 300 experiments and evaluates across both helpfulness-oriented tasks (MMLU, BBH, AlpacaEval) and harmlessness-oriented tasks (RealToxicity, Red-Instruct/DangerousQA). This breadth is valuable because many alignment claims in the literature are made under narrow settings, and this paper explicitly aims to test whether those claims survive under PEFT and across different model states. Table 1 on page 2 does a good job positioning the work relative to prior claims, and the paper largely follows through on that promise.

A second major strength is that the paper surfaces several nuanced findings that are genuinely useful for practitioners. The most important one is that the relative merits of SFT and DPO depend strongly on the base model state: SFT tends to be more effective for pre-trained models, while DPO tends to be more effective for instruction-tuned models. This is a meaningful refinement of the common narrative that “DPO is better than SFT.” The evidence for this appears consistently in the main comparisons, especially in Figure 4 on page 6 and Figure 5 on page 7. The paper also makes a compelling case that DPO is more faithful to explicit preferences such as harmlessness, while SFT can be less reliable in actually enforcing the intended preference. That distinction between downstream task performance and preference faithfulness is important and often blurred in alignment papers.

The dataset analysis is also a strong contribution. The comparison between HH-RLHF and BeaverTails is not framed as a superficial benchmark swap, but as a study of informativeness and response quality. The discussion around harmlessness data is particularly insightful: the paper argues that BeaverTails’ more informative safe responses avoid some of the degeneration or overfitting seen with HH-RLHF, especially under DPO. Figure 1 on page 4 supports this claim visually, and Figure 2 on page 4 adds a useful training-dynamics perspective by showing that higher-quality data can lead to faster gains or slower regression. This kind of analysis is practically relevant because many researchers treat preference datasets as interchangeable when they are not.

I also appreciated the inclusion of sample quantity effects. This is a relatively underexplored question, and the paper’s findings are interesting: instruction-tuned models can regress under continued SFT, while DPO often remains beneficial or at least more stable for them; pre-trained models may benefit from more alignment data differently; and there may be a “sweet spot” in the number of samples. Figure 3 on page 5 is helpful here. Even if some of these conclusions remain somewhat qualitative, the paper is asking the right question and provides evidence that the answer is not monotonic.

Another notable strength is the attempt to study preference mixtures and model merging. The result that naively mixing harmlessness and helpfulness data can degrade performance relative to training on individual preferences is useful, especially because one might have expected simple mixture training to be a straightforward way to combine objectives. Table 2 on page 6 makes this trade-off concrete. Similarly, the model-merging experiments in Table 3 on page 8 are an interesting addition: although somewhat exploratory, they suggest that merging separately aligned models can mitigate some trade-offs better than joint alignment on mixed preferences. I found this part less mature than the rest of the paper, but still worthwhile and suggestive of a promising direction.

The paper is generally clearly written and easy to follow. The experimental setup is well described, the key questions are explicit, and the section-wise “key takeaways” help distill the large number of results. The appendix is also useful, especially the extended results tables and the details on how BeaverTails splits were constructed. The inclusion of both LoRA and QLoRA is another practical plus, and Figure 6 on page 9 supports the claim that the qualitative trends are similar across the two PEFT methods.

That said, I do have several concerns, mostly about interpretation and experimental control rather than the overall value of the study.

My main concern is that some of the causal claims are stronger than what the evidence fully supports. For example, the paper often attributes differences to concepts like “informativeness,” “quality,” or the model’s ability to act as a reward model under DPO. These are plausible hypotheses, but they are not directly isolated. HH-RLHF and BeaverTails differ along multiple axes simultaneously: response style, prompt distribution, split construction, single-turn vs. multi-turn structure, dataset size, and possibly domain composition. Likewise, pre-trained vs. instruction-tuned models differ in many ways beyond “reward-model suitability.” So while the empirical trends are valuable, some of the mechanistic explanations should be presented more cautiously as hypotheses rather than conclusions.

Relatedly, the BeaverTails split construction introduces a potential confound. As described in Appendix C on page 12, the authors derive “helpful” and “harmless” subsets from BeaverTails labels rather than using native splits. This is reasonable, but it means the helpfulness and harmlessness subsets may differ in ways that are artifacts of the filtering procedure. Since several central conclusions depend on comparing alignment to “same” vs. “distinct” preferences, it would strengthen the paper to analyze the resulting prompt overlap, response length, topic distribution, and label balance of these constructed splits. Without that, it is hard to know how much of the observed effect is due to preference distinction versus data construction.

A second concern is fairness of hyperparameter comparison between SFT and DPO. The paper uses different LoRA ranks, alphas, learning rates, dropout, target modules, and schedulers for SFT and DPO (Table 6 on page 13), chosen via validation. This is understandable, but it complicates direct attribution of performance differences to the alignment objective alone. In particular, DPO uses a broader set of target modules than SFT, which may itself affect capacity and adaptation behavior. Since one of the headline claims is “SFT vs. DPO,” I would have liked either a matched-capacity ablation or at least a discussion of how much the conclusions might depend on these implementation choices.

A third issue is that the evaluation protocol mixes heterogeneous metrics whose interpretation is not always straightforward. For harmlessness, the paper uses a toxicity reward model score on RealToxicity and 100-ASR on Red-Instruct. These are reasonable proxies, but they are not equivalent notions of safety, and some reported values are difficult to interpret without more calibration. For example, negative RealToxicity “harmlessness scores” appear in several tables, but the paper does not fully explain the scale or what magnitude differences mean in practice. Since some conclusions hinge on “faithfulness to harmlessness,” a bit more metric exposition would help.

I also think the statistical reporting could be stronger. The paper states that all experiments are run with 5 seeds, which is commendable, but the main figures and tables do not appear to report variance, confidence intervals, or significance tests. Given that some differences are modest, especially on MMLU/BBH, it is difficult to judge which trends are robust and which may be within noise. This is especially important because the paper’s contribution is empirical synthesis rather than a new method; therefore, confidence in the stability of the observed trends is central.

There are also some limitations in external validity that the authors acknowledge but that remain important. The study is restricted to 7B models, two preference families (helpfulness and harmlessness), and two alignment datasets. These are sensible choices, but some of the paper’s broader recommendations should be framed accordingly. For example, the conclusion that instruction-tuned models align better with DPO may hold in this regime, but it is not yet clear whether it extends to stronger modern models, multilingual settings, or preferences such as honesty, style, or domain-specific constraints. Similarly, the finding that mixed-preference training degrades performance may depend on the particular pair of preferences chosen here, which are partly antagonistic in some data realizations.

I have a smaller concern about the model-merging section. The results in Table 3 on page 8 are interesting, but the section feels somewhat underdeveloped relative to the rest of the paper. It is not always clear what merge coefficients were used, how sensitive the results are to those coefficients, or whether the gains are consistent across seeds. Since the paper uses these results to motivate a takeaway that merged models can mitigate trade-offs, I would prefer either a slightly more thorough analysis or a more tentative framing.

Despite these concerns, I think the paper makes a meaningful contribution. Its value lies in careful empirical mapping of a design space that many researchers now operate in, and it succeeds in extracting several actionable lessons. In particular, the distinction between pre-trained and instruction-tuned starting points, the role of dataset quality for PEFT alignment, and the caution against naive preference mixing are all useful takeaways. Even where I am not fully convinced by the causal interpretation, the empirical observations themselves are informative.

To strengthen the paper further, I would suggest the following concrete improvements. First, add uncertainty estimates throughout the main results, ideally with error bars in Figures 1, 3, 4, 5, and 6 and standard deviations in the main tables. Second, clarify the harmlessness metric scales and provide a short discussion of how to interpret absolute differences. Third, include a more explicit characterization of the constructed BeaverTails helpful/harmless splits, including size, overlap, prompt/topic statistics, and response-length distributions. Fourth, temper some mechanistic claims about why DPO fails on pre-trained models or why BeaverTails is “higher quality,” unless supported by additional ablations. Fifth, if feasible, add one controlled comparison where SFT and DPO use more closely matched PEFT capacity/target modules to isolate the effect of the objective.

Overall, I found this to be a solid and useful empirical paper. It addresses an important practical question, covers a substantial experimental space, and produces several nontrivial findings that refine common assumptions in the alignment literature. While some interpretations should be made more cautious and the statistical reporting should be improved, the work is rigorous enough and sufficiently informative to be of interest to the community. I would be positive on acceptance.
