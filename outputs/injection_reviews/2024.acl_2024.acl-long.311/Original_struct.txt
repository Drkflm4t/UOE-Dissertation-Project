Summary: The paper presents an empirical study of parameter-efficient preference alignment for LLMs, focusing on three axes: alignment dataset, alignment method, and base model type. Using over 300 experiments with 7B-scale models (LLaMA-1, Vicuna-v1.3, Mistral-7b, Mistral-7b-Instruct), two datasets (HH-RLHF and BeaverTails), two alignment methods (SFT and DPO), and two PEFT approaches (LoRA/QLoRA), the authors evaluate downstream effects on helpfulness and harmlessness benchmarks. The main claims are that higher-quality preference data generally helps, SFT tends to work better for pre-trained models while DPO works better for instruction-tuned models, mixing distinct preferences often hurts, and model merging can mitigate some trade-offs.

Strengths: The paper studies an important and practically relevant problem: how PEFT choices affect preference alignment outcomes under realistic single-GPU constraints.; The experimental scope is broad, spanning datasets, methods, model families, pre-trained vs instruction-tuned variants, and both LoRA and QLoRA.; The paper includes multiple downstream evaluations covering both helpfulness and harmlessness, rather than relying on a single benchmark.; Several findings are practically useful, especially the distinction between when SFT vs DPO appears preferable and the observation that dataset quality matters substantially.; The appendix provides useful implementation details, including hyperparameter ranges, dataset construction notes, and extended result tables.

Weaknesses: Many conclusions are stronger than what the evidence supports; several claims are framed as general guidelines despite being derived from a narrow set of models, datasets, and preferences.; The experimental design changes multiple factors at once, making causal interpretation difficult. For example, comparisons between HH-RLHF and BeaverTails confound quality, size, prompt format, single-turn vs multi-turn structure, and custom split construction.; The DPO and SFT comparisons are not fully controlled because they use different adapter ranks, alphas, dropout, target modules, learning rates, schedulers, and optimization settings, so observed differences cannot be attributed cleanly to the alignment method alone.; The harmlessness evaluation is weakly validated: RealToxicity is scored by a separate toxicity reward model from Hugging Face, and Red-Instruct uses 100-ASR, but there is little discussion of metric reliability, calibration, or variance.; Some interpretations are speculative and not rigorously tested, such as the claim that pre-trained models are poor inherent reward models or that harmlessness is more objective and therefore easier for DPO.; The paper lacks statistical significance testing despite running 5 seeds; figures and tables mostly report means without uncertainty intervals, making it hard to judge robustness of small differences.; The model-merging section is underdeveloped and difficult to interpret because merge hyperparameters, compatibility assumptions, and variance analyses are not sufficiently detailed.

Methodological Flaws: The central SFT-vs-DPO comparison is confounded by unequal hyperparameterization. As reported in the training setup and Table 6, SFT and DPO use different PEFT ranks, alphas, dropout, target modules, learning rates, max grad norms, weight decay, and schedulers. Therefore, claims such as 'DPO performs better than SFT' or 'SFT is better for pre-trained models' are not method-isolated conclusions.; The dataset-quality analysis is not a clean quality comparison. HH-RLHF and BeaverTails differ not only in response informativeness but also in dataset size, annotation scheme, turn structure, and in BeaverTails the authors create custom helpful/harmless splits (Appendix C). Thus the paper cannot attribute performance differences specifically to 'quality' without stronger controls or matched subsets.; The quantity analysis appears to vary number of samples while keeping training steps fixed at 700. If so, the number of epochs over the data changes substantially across sample sizes, confounding sample quantity with optimization exposure. This undermines conclusions about a 'sweet spot' in sample count.; The paper repeatedly interprets downstream benchmark changes as evidence of alignment faithfulness, but this is only partially justified. For example, MMLU/BBH/AlpacaEval are not direct preference-faithfulness measures, and improvements there do not necessarily indicate better helpfulness alignment.; The harmlessness conclusions rely heavily on a single toxicity classifier/reward model for RealToxicity. This introduces evaluator bias and possible reward hacking concerns, especially when the paper argues one method is more 'faithful' than another.; The claim that DPO fails on pre-trained models because they are not strong reward models is speculative and unsupported by ablations. Alternative explanations include prompt formatting mismatch, optimization instability, or unfair hyperparameter choices.; The paper reports some very large degradations/improvements but does not provide confidence intervals, hypothesis tests, or per-seed dispersion. Given the modest absolute differences on several benchmarks, empirical validity is weakened.; The model-merging results are not fully convincing scientifically because the paper compares merged models to individually aligned models without enough detail on merge coefficients, selection criteria, or whether validation data was used to tune merges, raising risk of implicit cherry-picking.
