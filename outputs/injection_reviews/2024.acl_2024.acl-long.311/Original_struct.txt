Summary: This paper presents an empirical study of parameter-efficient preference alignment for LLMs, focusing on three axes: alignment dataset, alignment method, and base model type. Using over 300 experiments with 7B-scale models (LLaMA-1, Vicuna-v1.3, Mistral-7b, Mistral-7b-Instruct), two datasets (HH-RLHF and BeaverTails), two alignment methods (SFT and DPO), and two PEFT approaches (LoRA and QLoRA), the authors evaluate trade-offs between harmlessness and helpfulness on five benchmarks. The main findings are that higher-quality/more informative datasets generally help; SFT tends to work better for pre-trained models while DPO is stronger for instruction-tuned models and more faithful to explicit preferences like harmlessness; mixing distinct preferences in one alignment dataset often hurts; and some model-merging strategies can mitigate trade-offs. The paper is primarily a broad experimental benchmarking/guidelines paper rather than a methodological contribution.

Strengths: The paper studies an important and practical problem: how PEFT-based alignment choices affect downstream behavior, which is highly relevant for researchers without access to full fine-tuning budgets.; The experimental scope is broad: multiple base/instruction-tuned models, two major preference datasets, two alignment methods, both LoRA and QLoRA, and several evaluation benchmarks spanning helpfulness and harmlessness.; The paper is well organized around clear research axes and questions, making the empirical narrative easy to follow.; Several findings are practically useful, especially the distinction that SFT is often better for pre-trained models while DPO is more effective for instruction-tuned models.; The comparison between HH-RLHF and BeaverTails is valuable; the discussion of dataset informativeness/quality is grounded in concrete benchmark trends (e.g., figures on pages 4–5 and tables in the appendix).; The inclusion of model merging experiments adds an interesting extra angle beyond standard alignment comparisons.; The paper reports multiple seeds and gives substantial implementation detail, including hyperparameter grids and dataset construction details in the appendix.

Weaknesses: Many conclusions are stronger than what the evidence fully supports. The study is limited to a narrow set of models (all 7B), two preference dimensions, and only two alignment datasets, so several claimed 'guidelines' may not generalize broadly.; The methodology is not fully controlled across SFT and DPO: the two methods use different LoRA ranks, alphas, learning rates, dropout, target modules, and likely different optimization difficulty, making direct attribution to the alignment method itself less clean.; Training is capped at 700 steps for all settings, which may advantage some methods/models and disadvantage others; this fixed-budget setup may confound conclusions about intrinsic method quality.; Some evaluation choices are questionable or under-justified. For harmlessness, the paper relies on a toxicity reward model and 100-ASR on DangerousQA, which may not fully capture safety/alignment quality.; The paper often offers speculative explanations for observed behavior (e.g., pre-trained models not being strong reward models for DPO) without direct evidence.; Statistical significance is not clearly established in the main presentation despite running 5 seeds; variance/error bars and significance tests are largely absent from the reported figures/tables.; The model-merging section is interesting but somewhat shallow: only a small set of merge methods/settings are tried, and the explanation of why some merges help is limited.; The paper sometimes conflates performance on downstream benchmarks with 'faithfulness' to preferences, but the operationalization of faithfulness is not rigorously defined.

Soundness Issues: Direct SFT vs DPO comparisons are potentially confounded by unequal hyperparameterization and adapter configurations (page 13, Table 6), including different PEFT ranks and target modules.; The claim that DPO is better for instruction-tuned models and SFT for pre-trained models is plausible but may partly reflect the chosen models/datasets and fixed training budget rather than a robust causal conclusion.; The BeaverTails helpful/harmless splits are constructed by the authors (appendix page 12), which is reasonable, but this preprocessing introduces additional assumptions that may affect comparability with HH-RLHF.; The paper emphasizes dataset 'quality' differences between HH-RLHF and BeaverTails, but quality is not independently measured; the comparison may also reflect other differences such as scale, prompt distribution, single-turn vs multi-turn format, and label construction.; Harmlessness evaluation via a separate toxicity classifier/reward model may introduce evaluator bias and may not align perfectly with the paper's notion of harmlessness.; The paper reports many experiments and trends, but lacks formal uncertainty analysis in the main claims, making it hard to judge whether some observed differences are robust or small/noisy.
