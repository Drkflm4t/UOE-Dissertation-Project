Summary: This paper presents a large empirical study of parameter-efficient preference alignment for LLMs, focusing on three axes: alignment dataset, alignment method, and base model type. The authors run 300+ experiments using LoRA/QLoRA, two preference datasets (HH-RLHF and BeaverTails), two alignment methods (SFT and DPO), and four 7B models (LLaMA-1, Vicuna-v1.3, Mistral-7b, Mistral-7b-Instruct). Evaluations span helpfulness and harmlessness benchmarks including MMLU, BBH, AlpacaEval, RealToxicity, and Red-Instruct. The main findings are that higher-quality/more informative alignment data generally helps; SFT tends to work better for pre-trained models while DPO tends to work better for instruction-tuned models; DPO is more faithful for explicit preferences like harmlessness but can fail on weaker pre-trained bases; mixing distinct preferences often hurts; and model merging can partially mitigate trade-offs.

Strengths: Timely and practically relevant topic: PEFT-based alignment is important for researchers without access to full fine-tuning budgets.; Broad empirical scope with over 300 experiments across datasets, methods, model families, and both LoRA and QLoRA.; The paper studies multiple evaluation dimensions rather than relying on a single benchmark, covering both helpfulness and harmlessness.; Several findings are practically useful, especially the distinction that SFT is often preferable for pre-trained models while DPO is stronger for instruction-tuned models.; The comparison between HH-RLHF and BeaverTails is informative and highlights the importance of response informativeness/quality in alignment data.; The analysis of sample quantity and preference mixtures adds value beyond standard method comparisons.; The paper includes concrete tables/figures and summarizes findings into actionable guidelines for practitioners.; The appendix provides useful implementation details, dataset construction notes, and hyperparameter settings, improving reproducibility.

Weaknesses: The work is entirely empirical and offers limited mechanistic explanation for why the observed trends occur beyond plausible hypotheses.; The study is restricted to 7B-scale models and only two model families, so it is unclear how well the conclusions transfer to larger, stronger, or more recent models.; Only two preference dimensions are studied (helpfulness and harmlessness), which narrows the generality of the conclusions about alignment trade-offs.; Some conclusions are stated broadly even when the evidence appears model- or dataset-dependent.; The evaluation setup mixes different benchmark types and metrics, making it harder to aggregate or compare overall alignment quality consistently.; The model-merging section is interesting but relatively shallow; it feels more exploratory than conclusive.; The paper does not compare against stronger or more diverse alignment baselines beyond SFT/DPO, such as PPO/RLHF variants or other PEFT approaches.; Writing quality is generally understandable but there are occasional overstatements and some awkward phrasing/formatting issues.

Soundness Issues: The central claims are supported by a substantial experimental matrix, but some causal interpretations are speculative. For example, explanations about pre-trained models being weak reward models for DPO are plausible but not directly validated.; Hyperparameters differ between SFT and DPO (e.g., rank, alpha, learning rate, target modules), which is reasonable in practice but complicates strict apples-to-apples attribution of differences to the alignment method alone.; Training is capped at 700 steps across settings; this standardization may not be optimal for all datasets/models and could bias conclusions about relative method effectiveness.; The BeaverTails helpful/harmless splits are constructed by the authors from labels rather than provided natively, which is sensible but introduces preprocessing choices that may affect outcomes.; Some evaluation choices rely on proxy metrics or external evaluators (e.g., GPT-4 win rate in AlpacaEval, toxicity reward model for RealToxicity), which is common but adds evaluator dependence.; The claim that merged models generally perform better than individually aligned models is only partially supported and appears benchmark-dependent in Table 3, so this conclusion should be phrased more cautiously.
