Summary: This paper presents a large empirical study of parameter-efficient preference alignment for LLMs, focusing on three axes: alignment dataset, alignment method, and base model type. The authors run 300+ experiments using LoRA/QLoRA, two preference datasets (HH-RLHF and BeaverTails), two alignment methods (SFT and DPO), and four 7B-scale models (LLaMA-1, Vicuna-v1.3, Mistral-7b, Mistral-7b-Instruct). Evaluations span helpfulness and harmlessness benchmarks including MMLU, BBH, AlpacaEval, RealToxicity, and Red-Instruct. The main findings are that higher-quality/more informative alignment data generally helps; SFT tends to work better for pre-trained models while DPO tends to work better for instruction-tuned models; DPO is more faithful for explicit preferences such as harmlessness; mixing distinct preferences during alignment often hurts; and model merging can partially mitigate trade-offs. The paper aims to distill these observations into practical guidelines for PEFT-based alignment.

Strengths: The paper addresses an important and practically relevant problem: how PEFT choices affect preference alignment outcomes for open LLMs.; The experimental scope is substantial, with over 300 experiments across datasets, methods, model families, and both LoRA and QLoRA settings.; The study is organized around clear axes of variation—dataset, method, and base model—which makes the empirical conclusions easy to interpret and useful for practitioners.; The comparison between pre-trained and instruction-tuned models is valuable and yields actionable guidance: SFT is often preferable for pre-trained models, while DPO is often stronger for instruction-tuned ones.; The dataset-quality analysis is insightful, especially the comparison between HH-RLHF and BeaverTails and the observation that more informative harmless responses can reduce overfitting/degradation.; The paper evaluates both helpfulness and harmlessness rather than only one side of alignment, which gives a more balanced picture of trade-offs.; The inclusion of model merging experiments is a useful extension beyond standard alignment comparisons and suggests a promising mitigation strategy for conflicting preferences.; The appendix provides useful implementation details, including hyperparameter grids, dataset construction details for BeaverTails splits, and extended result tables.; The work is practically accessible, using single-GPU PEFT settings that many researchers can reproduce.; Several findings challenge common assumptions from prior literature, which increases the paper's value as an empirical reality check.

Weaknesses: The paper's claims are broad relative to the narrow experimental regime: only 7B models, two preference dimensions, and two datasets are studied, so generalization to larger models or other alignment settings remains uncertain.; Some conclusions are stated somewhat strongly despite mixed evidence across models and metrics; several trends appear model-dependent rather than universal.; The evaluation of harmlessness relies heavily on proxy metrics and a toxicity reward model, which may not fully capture real safety behavior or robustness.; The paper does not always disentangle whether observed gains come from preference alignment itself versus dataset/domain overlap or instruction-format compatibility.; The model-merging section is interesting but comparatively underdeveloped, with limited analysis of why certain merges help or fail.; Although 5 seeds are reportedly used, the main presentation does not consistently show variance/error bars or significance tests, making it hard to judge robustness of smaller differences.; The paper focuses on SFT and DPO but does not compare against stronger or more standard multi-stage baselines such as SFT followed by RLHF or other recent preference-optimization variants.

Methodological Flaws: There is a notable scientific-logic issue in the explanation of DPO behavior on pre-trained models: the paper repeatedly interprets the base model as 'acting like a reward model' and attributes failures to the model not being a 'strong inherent reward model.' While DPO can be derived from an implicit reward formulation, this explanation is informal and not rigorously justified by the experiments presented; the causal claim is speculative.; The training setups for SFT and DPO are not matched: they use different LoRA ranks, alphas, learning rates, dropout, target modules, and optimization settings (Table 6 / page 13). Therefore, some observed SFT-vs-DPO differences may be confounded by hyperparameterization rather than reflecting the alignment objective alone.; The comparison budget is not fully normalized. Both methods are trained for 700 steps, but because datasets differ greatly in size and DPO/SFT have different optimization dynamics, equal steps do not imply equal effective training exposure or fairness.; The BeaverTails helpful/harmless splits are constructed by the authors from labels (Appendix C), but the paper does not deeply analyze whether this induces selection biases or makes the helpfulness and harmlessness subsets incomparable to HH-RLHF.; The harmlessness metric on RealToxicity uses a separate toxicity classifier from Hugging Face rather than human evaluation; this introduces evaluator-model bias and weakens strong claims about safety alignment.; Some empirical claims are overstated relative to the evidence. For example, statements such as 'models merged across distinct preferences generally perform better than the models aligned on individual preferences' are not uniformly true across all metrics in Table 3, where some merges substantially hurt AlpacaEval or toxicity.; The paper does not provide statistical uncertainty in the main figures/tables despite claiming 5 seeds. Without confidence intervals or hypothesis testing, it is difficult to assess whether modest improvements (e.g., small MMLU/BBH changes) are meaningful.; There is no formal analysis of catastrophic forgetting or distribution shift, yet several conclusions about degradation under SFT are framed in those terms. The evidence is suggestive but not sufficient to isolate mechanism.
