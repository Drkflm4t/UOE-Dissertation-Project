Summary: This paper is a narrative review of literature on zero-shot cross-lingual transfer in multilingual language models (MLLMs). It organizes prior work into five factor categories: linguistic similarity, lexical overlap, model architecture, pre-training settings, and pre-training data. The paper summarizes findings from a curated set of studies (with overview tables on pages 3 and 13), discusses apparent contradictions across papers, and offers qualitative reconciliations—for example, that lexical overlap may matter more when word order differs or when source-language pre-training data is limited, and that syntax/word order effects may be overstated in studies using unrealistic synthetic perturbations. The paper’s main contribution is synthesis and taxonomy rather than new experiments or formal theory.

Strengths: Timely and useful survey topic: understanding cross-lingual transfer factors in MLLMs is important for multilingual NLP and low-resource language transfer.; Clear organizational structure around five factor classes, making the literature easier to navigate.; Good breadth of cited work across linguistic, architectural, and data-centric explanations.; The tables (especially page 3 and Appendix table on page 13) are helpful for summarizing papers, tasks, models, language types, and investigated factors.; The paper does more than list papers: it attempts to reconcile conflicting findings and identify interactions among factors.; The discussion section appropriately highlights limitations of prior evidence, including differences between synthetic and natural language studies and the risks of over-interpreting feature importance from predictive meta-models.; The paper identifies several actionable future directions, such as studying factor interactions, task-specific effects, and generative multilingual models.

Weaknesses: The review methodology is insufficiently rigorous for a survey/review paper: there is no systematic search protocol, inclusion/exclusion criteria, time window, database coverage, or reproducible paper selection process.; The synthesis is largely qualitative and sometimes speculative; several conflict-resolution claims are plausible but not strongly substantiated by a systematic comparative framework.; There is no quantitative meta-analysis, standardized evidence grading, or explicit weighting of studies by experimental quality, scale, or ecological validity.; Some conclusions are stronger than the evidence warrants, especially when inferred from a small number of heterogeneous studies.; The taxonomy is useful but not obviously exhaustive or orthogonal; interactions among categories are acknowledged but not formalized.; The paper occasionally relies on feature-importance analyses from predictive models to infer causal importance, while also noting this limitation; this creates some tension in the argumentative logic.; As a review, it would benefit from clearer separation between reported findings from prior work and the authors’ own hypotheses/speculations.; Coverage appears focused on encoder-style models such as mBERT/XLM-R, with limited treatment of newer multilingual generative LMs, reducing current relevance.

Methodological Flaws: Major review-methodology weakness: the paper does not provide a systematic literature review protocol. Without explicit search terms, venues, screening criteria, and selection procedure, claims of comprehensiveness or consensus are difficult to verify and may suffer from selection bias.; The paper frequently compares results across studies with heterogeneous tasks, datasets, language sets, metrics, and model scales, but lacks a principled normalization framework. This weakens the validity of cross-study causal conclusions.; Several reconciliations of contradictory findings are speculative rather than demonstrated. For example, the explanation that discrepancies about lexical overlap are due to confounding with word order is plausible, but the review does not systematically test this hypothesis across the surveyed studies.; The paper sometimes treats correlations or feature-importance rankings from meta-predictive models as evidence of factor importance. Scientifically, feature importance in models such as boosted trees or Lasso does not establish causal effect and is sensitive to collinearity, model class, and preprocessing.; The conclusion that syntax may be overestimated because random permutation/inversion is unnatural is reasonable, but it is not backed by a formal assessment of ecological validity across the surveyed studies; thus it should be framed more cautiously.; No formal quality assessment of included studies is provided. For a review paper, absence of study-quality appraisal makes it hard to distinguish stronger empirical evidence from weaker controlled-but-artificial setups.; The paper does not clearly define how conflicting evidence is adjudicated when studies differ in external validity (synthetic vs. natural languages), which can lead to inconsistent scientific weighting of evidence.
