This paper presents a review of factors that influence zero-shot cross-lingual transfer in multilingual language models, with the explicit goal of consolidating a fragmented literature into a common framework. The paper organizes prior work into five categories—linguistic similarity, lexical overlap, model architecture, pre-training settings, and pre-training data—and attempts not only to summarize findings but also to reconcile apparent contradictions across studies. Overall, I found the paper timely, useful, and thoughtfully written. Cross-lingual transfer in multilingual LMs remains one of the most practically important yet conceptually under-explained phenomena in multilingual NLP, and a synthesis paper focused specifically on explanatory factors is valuable. The manuscript succeeds in providing a structured map of the literature and, importantly, goes beyond a superficial catalog by offering hypotheses for why prior results disagree.

A major strength of the paper is its scope and organization. The five-factor taxonomy is intuitive and broad enough to cover the main explanatory axes that have emerged in the literature, while still being concrete enough to guide future work. The paper is especially effective in Sections 3.1 and 3.2, where it carefully disentangles linguistic similarity and lexical overlap—two notions that are often conflated in multilingual NLP discussions. The authors do a good job of showing that these factors are neither interchangeable nor universally predictive, and that their effects depend on task type, source-target language pair, and interactions with other variables such as word order and corpus size. This kind of nuanced synthesis is exactly what a good review should provide.

Another strong aspect is the paper’s effort to resolve contradictions rather than merely list them. For example, in the discussion of word order, the paper notes that studies using random permutation or reversal may overstate the importance of syntax relative to more realistic structured modifications. Likewise, in the lexical overlap section, the authors propose a plausible reconciliation: lexical overlap appears more important when word order differs or when source-language pretraining data is limited, which helps explain why some studies find strong effects and others do not. These integrative arguments are among the most valuable contributions of the paper. They demonstrate that the authors have engaged critically with the literature rather than simply summarizing it.

The tables are also useful. Table 1 on page 3 gives a compact overview of studies on linguistic similarity, including task, model, language type, features, and metrics. Table 2 in the appendix similarly summarizes the surveyed papers by task, model, language type, and factor studied. These tables substantially improve the paper’s utility as a reference document. I also appreciated that the paper explicitly distinguishes studies using natural versus synthetic languages, since this methodological distinction is central to interpreting the evidence.

The discussion section is thoughtful and forward-looking. I particularly liked the argument that future multilingual modeling might benefit from thinking in terms of distributions of linguistic features rather than distributions of named languages. That is a provocative and potentially fruitful perspective. The paper also appropriately highlights the need to study interactions among factors and task-specific differences, rather than searching for a single dominant explanation of cross-lingual transfer. The call for more multilingual evaluation datasets and for more work on generative models is well motivated.

That said, there are some limitations that prevent the paper from being fully definitive. The most important is methodological: the review does not appear to follow a systematic review protocol. The paper acknowledges possible selection bias in the limitations section, but the issue is somewhat broader. It is not entirely clear how papers were identified, what inclusion/exclusion criteria were used, whether the search was exhaustive, or how the authors decided which findings counted as sufficiently comparable to synthesize. For a review paper whose main contribution is consolidation of evidence, more transparency on the literature collection process would strengthen confidence in the comprehensiveness and reproducibility of the survey. Even a concise paragraph describing search strategy, time window, venues, and screening criteria would help substantially.

Relatedly, while the paper often discusses “consensus” and “contradictions,” it does not provide a formal framework for weighing evidence across studies. Since the surveyed works differ substantially in tasks, models, language sets, synthetic vs. natural settings, and evaluation methodology, some findings should arguably carry more weight than others. At present, the synthesis is largely narrative. That is acceptable for a review of this kind, but the paper would be stronger if it more explicitly distinguished robust conclusions from tentative hypotheses. For example, one could imagine a summary table indicating for each factor whether evidence is strong, mixed, or limited, and under what conditions.

A second limitation is that some categories remain somewhat broad internally. “Pre-training settings” and “pre-training data” each bundle together several heterogeneous variables. This is understandable, but it occasionally makes the narrative feel less sharply structured than in the linguistic sections. For instance, tokenizer quality, vocabulary size, sequence length, and NSP removal are all grouped under pre-training settings, yet these operate at rather different levels. Similarly, corpus size and corpus source/domain are both important but conceptually distinct aspects of pre-training data. A slightly more hierarchical decomposition within these sections could improve clarity.

I also think the paper could do a bit more to define what exactly is meant by “cross-lingual transfer capacity.” In practice, the review focuses on zero-shot transfer performance after source-language fine-tuning, which is a reasonable operationalization and is stated in Section 2.2. However, some surveyed studies probe representation alignment directly, while others evaluate downstream task accuracy. These are related but not identical notions. A more explicit discussion of this distinction would help readers interpret the evidence, especially when the paper uses representational findings to support claims about downstream transfer.

Another area where the paper could be strengthened is in coverage of model families beyond encoder-only Transformer models such as mBERT and XLM-R. The paper does mention in the discussion that generative models remain underexplored, which is true, but the review is still heavily centered on the BERT/XLM-R era. Given the title’s broad framing around multilingual language models, readers may expect somewhat more explicit positioning with respect to encoder-decoder and decoder-only multilingual models, even if the conclusion is that the literature is still sparse. This is not a fatal issue, but a short subsection clarifying the scope would make the contribution more precise.

There are also a few places where the paper’s interpretive claims are plausible but somewhat speculative. For example, the explanation that Lasso regression may understate nonlinear effects relative to tree-based models is sensible, but it remains a hypothesis unless directly validated in the reviewed literature. Similarly, some reconciliations of contradictory findings rely on inferred correlations among lexical overlap, script, and word order in the evaluated language sets. These arguments are useful and often convincing, but the paper should consistently signal when it is moving from reported evidence to author interpretation.

On presentation, the manuscript is generally clear and readable. The prose is polished, and the section-level conclusions are helpful. I noticed only minor issues: a few stylistic inconsistencies, occasional awkward phrasing, and some formatting artifacts in the parsed text (for example, punctuation duplication around “effectiveness..” on page 2), which are likely easy to fix. These do not materially affect the paper’s quality. The references appear broad and relevant, and the related work section appropriately distinguishes this survey from prior broader surveys on multilingual models and cross-lingual transfer.

In terms of significance, I think this paper fills a useful niche. There are many empirical papers on multilingual transfer and several broad surveys, but fewer works that specifically synthesize explanatory factors behind zero-shot transfer and try to unify conflicting findings. For researchers entering the area, this paper would be a valuable starting point; for established researchers, it offers a compact synthesis and several useful hypotheses about interactions among factors. I can also imagine it being practically useful for designing transfer setups, selecting source languages, or motivating new multilingual pretraining strategies.

My overall assessment is positive. The paper’s main contribution is not new empirical evidence but a careful conceptual consolidation of an important literature, and I think it does that well. Its strongest contributions are the five-factor taxonomy, the nuanced synthesis of linguistic similarity and lexical overlap, the explicit attention to contradictions across prior studies, and the forward-looking discussion of interactions and future directions. The main weaknesses are the lack of a more systematic review methodology and the somewhat uneven depth across categories, but these are addressable and do not undermine the paper’s value as a scholarly synthesis.

I would recommend acceptance, especially if the venue values survey/review contributions that clarify an active research area. To further strengthen the final version, I would encourage the authors to add a brief methodology section for paper selection, sharpen the distinction between stronger and weaker conclusions, and perhaps include a compact summary figure or table that maps each factor to the tasks/settings where its effect is most consistently observed. Even without these additions, I found the paper informative, well motivated, and genuinely useful.
