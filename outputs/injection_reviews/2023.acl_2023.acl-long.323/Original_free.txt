Summary

This paper is a review of factors that influence zero-shot cross-lingual transfer in multilingual language models (MLLMs). The authors organize prior work into five categories: linguistic similarity, lexical overlap, model architecture, pre-training settings, and pre-training data. They aim not only to summarize findings, but also to reconcile contradictions across studies and provide a common reference point for future work. The paper includes useful summary tables, especially Table 1 (page 3) on linguistic-similarity studies and Table 2 in the appendix (page 13) listing surveyed papers by task, model, language type, and factor.

Overall assessment

The paper addresses an important and timely topic. Cross-lingual transfer remains central to multilingual NLP, and the literature is indeed fragmented across tasks, models, and experimental setups. The paper’s main strength is its attempt to synthesize disparate findings into a coherent taxonomy and to discuss why prior studies disagree. In particular, the discussion of interactions between factors—especially lexical overlap and word order—is thoughtful and more useful than a purely descriptive survey.

That said, the paper is somewhat limited as a rigorous review. The methodology for selecting papers is underdeveloped, the coverage is selective rather than systematic, and some of the “conflict resolution” remains speculative. The paper is valuable as a narrative review and orientation piece, but it falls short of the standards of a fully systematic survey or meta-analysis. I would view it as a useful synthesis with moderate novelty, but with room for improvement in rigor, completeness, and precision.

Strengths

1. Clear and useful taxonomy
The five-factor organization is intuitive and effective. It gives the review a strong structure and makes it easier for readers to navigate a broad literature. The categories are broad enough to cover most of the relevant space while still being concrete.

2. Good synthesis of conflicting findings
The paper does more than list papers. In several places it tries to explain why prior results differ. The discussion of lexical overlap is the strongest example: the authors argue that lexical overlap may matter more when word order differs or when source-language pretraining data is limited. This is a useful integrative perspective.

3. Attention to interactions and task dependence
A recurring theme is that factors do not operate independently and that their importance varies by task. This is an important message for the field. The paper repeatedly notes that lower-level tasks and higher-level tasks may behave differently, and that conclusions drawn from one setup may not generalize.

4. Helpful summary tables
Table 1 on page 3 and Table 2 on page 13 are useful reference resources. They summarize tasks, models, language types, features, and metrics in a compact way. For readers entering the area, these tables add practical value.

5. Balanced tone
The paper generally avoids overclaiming. The limitations section acknowledges selection bias, methodological heterogeneity, and publication bias. This is appropriate for a review paper.

Weaknesses

1. Review methodology is not sufficiently rigorous
The biggest weakness is the lack of a clear survey methodology. The paper says it surveys literature and provides a list in Appendix A, but it does not explain:
- how papers were identified,
- what inclusion/exclusion criteria were used,
- what time period was covered,
- whether the search was systematic,
- how disagreements or borderline cases were handled.

Without this, it is hard to assess completeness or reproducibility. For a review paper, this is a significant issue.

2. “Resolving conflicts” is sometimes speculative
The paper often proposes plausible explanations for contradictory findings, but these explanations are usually not directly validated. For example, the discussion of why lexical overlap findings differ across Pires et al., Tran and Bisazza, Wu and Dredze, K et al., and Conneau et al. is interesting, but still largely inferential. That is acceptable in a narrative review, but the paper sometimes presents these reconciliations with more confidence than the evidence warrants.

3. Coverage is somewhat narrow and centered on encoder-style MLMs
The review focuses mainly on mBERT, XLM, XLM-R, and related encoder models. That is understandable given the topic, but the title is broad (“multilingual language models”), and the paper itself notes only briefly in the discussion that generative models remain underexplored. Given the current landscape, the scope should either be narrowed more explicitly in the title/positioning or expanded to include multilingual seq2seq and decoder-based models where relevant.

4. Limited depth on evaluation methodology
The paper discusses factors affecting transfer, but less attention is given to how transfer is measured and how evaluation choices shape conclusions. For example:
- zero-shot transfer can vary substantially by source language choice,
- benchmark composition can bias conclusions,
- task formulations differ across papers,
- some studies use synthetic languages while others use natural languages,
- some use feature-importance predictors rather than direct causal interventions.

These differences are mentioned, especially in the discussion, but they deserve a more systematic treatment earlier in the paper.

5. Some categories overlap conceptually
The distinction between “linguistic similarity” and “lexical overlap” is useful, but in practice the literature often entangles them. Likewise, “pre-training settings” and “pre-training data” interact strongly. The paper acknowledges interactions, but the taxonomy can sometimes give a cleaner separation than the evidence supports.

6. Writing quality is generally good but uneven in places
There are a few awkward phrasings and minor grammatical issues. Examples include occasional punctuation problems, repeated wording, and some slightly imprecise claims. These do not prevent understanding, but they reduce polish. There is also at least one typo-like issue on page 2 (“effectiveness..”).

Detailed comments by section

Introduction
The introduction motivates the problem well. The contrast between models with explicit cross-lingual objectives and those trained only on monolingual corpora is a good entry point. The five-factor framing is introduced clearly.

One suggestion: the introduction could better define what counts as an “explanation” of cross-lingual transfer. Is the goal causal understanding, predictive understanding, or practical guidance? The paper mixes these aims somewhat.

Background
The background section is concise and adequate. The explanation of zero-shot transfer is clear. The decision to exclude translation-based transfer methods from the main focus is reasonable and well justified.

However, the background could better distinguish:
- transfer performance as an empirical outcome,
- representational alignment as a hypothesized mechanism,
- and source-language selection as a downstream practical problem.

These are related but not identical.

Section 3.1: Linguistic Similarity
This is one of the stronger sections. The paper does a good job summarizing metrics such as WALS and lang2vec and distinguishing studies using natural versus synthetic languages. Table 1 on page 3 is especially helpful here.

The discussion of word order is nuanced. I appreciated the point that random permutation or reversal may overestimate the importance of syntax because such perturbations are unnatural. That is a valuable caution.

Still, the section could be improved by being more precise about what “syntax” means across studies. Word order is only one aspect of syntax, and some cited works may operationalize syntactic similarity differently. The review sometimes slides between “word order” and “syntax” too quickly.

Section 3.2: Lexical Overlap
This is probably the paper’s best section. The authors do a strong job of organizing apparently contradictory findings and proposing a coherent interpretation: lexical overlap is not a sufficient standalone explanation, but it matters more under certain conditions, especially when word order differs or when source-language resources are limited.

The discussion of cross-script transfer is also useful. The paper correctly notes that successful transfer across scripts argues against simplistic lexical-overlap explanations.

My main concern here is evidential strength. The synthesis is plausible and insightful, but it would benefit from a clearer distinction between:
- directly established findings,
- the authors’ interpretation,
- and open hypotheses.

Section 3.3: Model Architecture
This section is informative but thinner than the previous two. The review highlights depth, parameter count, attention heads, parameter sharing, and embedding alignment. The summary that overparameterization may encourage language-specific subspaces is interesting.

However, this section feels underdeveloped relative to the importance of the topic. For example, there is little discussion of:
- encoder-only vs encoder-decoder architectures,
- vocabulary sharing as an architectural/design choice versus a tokenizer choice,
- layerwise specialization,
- positional encoding choices,
- or scaling effects beyond the specific cited studies.

If the literature is sparse, that is fine, but then the paper should say more explicitly that this area remains underexplored.

Section 3.4: Pre-Training Settings
This section is useful, especially the discussion of NSP, subword vs word/character units, sequence length, and tokenizer quality. The distinction between tokenizer quality effects on token-level versus sentence-level tasks is sensible.

Still, the section could better separate:
- objective design,
- tokenization/vocabulary design,
- and optimization/training schedule choices.

Currently these are grouped together under “pre-training settings,” which is understandable, but somewhat broad.

Section 3.5: Pre-Training Data
This section makes an important point: target-language pretraining corpus size and corpus comparability matter, and their effects vary by task. The discussion of domain/source mismatch is useful.

A limitation is that the section focuses mostly on corpus size and source/domain, but less on other important data properties such as:
- data quality/noise,
- deduplication,
- balance across languages,
- genre diversity,
- script normalization,
- and token distribution skew.

Again, perhaps the literature is limited, but the review could frame these as open dimensions more explicitly.

Related Work and Discussion
The related work section is brief but adequate. The discussion section is thoughtful and contains some of the paper’s most interesting forward-looking ideas, especially the suggestion that multilingual modeling might benefit from organizing around linguistic features rather than language identities. That is provocative and worth raising.

At the same time, some of these proposals are quite speculative and would benefit from being framed more cautiously.

Limitations and ethics
It is good that the paper includes both sections. The limitations section is honest, though it also underscores the need for a more systematic review methodology. The ethics statement is well intentioned, though somewhat generic.

Novelty and significance

For a review paper, the novelty lies in synthesis rather than new experiments. The main contribution is the five-part taxonomy and the attempt to reconcile conflicting findings. I think this is moderately novel and useful. The paper is likely to help newcomers and could serve as a reference point for researchers designing multilingual transfer studies.

Its significance is practical rather than foundational: it organizes the space and highlights interactions and caveats. That said, because the review is not systematic and some conclusions are interpretive, I would be cautious about treating it as definitive.

Questions / suggestions for improvement

1. Strengthen the review methodology
Please add a dedicated section describing:
- search sources,
- keywords,
- date range,
- inclusion/exclusion criteria,
- number of papers initially identified and finally included,
- and how papers were categorized.

Even a lightweight PRISMA-style description would substantially improve rigor.

2. Clarify scope in title and framing
If the review is mainly about encoder-style multilingual masked language models and zero-shot transfer, say so more explicitly. The current title is broad.

3. Distinguish evidence from interpretation
Throughout Sections 3.1 and 3.2 especially, mark more clearly which statements are:
- consensus findings,
- mixed findings,
- or the authors’ hypotheses for reconciliation.

4. Add a summary figure
A visual summary would help. For example, a matrix with factors on one axis and tasks/models/settings on the other, indicating strength/consistency of evidence, would make the review more actionable.

5. Discuss causality more explicitly
Some cited studies use controlled interventions, while others use correlational predictors or feature importance. These support different strengths of inference. The review should explicitly distinguish causal evidence from correlational evidence.

6. Expand discussion of underexplored areas
The paper already mentions generative models. It would also be useful to highlight gaps such as:
- multilingual instruction tuning,
- decoder-only multilingual LMs,
- code-switching,
- morphology-rich low-resource languages,
- and evaluation beyond standard benchmarks.

7. Improve writing and polish
A careful proofreading pass would help. There are minor grammatical and stylistic issues throughout.

Recommendation

Weak Accept / Borderline

Rationale

I find the paper useful and reasonably well written, and I think the taxonomy plus the synthesis of conflicting findings provide value. The tables, especially Table 1 on page 3 and Table 2 on page 13, make the paper a practical reference. However, the paper is better characterized as a narrative review than a rigorous systematic survey, and some of its conflict-resolution claims are more speculative than conclusive. If the venue values broad, integrative review papers, I would lean positive. If it expects stronger methodological rigor for surveys, I would be more hesitant.

Confidence

High. The paper’s goals, structure, and claims are clear, and the main strengths and weaknesses are straightforward to assess from the manuscript.
