This paper is a survey/review of factors that influence zero-shot cross-lingual transfer in multilingual language models (MLLMs). It organizes prior work into five categories—linguistic similarity, lexical overlap, model architecture, pre-training settings, and pre-training data—and aims not only to summarize findings but also to reconcile apparent contradictions across studies. Overall, I found the paper timely, useful, and reasonably well structured. The topic is important: cross-lingual transfer remains one of the central empirical phenomena behind multilingual NLP, and a synthesis of what is known about its drivers is valuable for both practitioners and researchers. The paper’s main strength is that it goes beyond a superficial catalog of papers and attempts to articulate where the literature agrees, where it conflicts, and why those conflicts may arise.

The paper is clearly motivated. The introduction frames the puzzle well: strong cross-lingual transfer emerges even in models such as mBERT and XLM-R that are not explicitly trained with parallel supervision. The authors then define a concrete scope—traditional zero-shot transfer without translation-based pipelines—which is a sensible boundary for a review of this kind. The five-factor taxonomy is intuitive and broad enough to cover much of the literature. I also appreciated the effort to provide consolidated tables. In particular, Table 1 on page 3 and Table 2 in Appendix A on page 13 are helpful reference artifacts: they summarize papers by task, model, language type, features/factors, and metrics, making the survey easier to navigate than many narrative-only reviews.

A major positive aspect is the paper’s attempt to explain contradictions rather than merely listing them. For example, in the discussion of linguistic similarity, the authors note that studies using random permutation or reversal of word order may overstate the importance of syntax relative to more realistic structured modifications. This is a thoughtful point. Similarly, in the lexical overlap section, the paper proposes that conflicting findings can be partly explained by interactions with word order and resource level. The synthesis around Deshpande et al. (2022), K et al. (2020), and Conneau et al. (2020b) is one of the stronger parts of the paper: it gives the reader a plausible unifying interpretation instead of leaving the literature fragmented. The paper also repeatedly emphasizes interactions among factors and task-specific effects, which is exactly the right message for this area.

That said, the paper also has several limitations that reduce its rigor as a top-tier survey. The most important issue is methodological transparency. The paper states that it surveys the literature and provides a list of papers in Appendix A, but it does not describe a systematic search and selection protocol. There is no explicit account of search terms, databases, inclusion/exclusion criteria, time window, screening process, or how the authors handled overlapping versions (e.g., arXiv vs. conference publications). As a result, the review reads more like a narrative survey than a systematic review. That is acceptable in principle, but then the claims about “consensus,” “conflicts,” and “resolution” should be framed more cautiously. Without a transparent methodology, it is difficult to assess completeness or selection bias. The authors do acknowledge selection bias in the limitations section on pages 9–10, which is good, but this remains a substantive weakness.

A second concern is that many of the paper’s “resolutions” of contradictory findings are plausible hypotheses rather than firmly established conclusions. For instance, the explanation that differences between tree-based models and Lasso regression may account for conflicting feature-importance results in lexical overlap is reasonable, but it is still speculative. Likewise, the claim that syntax may be overestimated because random permutations are unrealistic is sensible, but it is not directly validated within the survey. In several places, the paper moves from summarizing evidence to advancing its own interpretive hypotheses. That is not inherently problematic, but the distinction between evidence-backed synthesis and author conjecture should be made sharper. Phrases such as “we hypothesize” are used in some places, which helps, but the overall narrative occasionally sounds more definitive than the underlying evidence warrants.

A third weakness is that the taxonomy, while useful, sometimes blurs causal boundaries between factors. Linguistic similarity and lexical overlap are treated separately, but in practice they are often entangled with script, tokenizer behavior, vocabulary construction, and pre-training corpus composition. The paper acknowledges interactions, yet the organization remains largely factor-by-factor. This makes the survey readable, but it also risks reinforcing a decomposition that the literature itself increasingly suggests is not cleanly separable. A stronger survey might include a dedicated synthesis section or conceptual diagram showing which factors are likely upstream, downstream, or mediating variables. For example, script similarity affects lexical overlap, which affects tokenizer quality, which affects token-level transfer; corpus size and domain affect representation quality and may moderate the role of overlap. The current discussion hints at this but does not fully formalize it.

Relatedly, the paper could do more to distinguish correlation from causation across the surveyed evidence. Some studies use controlled synthetic interventions, while others rely on observational correlations over natural languages and feature-importance analyses from predictive meta-models. These are very different evidentiary regimes. The paper mentions this distinction in the discussion on page 9, especially regarding synthetic versus natural languages and the limitations of feature importance, but it would benefit from a more systematic treatment throughout. For example, each factor section could explicitly separate evidence from controlled manipulation studies versus evidence from correlational studies. That would help readers calibrate confidence in the conclusions.

The coverage is generally solid for the stated scope, but there are some omissions or underdeveloped areas. First, the survey is heavily centered on encoder-style models such as mBERT, XLM, and XLM-R, which is understandable given the literature up to 2023, but the paper itself notes in the discussion that generative models remain underexplored. Since the title is broad—“multilingual language models”—the paper could better signal in the title, abstract, or early framing that the review is primarily about encoder-based or BERT-like MLLMs and zero-shot transfer in discriminative tasks. Second, the survey focuses on zero-shot transfer performance but says relatively little about representation-level diagnostics beyond a few cited works. A more explicit subsection on probing/alignment evidence could strengthen the bridge between behavioral findings and mechanistic explanations. Third, the paper does not deeply engage with evaluation artifacts and benchmark limitations, despite the fact that cross-lingual transfer results are known to be sensitive to dataset translation quality, annotation projection, domain mismatch, and label semantics. Some of this is outside scope, but it matters for interpreting the surveyed findings.

I also found the treatment of tasks somewhat uneven. The paper repeatedly distinguishes “higher-level” tasks such as NLI and QA from “lower-level” tasks such as POS, NER, and DP, and this distinction is often useful. However, the categorization is a bit coarse and occasionally under-argued. NER, for example, is labeled as token-level/low-level, but its transfer behavior is also strongly shaped by named entity distribution, orthography, and domain. QA is grouped as higher-level, but multilingual QA datasets vary substantially in whether they test retrieval, span extraction, or translation artifacts. The survey would be stronger if it were more explicit about what dimensions of task variation matter: token vs. sentence level, syntactic vs. semantic, extractive vs. generative, domain sensitivity, label-space invariance, and so on.

On writing quality, the paper is mostly clear and readable. The structure is logical, and the section conclusions are helpful. The tables are useful, especially the summary table on page 13. However, there are a number of minor language and presentation issues. I noticed occasional grammatical awkwardness and typographical inconsistencies, such as duplicated punctuation (“effectiveness..” on page 2), inconsistent capitalization, and some slightly imprecise phrasing. These do not obstruct understanding, but they do give the manuscript a somewhat less polished feel than ideal for a survey paper. There are also places where claims could be more carefully hedged. For example, statements like “syntax has been suggested as potentially the most important linguistic contributor” are acceptable, but the confidence level should be tied more explicitly to the nature and breadth of the evidence.

One thing I appreciated is that the paper includes a limitations section and an ethics statement. For a review paper, this is good practice. The limitations section on pages 9–10 is honest about selection bias, methodological heterogeneity, and publication bias. Still, some of these limitations are severe enough that they should influence the framing of the contributions more strongly in the abstract and introduction. The abstract currently claims to “identify consensuses” and “resolve conflicts,” which may overstate what a narrative review without systematic methodology can do. A more accurate framing would be that the paper proposes explanations for conflicts and highlights emerging patterns.

In terms of novelty, the paper’s contribution is primarily organizational and synthetic rather than empirical. That is appropriate for a review. Its value lies in consolidating a scattered literature and offering a common vocabulary. I think it succeeds reasonably well at that. The five-factor categorization is not radically new, but it is practical and likely useful to readers entering the area. The strongest novel aspect is the cross-paper synthesis of contradictory findings, especially around lexical overlap and syntax. The weakest aspect is that some of these syntheses are not yet backed by a sufficiently systematic or formal comparative methodology.

For improvement, I would strongly encourage the authors to add a dedicated methodology section for the review process. Even a lightweight but explicit protocol would substantially improve credibility: where papers were searched, what years were covered, what counted as in-scope, whether only peer-reviewed papers were included, how preprints were handled, and how the final set in Appendix A was assembled. I would also recommend a clearer evidentiary taxonomy throughout the survey: controlled intervention studies, correlational studies over natural languages, predictive meta-model studies, and representation/probing studies. This would help readers understand which conclusions are robust and which are tentative. A summary figure showing hypothesized interactions among the five factor groups would also be valuable. Finally, the paper would benefit from a more explicit “open problems” section that prioritizes unresolved questions, such as disentangling script from overlap, understanding scaling effects in multilingual generative models, and designing benchmarks that isolate transfer mechanisms rather than dataset artifacts.

My overall assessment is positive but not unreservedly so. This is a useful and thoughtful survey on an important topic, with clear structure and several insightful syntheses. Its main weaknesses are the lack of systematic review methodology, occasional overstatement of conclusions, and insufficient separation of causal versus correlational evidence. If judged as a narrative survey, it is solid and likely helpful to the community. If judged by the standards of a rigorous top-tier review article, it would benefit from stronger methodological grounding and sharper calibration of claims.

In summary, I see this paper as a worthwhile contribution that can serve as a practical entry point and reference for researchers working on multilingual transfer. Its tables and factor-based organization are useful, and its discussion of contradictions is often insightful. To reach a higher standard, however, the paper should be more explicit about how the literature was selected, more careful in distinguishing evidence from hypothesis, and more systematic in organizing the strength and type of evidence behind each claimed factor.
