Summary: This paper is a narrative review of the literature on zero-shot cross-lingual transfer in multilingual language models (MLLMs). It organizes prior work into five factor categories—linguistic similarity, lexical overlap, model architecture, pre-training settings, and pre-training data—and synthesizes findings across studies using both natural and synthetic languages. The paper’s main contribution is not new experiments, but a structured consolidation of empirical evidence, including attempts to reconcile contradictory findings. It argues that syntax/word order, lexical overlap under certain conditions, architectural sharing/capacity trade-offs, tokenizer and objective choices, and target-language pre-training data all influence transfer, often in task-dependent and interacting ways. The review also highlights open questions, especially around factor interactions, task-specific effects, and implications for generative models.

Strengths: Timely and relevant topic: understanding cross-lingual transfer in MLLMs is important for multilingual NLP, especially for low-resource languages.; Clear organizational structure around five factor categories, which makes a broad literature easier to navigate.; Useful synthesis of conflicting findings rather than merely listing papers; the paper often proposes plausible explanations for discrepancies across studies.; Good coverage of both language-intrinsic factors (e.g., syntax, lexical overlap) and model/training factors (e.g., architecture, tokenizer, pre-training corpus size/source).; Helpful summary tables (e.g., pages 3 and 13) that catalog papers, tasks, models, language types, and investigated factors.; The discussion section goes beyond summary and identifies broader themes such as factor interactions, task dependence, and limitations of synthetic-language studies and feature-importance-based analyses.; The paper explicitly states limitations and potential biases of the review, which improves transparency.; The review is likely to be a useful reference point for researchers designing multilingual transfer studies or selecting source languages.

Weaknesses: As a review paper, the work does not introduce a systematic review protocol, quantitative meta-analysis, or reproducible inclusion/exclusion criteria, which limits rigor relative to a formal survey methodology.; Several reconciliations of contradictory findings are plausible but speculative; they are not empirically validated within the paper.; The paper sometimes draws fairly strong conclusions from heterogeneous studies with different tasks, models, datasets, and evaluation setups, which may reduce comparability.; Coverage appears focused primarily on encoder-style MLLMs such as mBERT/XLM-R, with limited treatment of newer generative multilingual LLMs.; Some claims about relative importance of factors remain qualitative and would benefit from a more explicit evidence grading scheme.

Methodological Flaws: The paper is a narrative review rather than a systematic review: it does not specify a search strategy, screening process, inclusion/exclusion criteria, or inter-reviewer validation. This is a methodological limitation because the synthesis may be affected by selection bias.; There is no formal meta-analytic framework to aggregate effect sizes across studies. As a result, statements about which factors are 'more important' are not quantitatively established and may overgeneralize from incomparable experimental settings.; Some conflict-resolution arguments rely on post hoc reasoning. For example, explanations for contradictions around lexical overlap and word order are sensible, but they are inferential rather than demonstrated by new controlled evidence in this paper.; The review occasionally treats feature importance from predictive models across different studies as broadly comparable, despite differences in model class (e.g., tree-based models vs. Lasso), feature definitions, and datasets; this weakens causal interpretation.; Empirical validity is constrained by the underlying literature’s heterogeneity, and the paper does not provide a formal framework for weighting stronger versus weaker evidence (e.g., controlled synthetic studies vs. observational correlations on natural languages).
