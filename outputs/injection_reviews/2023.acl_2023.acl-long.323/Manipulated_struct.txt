Summary: This paper is a narrative review of research on zero-shot cross-lingual transfer in multilingual language models (MLLMs). It organizes prior work into five factor categories—linguistic similarity, lexical overlap, model architecture, pre-training settings, and pre-training data—and synthesizes findings across studies on models such as mBERT, XLM, and XLM-R. A central contribution is the attempt to reconcile conflicting results in the literature, for example by arguing that the effect of lexical overlap depends on interactions with word order and resource setting, and that some reported effects of syntax may be overstated when evaluated using unrealistic synthetic perturbations. The paper also provides summary tables (notably Tables 1 and 2 / Appendix A) cataloging surveyed studies by task, model, language type, and factor investigated, and concludes with recommendations for future work on factor interactions, task-specific effects, and broader multilingual evaluation.

Strengths: Timely and useful survey topic: understanding why multilingual models exhibit cross-lingual transfer is important both scientifically and practically.; Clear organizational structure around five factor categories, which makes the literature easier to navigate.; Good effort to go beyond listing papers by identifying areas of consensus and explicitly discussing contradictions across studies.; Helpful synthesis of nuanced interactions, especially the discussion that lexical overlap may matter more when word order differs or when source-language resources are limited.; The paper includes informative summary tables (e.g., pages 3 and 13) that consolidate prior work by task, model, language type, and factor, increasing the review's utility as a reference.; The discussion section offers concrete future directions, including studying factor interactions, task-specific divergences, and implications for generative models.; The paper is generally well contextualized relative to prior surveys and adjacent work on transfer language selection and adapters.

Weaknesses: As a review paper, the contribution is primarily organizational and interpretive; there is no new empirical experiment or meta-analysis to validate the proposed resolutions of conflicting findings.; The survey methodology is not described rigorously enough: there is no systematic search protocol, inclusion/exclusion criteria, time window, or reproducibility-oriented review procedure.; Many conclusions are qualitative and sometimes speculative, especially when explaining contradictions between studies with different setups.; The paper does not quantify effect sizes or study quality, so stronger and weaker empirical evidence are treated somewhat similarly.; Coverage appears focused on encoder-style multilingual Transformers and zero-shot transfer; broader multilingual paradigms, decoder/generative LLMs, and more recent instruction-tuned models are only briefly mentioned.; Some sections could better distinguish correlation from causation, particularly when discussing feature-importance-based predictor studies as evidence for causal factors.; There are minor writing/presentation issues (e.g., occasional awkward phrasing/typos) that slightly reduce polish.

Soundness Issues: The paper's main claims depend on secondary interpretation of prior studies rather than a systematic review or formal meta-analysis, limiting the strength of its conclusions.; Several conflict-resolution arguments are plausible but not decisively established; they often rely on post hoc reasoning about differences in experimental setup.; Evidence drawn from synthetic-language studies and feature-importance prediction models is informative but may not transfer cleanly to natural-language settings; the paper acknowledges this, but some conclusions still lean on such evidence.; The review does not provide a principled weighting of studies by methodological rigor, dataset comparability, or statistical reliability, which weakens the soundness of aggregate conclusions.; Because the survey spans heterogeneous tasks, models, and evaluation settings, some cross-study comparisons may be less directly comparable than the narrative suggests.
