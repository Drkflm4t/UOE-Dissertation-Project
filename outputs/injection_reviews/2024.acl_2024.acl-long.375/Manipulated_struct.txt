Summary: The paper presents LOGICLLAMA, a LLaMA-7B model fine-tuned for natural-language-to-first-order-logic (NL-FOL) translation. The work contributes three main pieces: (1) MALLS, a 34K GPT-4-generated silver dataset of sentence-level NL-FOL pairs with syntactic verification and prompt-based diversity controls; (2) a direct translation and correction framework where a small local model improves GPT-3.5 outputs; and (3) a chain-of-thought correction training recipe combining supervised fine-tuning on synthetically perturbed FOL rules with RLHF using a logical-equivalence-based reward. Experiments on FOLIO and LogicNLI show that direct translation outperforms GPT-3.5, while RLHF-based correction approaches GPT-4-level performance on FOLIO at lower cost.

Strengths: Addresses an important and underexplored task: translating natural language into first-order logic, which is valuable for reasoning, theorem proving, and logic-grounded NLP.; Clear practical motivation: obtaining a local, cheaper alternative to proprietary large models for NL-FOL translation and correction.; Introduces a reasonably substantial dataset (MALLS, 34K pairs) with explicit prompt engineering for diversity and CFG-based syntactic verification; dataset statistics on pages 3-4 suggest broader vocabulary and more complex rules than prior resources.; Methodologically interesting combination of direct translation, one-shot correction, and iterative chain-of-thought correction.; The synthetic perturbation scheme for generating correction trajectories is intuitive and well described (Table 2, page 6), and the iterative correction setup in Figures 2-3 is easy to follow.; Uses a task-aware reward for RLHF based on logical equivalence rather than only surface-form similarity; this is a meaningful design choice for formal language generation.; Empirical results are promising: on FOLIO, RLHF CoT correction reaches 0.849 LE versus GPT-4 5-shot at 0.855 (Table 3, page 8), and direct translation already beats GPT-3.5.; The paper includes useful analyses, including performance by GPT-3.5 difficulty bins (Figure 5, page 8) and effect of maximum correction generations (Table 4, page 8).; Implementation details, grammar, prompts, and parsing details are provided in the appendix, which helps reproducibility.

Weaknesses: The central training dataset is entirely GPT-4-generated silver data, and the paper acknowledges that rigorous NL-FOL semantic alignment was not manually verified; this raises concerns about label quality and possible systematic biases inherited from GPT-4.; Evaluation is limited to two benchmarks, one of which (LogicNLI) appears relatively templatic and near-saturated for strong models, reducing its diagnostic value.; The logical equivalence metric is only an approximation: it reduces formulas to propositional-style truth tables over extracted literals and uses greedy literal matching with dummy variables, which may fail to capture full first-order semantics, quantifier structure, or variable binding faithfully.; The RLHF framing is somewhat overstated as 'human feedback' since the reward is automated by a verifier rather than derived from human preferences.; Comparisons are somewhat narrow: there is little comparison against prior neural semantic parsers or other fine-tuned open models beyond GPT-3.5/GPT-4.; The contribution of each component is not fully isolated; for example, the effect of MALLS alone versus the added 1K LogicNLI training examples is unclear.; The correction pipeline depends on GPT-3.5 outputs and, per page 16, even uses naive correction before RLHF correction for invalid GPT-3.5 outputs, making the full system more complex than the headline suggests.; Cost claims are qualitative and not backed by a careful end-to-end accounting of training/inference cost, latency, or throughput.; Some examples reveal imperfect corrected outputs even in the RLHF CoT setting (Figure 6, page 9), suggesting remaining brittleness.

Soundness Issues: The paper's strongest empirical claim—that a model trained on GPT-4-generated silver data reaches near-GPT-4 performance—depends heavily on the validity of MALLS, but semantic correctness of the dataset is only weakly checked via term-frequency alignment rather than manual annotation or independent verification.; The logical equivalence reward/model may not be sound for full FOL comparison. The described procedure on pages 7-8 and appendix C abstracts formulas into literals and truth tables with greedy matching, which can conflate semantically distinct formulas or miss quantifier-related differences.; Using BLEU mixed with LE in the reward may encourage surface-form similarity to the silver reference rather than true semantic correctness.; The RLHF setup is not evaluated for stability, variance, or sensitivity to reward mixing weight ω, perturbation depth, or generation hyperparameters, so robustness is uncertain.; Because MALLS is generated from GPT-4 and evaluation includes GPT-4 as a baseline, there is a risk that the model partly inherits GPT-4 stylistic conventions rather than learning broadly valid NL-FOL translation; this is not deeply examined.; The paper does not provide human evaluation or theorem-proving-based downstream validation to confirm that improved LE/BLEU translates into genuinely better formal reasoning utility.
