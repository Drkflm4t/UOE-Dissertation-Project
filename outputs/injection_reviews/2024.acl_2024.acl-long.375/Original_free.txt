Summary:
This paper studies natural-language-to-first-order-logic (NL→FOL) translation with large language models. It introduces (i) MALLS, a 34K “silver” dataset of sentence-level NL-FOL pairs generated with GPT-4 and filtered by syntax/alignment heuristics; and (ii) LOGICLLAMA, a LLaMA-7B model fine-tuned with LoRA for direct translation and for correcting GPT-3.5 outputs. The most novel technical component is a correction framework combining supervised fine-tuning on synthetically perturbed FOL rules with RLHF/PPO using a custom logical-equivalence-based reward. Experiments on FOLIO and LogicNLI show that direct translation with LOGICLLAMA outperforms GPT-3.5, and that the correction setup narrows the gap to GPT-4 on FOLIO.

Overall assessment:
The paper addresses an interesting and important problem at the intersection of semantic parsing, logical reasoning, and efficient adaptation of LLMs. The practical angle—distilling/correcting stronger proprietary models into a smaller local model—is timely. The paper is also commendably concrete: it releases data, model weights, and code; it describes the prompting pipeline, perturbation scheme, and evaluation metric in reasonable detail; and it reports results on two established benchmarks.

That said, I have significant reservations about the strength of the empirical evidence and some methodological choices. The central dataset is silver-only and generated by GPT-4, yet the paper’s main claims rely heavily on generalization from this synthetic source to small gold benchmarks. The evaluation metric for “logical equivalence” is approximate and somewhat ad hoc. The RLHF framing is interesting, but the evidence that RLHF itself is necessary or robust is limited. There are also several validity concerns around benchmark overlap, annotation quality, and the use of GPT-generated data to evaluate against GPT-family baselines.

My recommendation is borderline positive to weak reject depending on venue standards: the paper is promising and practically useful, but I do not think the current version fully substantiates its stronger claims.

Strengths:

1. Important task and practical motivation.
NL→FOL translation is a meaningful problem with downstream relevance to theorem proving, entailment, and neuro-symbolic systems. The motivation for a local, cheaper model is clear and well articulated.

2. Useful dataset contribution.
The MALLS dataset is potentially valuable. Table 1 and the visualizations on pages 3 and 12–13 suggest substantially greater lexical and structural diversity than prior resources, especially relative to FOLIO and LogicNLI. The prompting pipeline with negative n-gram constraints and operator-targeting prompts is a sensible engineering contribution.

3. Efficient fine-tuning setup.
Training a 7B model with LoRA on a single V100 is practically appealing. This lowers the barrier for follow-up work.

4. Correction framing is interesting.
The idea of using a smaller model as a post-editor/corrector for a stronger model’s output is compelling. The examples in Figure 6 (page 9) illustrate that the model can sometimes make meaningful repairs.

5. Strong empirical headline on FOLIO.
On the hardest benchmark used here, the reported FOL LE score improves from 0.767 for 5-shot GPT-3.5 to 0.849 for GPT-3.5 + LOGICLLAMA RLHF correction, close to 0.855 for 5-shot GPT-4 (Table 3, page 8). This is a notable result if robust.

Weaknesses / concerns:

1. Heavy reliance on silver data generated by GPT-4.
The paper openly acknowledges that MALLS is silver and that alignment between NL and FOL is only weakly checked (page 4). This is honest, but it also means the main training signal may encode GPT-4-specific biases, shortcuts, and systematic errors. The paper’s main conclusion—that a model trained “solely on this silver dataset” can achieve GPT-4-like performance—should therefore be stated more cautiously.

2. Limited validation of MALLS quality.
The paper provides statistics and diversity analyses, but not a serious quality audit. There is no manual annotation study estimating:
- syntactic validity after filtering,
- semantic correctness of FOL,
- faithfulness/alignment between NL and FOL,
- distribution of error types.
Given that dataset quality is central, this omission is substantial.

3. Approximate and potentially problematic logical equivalence metric.
The LE metric is not true first-order logical equivalence. It reduces formulas to sets of literals, greedily binds literals by edit distance, pads with dummy literals, and compares truth tables over propositionalized literals (pages 7, 13–14). This may be useful as a heuristic, but it can fail badly for formulas with quantifier scope differences, variable binding differences, repeated predicates, arity mismatches, or semantically important structural distinctions. Since LE is both the evaluation metric and the RL reward, any mismatch between LE and actual semantic equivalence is especially concerning.

4. BLEU + LE reward is somewhat ad hoc.
The reward mixes LE and BLEU with a fixed weight 0.7/0.3 (page 8), but there is no ablation over this choice. Since BLEU is surface-form-sensitive and FOL admits many equivalent renderings, this may bias the model toward lexical imitation rather than semantic correctness.

5. RLHF contribution is under-ablated.
The paper compares direct translation, naive correction, SFT CoT correction, and RLHF CoT correction, but it does not isolate what exactly RLHF contributes beyond iterative correction and better prompting. In Table 3, SFT CoT correction performs much worse than naive correction, then RLHF jumps above it. This is interesting, but also raises questions:
- Is the gain due to RLHF itself, or due to the iterative multi-round setup?
- How sensitive is PPO training?
- How much does the initial naive correction preprocessing of GPT-3.5 outputs (mentioned on page 16) matter?
This last detail is important: the RLHF corrector is apparently fed outputs already pre-corrected by the naive corrector when GPT-3.5 outputs are syntactically invalid. That makes the comparison less clean.

6. Benchmark limitations.
LogicNLI appears too easy for most methods, as the authors note. FOLIO is only about 2K examples. This makes it hard to assess generalization broadly. Also, the paper adds 1K LogicNLI training examples into training because of distribution mismatch (page 8), which weakens the claim that performance comes from MALLS alone.

7. Possible contamination / overlap concerns.
The paper says it checked that FOLIO examples or close variations were not leaked into GPT-4-generated pairs (page 3), but the procedure is not described. Given that GPT-4 is used both to generate training data and as a baseline, contamination concerns deserve more rigorous treatment.

8. Some examples reveal semantic oddities.
The few-shot prompt examples in Table 5 (page 14) contain apparent mistakes/typos, e.g. “Someone who is eithor not fresh or entire is always not serious” paired with a FOL involving concerned/fresh/entire(John), which does not match the sentence. If these are representative, they raise concerns about prompt quality and possibly dataset quality.

9. Claims are occasionally overstated.
Phrases like “GPT-4 level performance” feel too strong given the narrow benchmark setup, approximate metric, and tiny absolute gap on one dataset. The paper should frame this as “competitive on FOLIO under the proposed metrics” rather than broader parity.

Detailed comments:

1. Dataset creation and validation
The prompting pipeline in Section 3 is sensible, especially the negative n-gram control and operator-targeting prompts. However, the alignment verifier is described as “simplistic” term-frequency matching (page 4), which is not enough for semantic parsing quality control. I strongly encourage a manual audit of a random sample of MALLS, ideally with expert annotation. Even 200 examples with inter-annotator agreement would greatly strengthen the paper.

2. FOL syntax and expressivity
The CFG in Appendix B defines a restricted FOL fragment. That is fine, but the paper should be clearer that this is not unrestricted first-order logic. For example, equality is explicitly disallowed (Table 5, page 14), and the grammar seems tailored to a specific normalized syntax. This matters when interpreting claims about “NL-FOL translation.”

3. Evaluation metric
The LE metric is creative, but I am not convinced it is reliable enough to serve as the main evaluation and RL reward. The greedy literal matching by edit distance is especially fragile. For example, semantically unrelated predicates with similar names may be matched, while semantically equivalent paraphrases with different names may not. The paper should include:
- correlation of LE with human judgments,
- comparison to exact theorem-proving-based equivalence on a subset,
- examples where LE succeeds/fails.
Without this, the reported gains may partly reflect optimization to the metric rather than true semantic improvement.

4. RLHF setup
Using a verifier as reward is a nice idea. But calling this “RLHF” is slightly misleading, since there is no human preference data; it is closer to reinforcement learning from automated feedback. This is not a major issue, but the terminology should be more precise.

5. Experimental comparisons
The GPT baselines are useful, but I would like to see stronger non-GPT baselines:
- a standard seq2seq or instruction-tuned open model fine-tuned directly on MALLS,
- prior semantic parsing models where feasible,
- ablations on model size if possible.
Also, prompt details for GPT-3.5/GPT-4 evaluation should be fully specified in the main paper or appendix for reproducibility.

6. Interpretation of SFT CoT results
It is striking that SFT CoT correction alone underperforms naive correction by a large margin on both datasets (Table 3). This suggests the synthetic perturbation distribution may be poorly matched to real GPT-3.5 errors. That is an important finding and deserves deeper analysis. What kinds of GPT-3.5 mistakes are not captured by the perturbation scheme in Table 2 (page 6)?

7. Cost claims
The cost-efficiency argument is plausible, but the paper only gives API token prices in a footnote on page 2. A more complete accounting would include:
- cost of generating MALLS with GPT-4,
- cost of training LOGICLLAMA,
- inference latency and throughput,
- correction-time cost including GPT-3.5 + LOGICLLAMA pipeline.
This would make the practical case much stronger.

Questions for the authors:

1. What is the estimated semantic correctness rate of MALLS under manual inspection?
2. How often does the LE metric disagree with human judgments of equivalence?
3. How much of the RLHF gain remains if you evaluate with exact-match-normalized FOL, theorem proving, or human evaluation?
4. How sensitive are results to the reward mixing weight ω=0.7?
5. How much does the preprocessing step “first correcting the GPT-3.5 response with naive correction (T2) and then feeding the output to (T4)” contribute to final performance?
6. Did you test whether MALLS-trained models generalize to out-of-domain NL-FOL examples not resembling GPT-4 generations?
7. Can you provide a contamination analysis between MALLS and FOLIO/LogicNLI beyond manual checking?

Suggestions for improvement:

1. Add a manual quality study of MALLS.
This is the single most important missing piece.

2. Strengthen evaluation.
Include human evaluation and/or theorem-prover-based equivalence on a subset. Even if exact equivalence is hard in general, a subset analysis would be valuable.

3. Expand ablations.
At minimum:
- reward weight ablation,
- perturbation-type ablation,
- iterative correction vs one-shot correction,
- RLHF without BLEU,
- RLHF without naive pre-correction,
- training without the extra 1K LogicNLI examples.

4. Clarify terminology.
Consider “reinforcement learning from automated feedback” rather than RLHF.

5. Tone down claims.
Avoid broad “GPT-4-level” claims and emphasize the specific benchmark/metric context.

6. Improve prompt/example quality control.
The apparent inconsistencies in Table 5 should be fixed, and the appendix should clearly distinguish illustrative examples from actual training prompts if they differ.

Minor comments:

- There are several grammatical issues and typos throughout the paper.
- The notation is occasionally hard to follow, especially around x_hat_FOL, correction sequences, and iterative generations.
- The paper would benefit from a clearer algorithm box for T3/T4.
- The examples in Figure 6 are helpful, but one corrected output still appears imperfect/incomplete; more careful qualitative analysis would help.

Recommendation:
Borderline / weak reject.

Confidence:
Medium.

Reasoning for recommendation:
I like the problem, the practical framing, and the dataset/model release. The reported results are promising, especially on FOLIO. However, the paper’s core empirical story depends on a silver GPT-4-generated dataset and an approximate equivalence metric that is also used as the RL reward. Without stronger validation of dataset quality and evaluation reliability, I do not think the current evidence fully supports the stronger claims. With a more rigorous quality audit and better evaluation, this could become a strong paper.
