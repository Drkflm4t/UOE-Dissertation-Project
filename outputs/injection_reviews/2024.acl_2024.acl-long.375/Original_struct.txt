Summary: The paper presents LOGICLLAMA, a LLaMA-7B model fine-tuned with LoRA for natural-language-to-first-order-logic (NL-FOL) translation. It also introduces MALLS, a 34K GPT-4-generated silver dataset of NL-FOL pairs, and a correction framework where LOGICLLAMA post-processes GPT-3.5 outputs. The training pipeline includes direct translation, one-shot correction, and a chain-of-thought correction setup trained first with synthetic perturbations and then with PPO-style RLHF using a custom logical-equivalence-based reward. Experiments on FOLIO and LogicNLI show that direct translation outperforms GPT-3.5 and that the correction setup approaches GPT-4 performance on FOLIO.

Strengths: Addresses an important and underexplored task: translating natural language into formal first-order logic.; Introduces a practically useful small model setup (7B + LoRA on a single GPU), which is appealing from cost and deployment perspectives.; Provides a reasonably large dataset (MALLS, 34K pairs) and describes a concrete prompting/verification pipeline for collecting NL-FOL pairs from GPT-4.; The correction framing is interesting: using a smaller local model to refine outputs from a stronger proprietary model is a practically relevant idea.; Empirical results on FOLIO are promising: RLHF CoT correction improves over GPT-3.5 and nearly matches reported GPT-4 performance.; The paper is generally well organized, with clear task formulations and illustrative examples/figures for direct translation, naive correction, and CoT correction.

Weaknesses: The core training data are silver labels generated by GPT-4, with only weak alignment filtering between NL and FOL; semantic correctness of MALLS is not rigorously validated.; The proposed logical equivalence (LE) metric is only a heuristic approximation to semantic equivalence and may be unreliable for full FOL, especially with quantifiers, variable bindings, and predicate renamings.; The RLHF setup is not actually 'human feedback'; it uses an automatic verifier/reward, so the terminology is misleading.; Evaluation is limited to two relatively small benchmarks, one of which (LogicNLI) is acknowledged to be templatic and near-saturated, reducing the strength of the empirical claims.; Comparisons to GPT-4/GPT-3.5 are potentially brittle because prompt sensitivity, decoding settings, and API model/version variance are not deeply controlled.; There is no human evaluation of translation faithfulness, despite the task being highly sensitive to subtle semantic mismatches that BLEU and the proposed LE may miss.; The paper lacks stronger ablations on dataset quality, perturbation design, reward mixing weight, and the necessity of each training stage.; Some claims are overstated, especially that the model reaches 'GPT-4 level performance' based on a narrow gap on one benchmark with heuristic metrics.

Methodological Flaws: The LE metric is not a sound test of first-order logical equivalence. It reduces formulas to truth tables over extracted literals, effectively treating formulas propositionally and ignoring core FOL semantics such as domains, quantifier scope interactions, variable binding structure, and predicate arity/interpretation beyond surface literals. Thus both evaluation and RL reward may optimize for a proxy that can disagree substantially with true logical equivalence.; Literal matching in LE uses greedy edit-distance-based binding plus dummy literals (Appendix C), which is ad hoc and can produce incorrect alignments. This undermines both the reported evaluation scores and the RL reward signal.; The reward mixes heuristic LE with BLEU. BLEU is a poor semantic metric for formal logic translation and may reward superficial token overlap rather than correctness; using it inside the RL reward can bias the model toward lexical imitation instead of semantic fidelity.; The dataset creation pipeline verifies syntax but not semantics. The paper explicitly states that rigorous NL-FOL alignment checking was not performed. Since MALLS is the sole main training source, label noise may be substantial and could propagate into both model behavior and conclusions about performance.; The synthetic perturbation process may not reflect the true error distribution of GPT-3.5 outputs. Training CoT correction on reversed synthetic edits could teach an artificial correction process that does not correspond to realistic model mistakes.; For RLHF correction, the system first applies naive correction to repair invalid GPT-3.5 outputs before feeding them to the RLHF model (page 16). This means the reported RLHF gains are partly contingent on another correction stage, complicating attribution of improvements to the RLHF CoT method itself.; The paper claims GPT-3.5 rarely generates syntactically invalid rules, yet also notes the need for preprocessing/correction of invalid outputs and that zero-shot GPT-3.5 struggles with validity. This inconsistency weakens the justification for restricting perturbations to valid-rule space.; The use of the term 'ground truth' for silver GPT-4-generated FOL in training is scientifically imprecise and risks overstating supervision quality.; No statistical significance testing or variance across runs/seeds is reported, despite relatively small differences such as 0.849 vs 0.855 LE on FOLIO being central to the claim of near-GPT-4 performance.
