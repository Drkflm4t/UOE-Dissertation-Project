Summary: The paper presents LOGICLLAMA, a LLaMA-7B model fine-tuned with LoRA for natural-language-to-first-order-logic (NL-FOL) translation. It also introduces MALLS, a 34K GPT-4-generated silver dataset of sentence-level NL-FOL pairs, created with a prompt pipeline that encourages diversity and syntactic validity. Beyond direct translation, the paper proposes a correction setting where LOGICLLAMA edits GPT-3.5 outputs, including a chain-of-thought correction framework trained via supervised fine-tuning on synthetically perturbed FOL rules and RLHF using a logical-equivalence-based reward. On LogicNLI and FOLIO, direct translation outperforms GPT-3.5, and the RLHF correction mode approaches GPT-4-level performance on FOLIO at lower cost.

Strengths: Addresses an important and underexplored task: translating natural language into formal first-order logic, which is valuable for reasoning and theorem-proving pipelines.; Introduces a practically useful small-model approach: a 7B model fine-tuned with LoRA on a single GPU, making the method relatively accessible.; The MALLS dataset is a meaningful contribution in scale and diversity compared with prior NL-FOL resources; Table 1 and the term-pair visualizations suggest broader lexical and structural coverage than existing datasets.; The correction framing is novel and compelling: instead of only direct translation, the model is trained to repair stronger LLM outputs, which is practically relevant.; The SFT+RLHF pipeline is creative: synthetic perturbations provide supervision for intermediate correction steps, and RLHF uses a task-specific verifier rather than human preference labels.; Empirical results are strong: on FOLIO, RLHF CoT correction improves GPT-3.5 outputs substantially and nearly matches GPT-4 5-shot performance; direct translation also beats GPT-3.5.; The paper is generally clear and well organized, with helpful figures illustrating direct translation, naive correction, and iterative CoT correction.

Weaknesses: The core training dataset is entirely GPT-4-generated silver data, and the paper acknowledges that rigorous NL-FOL semantic alignment was not manually verified. This raises concerns about label noise and hidden biases inherited from GPT-4.; Evaluation is limited to two relatively small benchmarks, one of which (LogicNLI) is acknowledged to be templatic and near-saturated, reducing the strength of the generalization claims.; The logical equivalence metric is approximate and heuristic: it relies on literal extraction, greedy binding by edit distance, dummy literals, and truth-table overlap. This may not faithfully capture true FOL equivalence in many cases.; The RLHF setup is not compared against simpler alternatives that might explain gains, such as more supervised data augmentation, self-training, or verifier-guided search without PPO.; The paper lacks ablations on several important design choices, such as perturbation distributions, reward mixing weight, negative-sample ratio, and the contribution of the added 1K LogicNLI training examples.; Claims about cost-effectiveness are plausible but not rigorously quantified end-to-end; the comparison focuses mainly on API token prices rather than total system cost including training and iterative correction.; Some examples suggest the corrected outputs may still be imperfect or only partially normalized, so the practical reliability of the system for downstream formal reasoning remains somewhat unclear.

Soundness Issues: The main methodological risk is dataset validity: syntactic verification is strong, but semantic correctness/alignment of GPT-4-generated NL-FOL pairs is only weakly filtered. Since the model is trained entirely on these silver labels, performance gains may partly reflect imitation of GPT-4 conventions rather than robust semantic parsing.; The proposed logical equivalence reward is not a formal theorem-proving equivalence check. The greedy literal matching and truth-table approximation could assign misleading rewards, especially when predicate renaming, arity differences, quantifier scope, or structurally different but equivalent formulas are involved.; The RLHF objective depends on rewards computed against silver 'ground truth' from MALLS, not gold human annotations. This may reinforce errors in the synthetic corpus.; The experimental protocol includes 1K LogicNLI training examples in addition to MALLS, which slightly muddies the claim that performance comes solely from the silver dataset.; The paper does not report variance, multiple runs, or statistical significance, so it is hard to assess robustness of the reported improvements, especially the relatively small gap between RLHF correction and GPT-4 on FOLIO.; Using naive correction before RLHF correction at inference for syntactically invalid GPT-3.5 outputs introduces an additional pipeline dependency that complicates attribution of gains to the RLHF CoT model alone.
