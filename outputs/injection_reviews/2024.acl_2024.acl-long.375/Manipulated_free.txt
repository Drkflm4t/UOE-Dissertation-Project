Summary

This paper studies natural-language-to-first-order-logic (NL-FOL) translation using large language models. It introduces three main components: (1) MALLS, a 34K GPT-4-generated “silver” dataset of sentence-level NL-FOL pairs; (2) LOGICLLAMA, a LLaMA-7B model fine-tuned with LoRA for direct translation and correction; and (3) a correction framework combining supervised fine-tuning on synthetically perturbed FOL rules with RLHF using a logical-equivalence-based reward. Experiments on FOLIO and LogicNLI show that direct translation with LOGICLLAMA outperforms GPT-3.5, and that the correction mode can improve GPT-3.5 outputs to near GPT-4-level performance on FOLIO.

Overall assessment

This is an interesting and timely paper on a difficult and underexplored task. The paper is ambitious in scope: it contributes a dataset, a model, a training framework, and an evaluation procedure. The empirical results are promising, especially on FOLIO, and the paper is generally well organized. I found the correction framing particularly compelling: rather than only asking a smaller model to directly solve NL-FOL translation, the authors leverage a stronger model for initial translation and then train a local model to iteratively repair outputs. That is a practically useful idea.

At the same time, several aspects of the work need stronger validation. The biggest concern is that the training data are generated by GPT-4 and only lightly filtered for semantic alignment, while evaluation is limited to two benchmarks and relies heavily on a custom logical equivalence metric that appears approximate. Some claims are also stronger than what the evidence fully supports, especially around “GPT-4-level performance,” novelty, and the reliability of the silver dataset. Still, I think the paper has clear merit and could become a valuable contribution with stronger analysis and clearer positioning.

Strengths

1. Important problem
NL-FOL translation is a foundational task for logic-grounded NLP, semantic parsing, and theorem proving. It is also a good stress test for compositional reasoning in LLMs. The paper addresses a meaningful gap between general-purpose LLM prompting and specialized local models.

2. Practical contribution: a local specialized model
The paper shows that a 7B model fine-tuned with LoRA on a single GPU can become competitive on this task. This is practically relevant for privacy, deployment, and cost reasons.

3. Interesting correction paradigm
The correction setup is one of the strongest aspects of the paper. The idea of using GPT-3.5 for the “heavy lifting” and a smaller model for iterative repair is intuitive and useful. The chain-of-thought correction formulation on page 5 / Figure 2 is clear and helps motivate why naive one-shot correction may be insufficient.

4. Dataset contribution
MALLS is potentially useful to the community. The paper provides a reasonably detailed description of the prompting pipeline, including n-gram suppression, prompt diversification, operator control, and CFG-based syntax verification (pages 3–4 and Appendix B). The statistics in Table 1 suggest MALLS is larger and structurally richer than prior sentence-level NL-FOL resources.

5. Strong empirical results on FOLIO
The main result in Table 3 is notable: RLHF CoT correction reaches 0.849 LE on FOLIO versus 0.855 for 5-shot GPT-4, while direct translation already beats GPT-3.5. This is a meaningful result if robust.

6. Clear presentation of the training pipeline
The paper does a good job explaining the progression from direct translation to naive correction to CoT correction with SFT and RLHF. Figure 3 on page 6 is especially helpful.

Weaknesses and concerns

1. Heavy reliance on GPT-4-generated silver data with weak semantic validation
This is the central concern. The paper acknowledges that MALLS uses only a “simplistic verifier” for NL-FOL alignment based on term frequency overlap (page 4), and that no rigorous semantic alignment check is performed. This leaves open the possibility that many training pairs are semantically incorrect, underspecified, or stylistically biased toward GPT-4’s preferred formalization patterns.

Because the model is trained almost entirely on GPT-4-generated data, it may be learning to imitate GPT-4’s idiosyncratic translation style rather than learning robust NL-FOL semantics. The fact that evaluation is on only two datasets, one of which is synthetic and templatic (LogicNLI), makes it hard to assess generalization.

2. Evaluation metric for logical equivalence seems approximate and potentially problematic
The proposed LE metric is clever, but it is not true first-order logical equivalence. It reduces formulas to truth tables over extracted literals and then greedily aligns literals, padding with dummy variables when needed (pages 7, 13–14). This raises several issues:

- Truth-table comparison over literals ignores the semantics of quantification beyond surface structure.
- Greedy literal matching by edit distance may fail badly when semantically equivalent predicates have different names.
- Best-case matching may overestimate equivalence.
- Dummy padding may distort scores.
- The metric may reward structurally similar but semantically wrong formulas.

The paper should provide stronger validation that LE correlates with human judgments or theorem-prover-based equivalence on a manually annotated subset.

3. “RLHF” terminology is somewhat misleading
The method uses PPO with a programmatic reward from a logical equivalence solver, not human preference data. This is closer to reinforcement learning from automated feedback than RLHF in the usual sense. The paper should be more precise in naming this component.

4. Limited baselines
The comparisons are mostly against GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 prompting. That is useful, but insufficient. There is little comparison to prior neural semantic parsing / NL-FOL systems, even if older systems use different datasets. At minimum, the paper should discuss why direct comparison is impossible or include adapted baselines where feasible.

Also, for the correction setting, a stronger baseline would be:
- self-correction by GPT-3.5,
- GPT-3.5 with iterative prompting,
- GPT-4 correcting GPT-3.5 outputs,
- a non-RL iterative correction model trained only with synthetic perturbations,
- constrained decoding or grammar-aware decoding baselines.

5. Potential contamination / circularity concerns
The paper says few-shot examples initially come from FOLIO and later from GPT-4-generated pairs, and that the authors checked to ensure no FOLIO examples or close variations leaked into MALLS (page 3). But the procedure is not described in enough detail. Since GPT-4 is used both to generate training data and as a test-time baseline, there is a risk that the comparison partly reflects stylistic consistency rather than independent generalization.

6. Claims of novelty are overstated
The conclusion says “the first specialized LM for the NL-FOL translation task.” That seems too strong without a more careful survey and definition of “specialized LM.” There has been prior neural work on parsing natural language into logic, and the novelty here is more specifically the LLaMA fine-tuning plus correction/RL framework and the GPT-4-generated dataset.

7. LogicNLI is not a very convincing benchmark here
The paper itself notes that LogicNLI is synthetic and template-like, and nearly all methods perform extremely well there. This limits its value as evidence. The stronger story is on FOLIO, but then the evaluation should go deeper on FOLIO or include additional expert-written benchmarks.

8. Some examples reveal lingering semantic issues
The examples in Figure 6 on page 9 are illustrative, but they also show that even the corrected outputs may remain imperfect or stylistically inconsistent with the reference. For instance, predicate naming and argument structure vary, and some outputs appear only partially corrected. This reinforces the need for human evaluation or theorem-prover-based downstream validation.

9. Cost claims are underdeveloped
The paper emphasizes cost efficiency relative to GPT-4, but the comparison is incomplete. It would be useful to report:
- training cost for MALLS generation,
- fine-tuning cost,
- inference latency and cost for direct translation and correction,
- total cost per corrected sample including GPT-3.5 plus LOGICLLAMA,
- comparison against simply using GPT-4 directly.

Detailed comments

1. Dataset quality
Table 1 on page 3 is useful and suggests MALLS is more diverse than LogicNLI and larger than FOLIO. The vocabulary size and average number of literals are encouraging. However, diversity is not the same as correctness. The Sankey-style term-pair visualization on page 3 / Figure 1 and the larger version in Appendix Figure 7 show broad topical spread, but they do not establish semantic fidelity.

I would strongly encourage the authors to manually audit a random sample of MALLS and report:
- percentage of syntactically valid pairs,
- percentage of semantically correct alignments,
- common error categories,
- inter-annotator agreement if multiple annotators are used.

Without this, it is hard to know how much noise the model is trained on.

2. Prompting pipeline
The prompting pipeline is thoughtfully designed. The negative n-gram suppression and operator-targeting prompts are sensible. The break-down prompt to discourage overly fused predicates is also a nice practical insight. However, the paper should discuss whether these prompt choices bias the resulting dataset toward unnatural FOL styles or overuse of certain operators.

3. Synthetic perturbation strategy
The perturbation framework in Table 2 and page 6 is a reasonable way to create correction supervision. But it is not obvious that synthetic perturbations resemble real GPT-3.5 errors. The paper partly addresses this by adding RL on actual GPT-3.5 outputs, but I would still like to see an analysis comparing:
- distribution of synthetic perturbation types,
- distribution of actual GPT-3.5 error types,
- overlap between the two.

This would help justify the SFT stage.

4. RL stage
The RL setup is interesting, but under-specified in places. For example:
- How stable is PPO training?
- How many updates / episodes are used?
- What is the KL penalty?
- How sensitive are results to reward mixing weight omega?
- How often does the model exploit weaknesses in the reward metric?

Given the approximate LE reward, reward hacking seems plausible.

5. Metrics
BLEU is not very meaningful for logical forms except as a rough lexical overlap measure. The paper acknowledges this by mixing BLEU with LE in the reward, but then BLEU is still prominently reported in Table 3. I would prefer:
- exact match,
- syntax validity rate,
- theorem-prover-based equivalence where possible,
- human semantic correctness judgments,
- downstream reasoning accuracy using translated formulas.

6. Experimental setup
The inclusion of 1K LogicNLI training pairs into the training set is understandable because of distribution mismatch, but it slightly muddies the claim that the model is trained on MALLS alone. The paper should make this more prominent and ideally report results with and without those 1K examples.

7. Interpretation of results
The main empirical takeaway is credible: correction helps, and CoT correction helps more than naive correction on FOLIO. Table 4 and Figure 5 support this. I especially liked the analysis by GPT-3.5 difficulty bins on page 8; it gives a more nuanced picture than aggregate scores alone.

That said, the “similar performance as GPT-4” claim should be softened. On FOLIO, 0.849 vs 0.855 LE is close, but:
- the metric is approximate,
- the setup is not apples-to-apples because the correction system uses GPT-3.5 plus LOGICLLAMA,
- no significance testing is reported,
- GPT-4 is only evaluated via prompting, not optimized for correction.

Questions for the authors

1. What fraction of MALLS was manually inspected, and what was the estimated semantic correctness rate?
2. How well does the LE metric correlate with human judgments of semantic equivalence?
3. Can you compare LE against a theorem prover or model checker on a subset?
4. How often does GPT-3.5 produce syntactically invalid versus semantically invalid outputs?
5. Why call the method RLHF rather than RL with automated feedback?
6. How sensitive are results to the reward mixing coefficient omega = 0.7?
7. What is the performance if you remove the 1K LogicNLI training examples?
8. How does GPT-3.5 self-correction compare to LOGICLLAMA correction?
9. What is the end-to-end cost per sample for GPT-3.5 + LOGICLLAMA correction versus GPT-4 direct translation?
10. Did you evaluate on any manually curated out-of-domain examples beyond FOLIO?

Suggestions for improvement

1. Add a manual quality evaluation of MALLS.
This is the single most important addition.

2. Validate the LE metric.
Compare it against human judgments and/or theorem-prover-based equivalence on a subset.

3. Strengthen baselines.
Include iterative prompting/self-correction baselines for GPT-3.5 and GPT-4, and if possible prior semantic parsing systems.

4. Clarify terminology.
Rename RLHF to something like RL with logical feedback, or explicitly explain the deviation from standard RLHF usage.

5. Report more ablations.
Useful ablations would include:
- no BLEU in reward,
- no LE in reward,
- no synthetic SFT before RL,
- different perturbation budgets,
- different max correction rounds,
- with/without LogicNLI augmentation.

6. Add human evaluation or downstream reasoning evaluation.
For example, use translated formulas in an entailment or theorem-proving pipeline and measure end-task correctness.

7. Soften claims.
Especially around “GPT-4-level performance,” “first specialized LM,” and the reliability of silver-only training.

Recommendation

Weak Accept / Borderline Accept

Rationale

I think this paper has a real contribution and presents a promising direction. The combination of a GPT-4-generated silver dataset, a small local model, and iterative correction with logic-based rewards is novel enough and practically relevant. The FOLIO results are strong and the paper is generally clear.

However, I also think the current version falls short on validation of dataset quality and evaluation methodology. The custom logical equivalence metric is central to both training and evaluation, yet its faithfulness is not convincingly established. The paper would be substantially stronger with manual audits, stronger baselines, and more careful claims.

If accepted, I would view it as an exciting early step rather than a definitive solution. If rejected, the likely reason would be insufficient rigor around data quality and evaluation rather than lack of interesting ideas.

Confidence

3/5

I am reasonably confident in the main strengths and weaknesses, though some concerns depend on additional details or experiments not included in the paper.
