This paper presents a systematic empirical study of task-specific knowledge distillation for natural language generation under what the authors call a realistic setup: medium-resource labeled data, abundant unlabeled task-specific inputs, off-the-shelf pretrained models, and a strong emphasis on inference-time efficiency. The paper studies multiple design axes, including architecture choice, pruning strategy, KD objective, pseudo-target generation, use of unlabeled data, multiple pseudo-targets, decoding strategy for pseudo-targets, and a new “Joint-Teaching” method that mixes teacher- and student-generated pseudo-targets. Experiments span four English generation tasks—summarization, question generation, abductive reasoning, and Shakespeare-to-modern-English style transfer/simplification—with additional exploratory experiments distilling GPT-4 in a no-labeled-data setting.

Overall, I found the paper strong, practically relevant, and unusually thorough in scope. Its main contribution is not a single radically new algorithmic idea, but rather a careful decomposition of the NLG distillation design space and a set of empirically grounded conclusions that are likely to be useful to practitioners. The paper is especially valuable because it moves beyond the common high-resource, labeled-only setting and demonstrates that pseudo-target training on unlabeled task-specific data can be a major lever for compression in NLG. The proposed Joint-Teaching method is also interesting and reasonably motivated by exposure bias considerations. The paper is generally well written, the experimental pipeline is clearly organized, and the appendix contains substantial implementation detail.

The strongest aspect of the paper is the breadth and coherence of the empirical study. Figure 1 on page 4 clearly lays out the staged research design, and the progression from architecture decisions to pruning, then to KD objectives, and finally to increasingly sophisticated pseudo-target usage is easy to follow. This staged methodology is sensible given the combinatorial size of the design space. The paper also does a good job of grounding its choices in practical deployment concerns, including latency and throughput rather than only parameter count or FLOPs. Table 2 on page 7 and Table 8 in the appendix are particularly useful in this regard. I appreciated that the authors explicitly distinguish latency from throughput and discuss why these can behave differently depending on input/output lengths and hardware utilization.

The empirical findings themselves are meaningful. First, the comparison between encoder-decoder and decoder-only models for conditional NLG fine-tuning is useful and, in my view, one of the more practically important takeaways. The paper argues, with supporting evidence in Table 2 and Appendix B, that encoder-decoder models dominate decoder-only models in this task-specific conditional generation regime, even when the latter are competitive in parameter count. Second, the pruning result—that decoder pruning is preferable to encoder pruning when balancing quality and latency—is intuitive in hindsight but still valuable to document carefully. The analysis in Appendix B.2 and the plot on page 18 help explain why this happens. Third, the pseudo-target results are compelling: unlabeled data helps substantially, multiple pseudo-targets help beyond a single beam output, and sampling-based pseudo-target generation often outperforms beam search. These are concrete, actionable findings.

I also think the paper’s framing of pseudo-target training through the lens of exposure bias is a useful conceptual contribution. The discussion in Sections 3.4 and 3.5 is thoughtful, and Figure 2 on page 5 provides some empirical support for the claim that the teacher can better continue sequences partially generated by the student than the student itself can. This motivates Joint-Teaching in a reasonably convincing way. While the method itself is simple—alternating between teacher- and student-generated pseudo-targets and applying word-level KD—it is exactly the kind of simple intervention that can matter in practice.

That said, I do have several concerns, mostly around experimental methodology and the strength of some claims.

My main concern is the sequential stage-wise model selection protocol. The paper makes one decision at each stage based on average development performance over four tasks, then carries only the selected option forward. This is understandable computationally, but it introduces path dependence and makes it difficult to disentangle interactions between choices. For example, the conclusion that Logits KD is the best base objective is made before the more extensive pseudo-target experiments, but it is plausible that another objective might interact better with multiple sampled pseudo-targets or with unlabeled data. Similarly, the choice of one architecture family and one pruning strategy may bias downstream conclusions. The authors acknowledge that exhaustive combinatorial exploration is too expensive, but the paper would be stronger if it more explicitly framed its conclusions as conditional on this greedy search procedure. As written, some conclusions sound broader than the evidence fully supports.

Relatedly, the aggregation methodology in Table 3 deserves more scrutiny. The paper reports “fraction of the student-teacher gap closed,” averaged over metrics and student-teacher pairs, plus win counts over 32 setups. This is a reasonable normalization, but it can also obscure absolute effect sizes and metric-specific behavior. In particular, averaging across BLEU, ROUGE, BERTScore, and perplexity may mix metrics with different sensitivities and meanings, especially across tasks as different as summarization and abductive reasoning. The paper partly mitigates this by providing full tables in the appendix, but the main text leans heavily on the aggregated summaries. I would have liked more discussion of variance across seeds or runs as well. The extreme setup section explicitly notes high variability across learning rates and initializations, but the main realistic setup does not report confidence intervals or standard deviations. Given that many improvements in Table 3 are incremental, uncertainty estimates would help assess robustness.

A second concern is that the novelty of the proposed methods is moderate. The paper’s strongest contribution is systematic evaluation rather than fundamentally new distillation machinery. Joint-Teaching is a nice idea, but it is also fairly close in spirit to using student roll-ins with teacher supervision, which has analogues in imitation learning and exposure-bias mitigation. The paper does not position this connection as deeply as it could. I do not view this as fatal—the empirical study itself is valuable—but it does affect how I assess the paper relative to a top-tier ML venue’s novelty bar.

A third concern is the limited scope of tasks and languages. The paper studies four English-to-English tasks, all with relatively short outputs compared to many generation settings. The authors are transparent about this in the limitations section on page 10, but it still matters. Some of the strongest claims—especially about encoder-decoder superiority, pseudo-target sampling, and decoder pruning—may not transfer cleanly to machine translation, long-form generation, dialogue, code generation, multilingual tasks, or multimodal generation. Since the paper often uses broad phrasing like “for NLG,” I think the claims should be more carefully scoped to conditional English generation with short-to-medium outputs and medium-resource task-specific fine-tuning.

I also have some concerns about the extreme GPT-4 setup. I appreciate the attempt to test transferability of the conclusions, but the section feels somewhat underdeveloped relative to the rest of the paper. The tokenizer alignment procedure described in Appendix C is clever, but also quite brittle. In particular, assigning probability one when a student token does not match any of the teacher’s top-5 tokens seems like a very rough approximation that may distort the KD signal substantially. The paper itself attributes mixed results to tokenizer alignment issues, which is plausible. Because of this, I would treat the GPT-4 experiments as exploratory rather than confirmatory. The paper mostly does so, but the abstract and introduction might lead some readers to expect stronger evidence here than is actually provided.

Another methodological issue is the lack of training-cost accounting. The paper explicitly prioritizes inference-time efficiency and argues that one-time training cost is negligible in many deployment settings. That is a fair perspective, but some of the proposed methods—especially generating many pseudo-targets for labeled and unlabeled data, and Joint-Teaching with on-the-fly student generation—can be expensive. Since one of the paper’s themes is practical guidance, it would be useful to quantify at least approximate wall-clock or GPU-hour costs for the main methods. Without this, it is hard to judge the tradeoff between the gains from, say, multiple sampled pseudo-targets and the additional offline cost. The limitations section acknowledges this, but some quantitative reporting would still improve the paper.

On evaluation, I think the paper is mostly solid, but there are a few weaknesses. Automatic metrics are standard, and the inclusion of BERTScore and perplexity is helpful. However, for tasks like abductive reasoning, the paper itself notes that automatic metrics may underestimate quality. The human evaluation in Appendix F is therefore welcome, but it is quite small: 50 examples, 2 annotators, and only for one dataset and one final comparison. Given that ART10 is the task where the automatic results look weakest, this helps, but a somewhat broader human evaluation would strengthen the paper’s claims about practical quality preservation. Also, the paper reports that in some cases the distilled student outperforms the teacher, especially on Shake7. That is interesting, but it would benefit from more analysis. Is this due to regularization, metric mismatch, or noise in teacher outputs? The paper mentions it but does not investigate.

I have a few more specific comments and questions.

The exposure-bias argument is plausible, but Figure 2 on page 5 is based on a single setup (T5-S/T5-L on SQuAD17). Since this figure plays an important motivational role for Joint-Teaching, it would be stronger if the paper showed that the pattern generalizes across at least one additional task or model pair.

The comparison between beam search and sampling for pseudo-target generation is one of the paper’s more interesting findings. However, the implementation choice in Appendix A/D—using one pseudo-target per epoch even when multiple pseudo-targets are available—means the comparison is partly about curriculum/exposure scheduling rather than purely about target set quality. That choice is reasonable for fairness, but it should be emphasized more clearly in the main text because it affects interpretation.

The paper uses two student-teacher pairs in the KD stages: T5-S/T5-L and BART-6:2/BART-L. This is better than a single pair, but still somewhat narrow. Since one of the paper’s conclusions is that encoder-decoder students are preferable, it would be useful to know whether the pseudo-target findings are robust across more student scales or across cross-family distillation settings within the realistic setup.

The paper’s claim that this is the first work to discuss exposure bias in KD for NLG may be directionally true, but it should be stated carefully. There is a broad literature on imitation learning, scheduled sampling, and sequence-level training that touches related ideas. The paper should make sure the novelty claim is specifically about this framing in task-specific NLG distillation.

The writing is generally clear, but there are a few places where the presentation could be tightened. Some terminology shifts between “PT training,” “sequence-level KD,” and “pseudo-target augmentation”; these are related but not always identical, and a slightly sharper distinction would help. Also, the main text occasionally overstates conclusions that are more nuanced in the appendix and limitations.

Despite these concerns, I think the paper makes a meaningful contribution. It addresses an important and underexplored practical regime, provides a substantial empirical study, and offers several useful lessons for compressing NLG systems. The results on unlabeled data and pseudo-target generation are especially valuable. The paper is not primarily a theory paper, and its algorithmic novelty is moderate, but as a rigorous systems-and-methods study for NLG distillation, it is strong.

If accepted, I would encourage the authors to revise the paper along the following lines. First, temper the broadest claims and make clearer that the conclusions are conditional on the staged greedy search and on the specific task regime studied. Second, add uncertainty estimates or at least multi-seed results for the main comparisons in Table 3. Third, discuss more explicitly the offline computational cost of the proposed recipe. Fourth, strengthen the positioning of Joint-Teaching relative to imitation-learning-style roll-in/roll-out ideas. Fifth, if space permits, include one more analysis showing whether the exposure-bias motivation and/or Joint-Teaching gains hold beyond the single setup in Figure 2.

In summary, I view this as a strong empirical paper with high practical relevance, good experimental coverage, and several useful findings, offset by moderate novelty and some methodological limitations in the staged selection protocol and lack of uncertainty reporting. My overall assessment is positive.
