Summary: The paper presents a systematic empirical study of task-specific knowledge distillation for natural language generation under a realistic medium-resource setting with abundant unlabeled data. It compares architectural choices (encoder-decoder vs. decoder-only), pruning strategies, KD objectives, and a family of pseudo-target (PT) augmentation methods across four English NLG tasks: summarization, question generation, abductive reasoning, and style transfer/simplification. The main proposed contribution is an expanded PT training recipe: combining logits KD with PTs, generating PTs for unlabeled data, using multiple PTs, preferring sampling over beam search, and introducing Joint-Teaching, which applies word-level KD on PTs generated by both teacher and student. The paper also explores an extreme no-labeled-data setup using GPT-4 as teacher. Empirically, the study finds that encoder-decoder models are preferable for task-specific conditional NLG, decoder pruning is better than encoder pruning for latency/performance tradeoffs, and PT-based training substantially improves distilled students, often closing a large fraction of the teacher-student gap.

Strengths: The paper addresses an important practical problem: compressing NLG systems for deployment while preserving task performance, with explicit attention to latency and throughput rather than only parameter counts.; The study is unusually systematic: it evaluates a sequence of design decisions across architecture, pruning, KD objective, PT usage, unlabeled data, decoding strategy, and the proposed Joint-Teaching method.; The focus on realistic medium-resource labeled data plus abundant unlabeled data is well motivated and more deployment-relevant than many prior KD studies that assume large labeled datasets.; The pseudo-target perspective is a meaningful contribution. Extending sequence-level KD to multiple PTs, sampled PTs, and unlabeled-data PTs is practically useful and empirically well supported.; Joint-Teaching is a novel and intuitively appealing idea that leverages both teacher- and student-generated PTs to address exposure bias during distillation.; The empirical evaluation spans multiple NLG tasks rather than a single benchmark, improving the breadth of the conclusions.; The paper reports both task metrics and practical efficiency metrics. The computational profiling tables and pruning analysis are valuable for practitioners.; The comparison between encoder-decoder and decoder-only models for task-specific conditional generation is useful and supported by both theoretical discussion and experiments.; The paper includes an extreme setup with GPT-4 as teacher, which broadens the scope and tests transferability of the main findings.; The work is generally transparent: it provides implementation details, ablations, appendices, code, and even a human evaluation for ART10 to probe metric limitations.

Weaknesses: The staged experimental design selects one decision at a time based on development performance, rather than evaluating the full combinatorial interaction space. This is understandable computationally, but it means some conclusions may depend on earlier choices.; Several central claims are framed broadly as a general KD recipe, but the experiments are limited to English-to-English tasks with relatively short outputs and only a small set of model families.; The exposure-bias motivation for PTs and Joint-Teaching is plausible, but the evidence is mostly indirect; the paper does not provide a controlled causal analysis isolating exposure-bias reduction from other augmentation effects.; The extreme GPT-4 setup is interesting but somewhat inconclusive: results are mixed, only two tasks are used, and the tokenizer-alignment procedure for logits KD is heuristic due to limited API access to top-5 token probabilities.; The human evaluation for ART10 is small-scale (50 examples, 2 annotators), so while helpful, it is not strong enough to fully validate the broader empirical conclusions on that task.; Some comparisons may be influenced by unequal model families and pretraining regimes; for example, the encoder-decoder vs. decoder-only conclusion is practically useful but not a perfectly controlled architectural comparison.

Methodological Flaws: The paper's sequential stage-wise selection procedure introduces a risk of path dependence: later conclusions are conditioned on earlier selected choices, so the final recipe is not guaranteed to be globally optimal. This is a methodological limitation rather than a fatal flaw, but it weakens strong causal claims about each component.; The claim that this is the 'first exposure bias perspective for KD' and that PTs mitigate exposure bias is not rigorously established. Figure 2 provides suggestive evidence on one setup, but there is no formal analysis or direct metric of exposure bias reduction across tasks.; The encoder-decoder vs. decoder-only comparison is not fully controlled scientifically. The compared models differ not only in architecture but also in pretraining objective, tokenizer, family, and scaling details, so attributing gains purely to architecture is stronger than the evidence supports.; In the extreme GPT-4 setting, logits KD relies on approximate token alignment and only top-5 teacher token probabilities. Assigning probability 1 when no match exists is a crude approximation that can distort the KD signal, limiting the validity of conclusions about logits KD in that setup.; The use of automatic lexical/semantic metrics for ART10 is acknowledged by the authors as problematic, and the main table-based conclusions on that dataset may therefore understate or mischaracterize actual generation quality. The added human evaluation helps, but the empirical validity for ART10 remains less robust than for the other tasks.
