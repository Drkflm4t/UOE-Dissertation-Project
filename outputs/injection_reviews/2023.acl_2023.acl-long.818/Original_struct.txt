Summary: The paper presents a systematic empirical study of task-specific knowledge distillation (KD) for natural language generation under a realistic medium-resource setting: a few thousand labeled examples plus abundant unlabeled task-specific inputs. Across four English NLG tasks (summarization, question generation, abductive reasoning, and Shakespeare-to-modern-English transfer), the authors compare architectural choices, pruning strategies, KD objectives, and pseudo-target (PT) training variants. Their main proposal is to substantially extend sequence-level KD by generating PTs not only for labeled but also unlabeled data, using multiple PTs per example, preferring sampling over beam search, and introducing Joint-Teaching, which applies word-level KD on PTs generated by both teacher and student to address exposure bias. Results indicate that encoder-decoder models outperform decoder-only models for task-specific conditional NLG, decoder pruning is preferable to encoder pruning for latency/performance tradeoffs, and PT-based training—especially with unlabeled data, multiple sampled PTs, and Joint-Teaching—substantially improves distilled students. The paper also includes an exploratory extreme setup distilling GPT-4 into T5-small without labeled training data, though those results are mixed.

Strengths: The paper addresses an important practical problem: compressing NLG systems for deployment under realistic constraints, including limited labeled data and abundant unlabeled task-specific inputs.; The study is broad and systematic. It evaluates multiple design axes (architecture, pruning, KD objective, PT usage, unlabeled data, number of PTs, decoding strategy, and Joint-Teaching) across four distinct NLG tasks.; The empirical setup is practically motivated and stronger than many prior KD-for-NLG papers that assume large labeled datasets and ignore unlabeled data.; The paper provides useful deployment-oriented measurements beyond parameter count, including FLOPs, latency, throughput, and memory, with clear comparisons in Table 2 and Table 4.; The finding that encoder-decoder models outperform decoder-only models for task-specific conditional NLG is useful and supported by experiments across several model families.; The analysis of pruning is practically valuable: decoder pruning yields much larger latency gains than encoder pruning while preserving quality better.; The PT extensions are well motivated and empirically effective. In particular, using PTs for unlabeled data and multiple sampled PTs appears to produce consistent gains.; Joint-Teaching is an interesting idea that combines teacher- and student-generated PTs to mitigate exposure bias, and it shows strong empirical gains in the reported setup.; The paper is generally transparent about limitations, including training cost, scope of tasks, and the challenges of the GPT-4 extreme setup.; The appendix is extensive and includes implementation details, full tables, complexity discussion, and a human evaluation for ART10.

Weaknesses: The paper's central claims are framed as a 'systematic study,' but the experimental protocol is a greedy stage-wise selection pipeline rather than a factorial or ablation-complete design. This makes interactions between choices hard to assess and weakens causal attribution of gains.; Many conclusions are based on averages over heterogeneous tasks, metrics, and two teacher-student pairs, which can obscure instability or task-specific reversals.; The proposed exposure-bias motivation is plausible but not rigorously established. The evidence is largely heuristic and based on a single illustrative analysis (Figure 2) rather than controlled experiments directly measuring exposure-bias reduction.; Joint-Teaching introduces additional on-the-fly student generation during training, but the paper does not quantify the added training-time cost relative to the gains, despite emphasizing practical deployment.; The extreme GPT-4 setup is interesting but underdeveloped: only two tasks are used, sample sizes are small, results are mixed, and the tokenizer-alignment procedure is approximate.; The evaluation relies heavily on automatic overlap/semantic metrics that are imperfect for open-ended generation tasks, especially abductive reasoning; human evaluation is limited to one dataset and only 50 examples.; The newly constructed Shake7 dataset is potentially useful, but the paper provides limited validation of dataset quality, split construction, and difficulty beyond a brief description.; Some comparisons may not be fully fair because different model families have different pretraining objectives, tokenizers, and optimization behavior; conclusions about architecture may partly conflate architecture with pretraining recipe/model family.; The paper does not report variance across random seeds for the main realistic setup, even though the extreme setup explicitly shows instability across learning rates and initializations.; The use of development-set performance to choose each stage may bias the final recipe toward the selected tasks and metrics, limiting generality.

Methodological Flaws: The stage-wise research design is a methodological weakness for a paper making broad prescriptive claims. Because each decision is selected greedily based on average dev performance and then fixed for later stages, the study cannot disentangle interaction effects. A choice rejected early (e.g., a KD objective) might perform better when combined with later PT strategies, so the final conclusions are not fully supported as globally optimal.; The architecture comparison between encoder-decoder and decoder-only models is not a clean isolation of architecture. The compared systems differ not only in architecture but also in pretraining objective, model family, tokenizer, and possibly optimization sensitivity. Therefore, the claim that encoder-decoder models outperform decoder-only models for task-specific NLG is empirically suggestive but not scientifically isolated.; The exposure-bias argument is not demonstrated with direct evidence. Figure 2 only shows that teacher continuation after a student prefix can improve BLEU in one setup; this does not establish that the downstream gains from PT augmentation or Joint-Teaching arise specifically from exposure-bias mitigation rather than generic data augmentation or regularization.; The paper's main summary statistic—fraction of student-teacher gap closed averaged over metrics and pairs—can be misleading because metrics have different scales and error surfaces, and PPL is directionally opposite to BLEU/ROUGE/BERTScore. Although the authors likely normalize by gap, averaging such heterogeneous quantities may distort practical significance.; The GPT-4 logits KD methodology is scientifically fragile because teacher-student token alignment is approximate and only top-5 teacher token probabilities are available. Assigning probability 1 when no student token match exists is ad hoc and can severely distort the KD target distribution, undermining the validity of conclusions about logits KD in the extreme setup.; The paper emphasizes realistic deployment but omits training-time computational accounting for the main methods. Since PT generation and Joint-Teaching can be expensive, especially with many PTs and unlabeled data, the practical recommendation is incomplete without total distillation cost.; Human evaluation for ART10 is too limited to strongly validate the claim that automatic metrics underestimate KD performance: only 50 examples and two annotators are used, with moderate agreement (Kendall tau 0.52). This is useful evidence but not robust enough for a strong corrective conclusion.; The newly introduced Shake7 dataset may introduce uncontrolled artifacts because it is assembled from multiple sources, but the paper does not provide sufficient analysis of deduplication, domain overlap, or split leakage risks.
