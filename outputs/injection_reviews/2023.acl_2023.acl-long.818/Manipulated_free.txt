Summary:
This paper presents a systematic empirical study of task-specific knowledge distillation (KD) for natural language generation (NLG), focusing on realistic medium-resource settings with limited labeled data and abundant unlabeled task-specific inputs. The authors compare architectural choices (encoder-decoder vs. decoder-only), pruning strategies, several KD objectives, and a family of pseudo-target (PT) augmentation methods. Their main proposal is to substantially extend sequence-level KD by using PTs more aggressively: on labeled and unlabeled data, with multiple PTs per example, generated via sampling rather than only beam search, and with a new Joint-Teaching method that applies word-level KD on PTs generated by both teacher and student. Experiments span four English-to-English NLG tasks (summarization, question generation, abductive reasoning, and Shakespeare-to-modern-English transfer/simplification), with additional exploration of an extreme no-labeled-data setup using GPT-4 as teacher.

Overall assessment:
This is a strong, practically motivated empirical paper with broad scope and several useful findings. Its main value is not a single narrowly defined algorithmic novelty, but rather a careful and fairly comprehensive study of design choices for NLG distillation under realistic deployment constraints. The paper is well organized, the research questions are clear, and the conclusions are actionable. I found the PT-related analysis especially valuable: the paper convincingly argues that prior sequence-level KD practice for NLG is too limited, and shows that unlabeled data, multiple PTs, and sampling-based PT generation can materially improve student quality. The proposed Joint-Teaching method is also interesting and intuitively motivated by exposure bias.

That said, the paper is somewhat ambitious in scope, and some parts are necessarily shallower than others. A few methodological choices could be better justified, and some claims would benefit from stronger controls or broader validation. Still, I think the paper makes a meaningful contribution and would be useful to both researchers and practitioners working on efficient NLG.

Strengths:

1. Strong practical motivation.
The paper is grounded in a realistic deployment scenario: medium-resource labeled data, abundant unlabeled task-specific inputs, off-the-shelf models, and emphasis on inference efficiency. This is a welcome contrast to much prior KD work that either assumes large labeled datasets or focuses on NLU.

2. Broad and systematic empirical scope.
The staged design in Figure 1 (page 4) is a real strength. The paper examines architecture, pruning, KD objectives, PT usage, unlabeled data, number of PTs, decoding strategy, and Joint-Teaching in a coherent progression. Even though the staged approach is not exhaustive, it gives the paper a clear structure and makes the findings easy to interpret.

3. Useful findings on pseudo-target training.
The PT results are the most compelling part of the paper. Table 3 (page 8) shows consistent gains from:
- combining logits KD with PTs rather than using sequence-level KD alone,
- adding unlabeled data,
- using multiple PTs,
- generating PTs with sampling rather than beam search,
- and finally using Joint-Teaching.
This is a substantial extension of standard sequence-level KD practice.

4. Novel perspective connecting KD and exposure bias.
The discussion in Sections 2.2, 3.4, and 3.5 is thoughtful. The paper frames PT augmentation as a way to mitigate exposure bias during distillation, and Figure 2 on page 5 provides a useful motivating analysis. This perspective is intuitive and helps unify the proposed methods.

5. Practical efficiency analysis beyond parameter count.
I appreciated the inclusion of latency and throughput, not just FLOPs or parameter counts. Table 2 (page 7) and Table 8 (page 23) provide practically relevant measurements. The observation that decoder pruning is preferable to encoder pruning for latency-sensitive NLG is useful.

6. Multi-task evaluation.
The paper evaluates on four distinct English generation tasks rather than a single benchmark. This improves confidence that the findings are not entirely task-specific.

7. Honest limitations section.
The paper explicitly discusses the limits of the setup, including the focus on medium-sized fine-tuned teachers, the omission of training-time cost from the main optimization target, and the challenges of using GPT-4 in the extreme setup.

Weaknesses and concerns:

1. The staged experimental design may miss interactions.
The authors acknowledge that they optimize one decision at a time because a full combinatorial search is too expensive. This is understandable, but it means later conclusions depend on earlier selections. For example, the best PT decoding strategy may depend on the KD objective, student architecture, or pruning choice. The paper should be a bit more cautious in presenting some conclusions as general.

2. Limited novelty at the algorithmic level.
The paper’s main contribution is empirical/systematic, plus the Joint-Teaching variant. Many individual ingredients are natural extensions of existing KD practice: using unlabeled data, multiple PTs, sampling, and combining sequence-level and word-level KD. The novelty lies more in putting these together and validating them carefully than in a fundamentally new distillation principle.

3. Some comparisons could be stronger.
For example, the paper compares several KD objectives in stage 3, but the implementation details may matter a lot. Attention-relations KD is adapted from encoder-only settings; its weaker performance here may reflect implementation choices rather than a definitive conclusion. Similarly, Noisy KD is taken from self-distillation for summarization, so its transfer to this broader setup may not be fully optimized.

4. Limited diversity of model families in the KD stages.
Although stage 1 compares ED and DO models, the main KD study proceeds only with ED models and only two student-teacher pairs: T5-S/T5-L and BART-6:2/BART-L. This is reasonable given the findings, but it narrows the generality of the later conclusions. It would be helpful to know whether the PT findings hold for more student scales or other ED families.

5. Evaluation remains mostly automatic.
The paper uses BLEU, ROUGE, BERTScore, and perplexity, which is standard, but these metrics are imperfect for generation quality. The human evaluation for ART10 in Appendix F is helpful, especially since automatic metrics likely underestimate performance there, but it is limited to one dataset and 50 examples. More human evaluation would strengthen the claims.

6. The extreme GPT-4 setup is interesting but underdeveloped.
This section is more exploratory than conclusive. The tokenizer alignment procedure seems brittle, especially since only top-5 token probabilities are available. The mixed results are understandable, but this part feels less mature than the main paper. It is fine as an extension, but should not be overinterpreted.

7. Training cost is not fully accounted for.
The paper explicitly prioritizes inference-time efficiency, which is fair. Still, some of the proposed methods require generating many PTs over labeled and unlabeled data, and Joint-Teaching adds online student PT generation. In practice, these costs may matter, especially for large-scale industrial pipelines. A more quantitative accounting of training-time overhead would improve the practical picture.

Detailed comments:

- On architecture comparison:
The ED vs. DO comparison in Table 2 and Appendix B is useful and likely important for practitioners. The claim that ED models outperform DO models for task-specific conditional NLG is plausible and supported here. Still, the comparison is somewhat constrained by the chosen model families and pretraining recipes. I would phrase the conclusion as applying to the studied off-the-shelf small/medium models rather than as a universal statement.

- On pruning:
The decoder-pruning result is convincing. The complexity discussion in Appendix B.2 and the empirical measurements in Figure 3 (page 18) and Table 8 support the claim well. This is one of the most practically actionable findings in the paper.

- On PT generation:
The move from single beam PTs to multiple sampled PTs is well motivated. I especially liked that the paper does not just propose this intuitively, but shows incremental gains across stages in Table 3. This is the clearest empirical contribution.

- On Joint-Teaching:
This is an appealing idea. The motivation from exposure bias is sensible, and the results in Table 3E are promising. However, I would have liked more ablations:
  - different teacher/student PT mixing ratios instead of fixed alpha = 0.5,
  - curriculum or scheduling over training,
  - whether gains come mainly from regularization/diversity or from explicit exposure-bias mitigation.
The current evidence is good but not fully diagnostic.

- On unlabeled data:
This is an important contribution. The gains from adding PTs on unlabeled inputs are large in Table 3C. Since unlabeled task-specific data is common in practice, this result is highly relevant.

- On datasets:
The four-task setup is a strength. The new Shake7 dataset is potentially useful, though the paper could say more about its construction quality, deduplication, and whether train/dev/test leakage risks were checked when combining sources.

- On reporting:
The paper is generally clear, and the appendix is extensive. I appreciated the full result tables (pages 24-25), the computational profiling table (page 23), and the human evaluation appendix (page 26). The paper is dense, but readable.

Questions for the authors:

1. How sensitive are the PT findings to the amount of unlabeled data? Is the gain roughly monotonic with more unlabeled inputs, or does it saturate quickly?
2. For Joint-Teaching, did you try schedules for alpha rather than a fixed 0.5? For example, more teacher PTs early and more student PTs later?
3. How diverse are the sampled PTs in practice, and how much of the gain comes from diversity versus simply more training instances?
4. Did you observe any degradation in factuality or faithfulness when using sampled PTs instead of beam PTs, especially for summarization?
5. For Shake7, can you provide more detail on data cleaning and split construction to rule out overlap across sources?
6. In the GPT-4 setup, how much do results depend on the prompt demonstrations?

Suggestions for improvement:

1. Add a compact training-cost analysis.
Even a simple table with PT generation time and total KD training time for the main methods would help practitioners assess tradeoffs.

2. Strengthen the Joint-Teaching ablation.
Vary the teacher/student PT ratio and perhaps compare fixed versus scheduled mixing.

3. Clarify the scope of generalization.
Some conclusions are currently phrased broadly; they should be tied more explicitly to the studied setup: medium-resource, English-to-English, task-specific fine-tuning, off-the-shelf small/medium models.

4. Expand discussion of failure cases.
The weaker ART10 automatic results and the mixed GPT-4 findings are interesting. More analysis of when PT-based KD helps less would improve the paper.

5. If space permits, include one or two qualitative examples of sampled PTs versus beam PTs.
This would make the diversity argument more concrete.

Recommendation:
Accept / weak accept.

Confidence:
High.

Reason for recommendation:
This is a solid and useful empirical paper with clear practical relevance, strong experimental coverage, and several actionable findings for NLG distillation. Its main contribution is systematic understanding rather than a radically new algorithm, but that is valuable in its own right. The PT training results, especially with unlabeled data and sampling-based multiple PTs, are convincing and likely to influence future work and practice. The paper has some limitations in generality and depth of certain ablations, but overall I believe it merits acceptance.
