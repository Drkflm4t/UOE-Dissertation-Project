Summary: The paper proposes Masked thought Fine-Tuning (MFT), a very simple modification of supervised fine-tuning for mathematical reasoning: randomly mask or replace a subset of tokens in the chain-of-thought during training while keeping the original target reasoning as supervision. The authors argue that this acts as a regularizer that reduces over-reliance on local context and encourages stronger dependence on the original question and earlier reasoning steps. Empirically, across Llama-2 and Mistral base models and several math-training datasets (GSM8K, MATH, GSM8K-RFT, MetaMathQA, MathInstruct), MFT generally improves over standard SFT, often by 1.5-5.5 points on GSM8K and modestly on MATH. The paper also compares against several alternative noise-injection schemes, studies mask ratio scheduling, reports stronger robustness on GSM-IC distractor benchmarks, and provides analyses suggesting increased long-range dependency on question tokens. Overall, the work is interesting because of its simplicity, broad empirical coverage, and potentially useful regularization perspective for reasoning fine-tuning.

Strengths: The core idea is extremely simple, low-cost, and easy to implement, yet yields consistent gains across multiple datasets and two model families.; Empirical results are fairly broad: the paper evaluates on GSM8K, MATH, GSM-IC, OOD science reasoning datasets, and multiple training corpora including both raw and augmented math data.; The method appears complementary to stronger data-generation pipelines such as RFT/MetaMathQA/MathInstruct, which increases practical relevance.; The ablation study over different noise types and placements is useful and supports the claim that masking/replacing partial reasoning tokens is more effective than several generic regularization baselines.; The robustness improvement on GSM-IC is notable and supports the paper's claim that MFT helps models rely more on relevant premises in the question rather than nearby spurious context.; The paper includes mechanistic analyses (dependency statistics, case studies, error analysis) rather than only reporting benchmark gains, which strengthens the contribution.; The implementation details are reasonably complete, including mask ratios, epochs, datasets, and hardware, aiding reproducibility.

Weaknesses: The comparison to SFT is not perfectly controlled because MFT is often trained for more epochs than SFT (e.g., 10 vs 3 on smaller datasets), making it harder to isolate whether gains come purely from masking rather than additional optimization.; Some of the claimed mechanistic conclusions about 'dependency transfer' are suggestive rather than definitive; the evidence is correlational and based on bespoke perturbation analyses rather than a rigorous causal test.; OOD generalization results are mixed: improvements are small on some settings and negative for MetaMathQA-Mistral on the science benchmarks.; The method is mainly validated on mathematical reasoning; Appendix results indicate little benefit on general instruction tuning, so the scope of applicability is limited.; Several analyses rely on GPT-4 as an evaluator for step correctness and error types, which introduces evaluator bias and some uncertainty.; The paper does not compare against a stronger compute-matched baseline such as SFT trained for the same number of steps/epochs with tuned regularization, which would strengthen the empirical claim.

Methodological Flaws: A key empirical validity concern is the unequal training budget between SFT and MFT. The paper explicitly states that SFT typically uses 3 epochs while MFT uses 5-10 epochs depending on dataset size. Without a compute- or step-matched SFT baseline, part of the reported improvement may be attributable to longer training rather than the masking intervention itself.; The proposed interpretation that MFT improves reasoning by increasing long-distance dependency on questions and earlier steps is not proven. The dependency analysis is based on perturbing numeric tokens and observing output changes, which is an interesting proxy but not a formal measure of causal reasoning structure. Thus the mechanistic claim should be treated as a hypothesis rather than established fact.; The paper informally relates MFT to RL/process supervision (Appendix B), but the equivalence is not mathematically rigorous. Rewriting the objective with a token-level reward indicator does not establish that masked-input supervised learning is a special case of RL in any substantive optimization sense.; The false-positive and error-type analyses use GPT-4 as a judge on relatively small samples (e.g., 200 or 300 examples). This is acceptable as auxiliary evidence, but it is not strong enough to support firm conclusions about intermediate-step correctness or error distributions.; Some robustness claims may be overstated relative to the evidence: while GSM-IC gains are strong, cross-domain science reasoning improvements are inconsistent, including a negative average result in one setting. This weakens any broad claim of general reasoning enhancement beyond math fine-tuning.
