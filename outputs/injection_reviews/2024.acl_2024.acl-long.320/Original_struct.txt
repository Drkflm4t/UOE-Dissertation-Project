Summary: The paper proposes Masked thought Fine-Tuning (MFT), a simple modification of supervised fine-tuning for mathematical reasoning: randomly mask or replace a subset of tokens in the chain-of-thought during training while keeping the original target reasoning sequence as labels. The authors motivate MFT as a regularization/noise-injection method that encourages models to rely less on short-range local continuation patterns and more on the question and earlier reasoning steps. Empirically, across Llama-2 and Mistral base models and several math training datasets (GSM8K, MATH, GSM8K-RFT, MathInstruct, MetaMathQA), MFT generally improves over standard SFT, with especially strong gains on GSM8K and the distractor-heavy GSM-IC benchmark. The paper also compares MFT to other noise/regularization methods, presents dependency analyses and case studies suggesting increased long-distance dependence, and argues that MFT is complementary to explicit data augmentation methods such as RFT and GPT-generated math instruction datasets.

Strengths: The method is extremely simple, low-cost, and easy to implement within standard SFT pipelines, which is practically valuable.; Empirical gains over SFT are fairly consistent across multiple datasets and two model families, especially on GSM8K and GSM-IC.; The paper includes useful ablations over noise types and masking strategies; Table 5 is informative and helps isolate which perturbations are effective.; The comparison to RFT is interesting: MFT achieves comparable gains without requiring additional sampled data, and combining MFT with RFT further helps.; The distractor robustness result on GSM-IC is a notable strength, suggesting the method may improve reliance on relevant premises rather than superficial local continuation.; The paper attempts mechanistic analysis rather than reporting only benchmark gains, including dependency statistics, case studies, and error analyses.; The authors are transparent about limitations and show that the method does not obviously help generic instruction tuning (Alpaca), which supports the claim that the effect is reasoning-specific.

Weaknesses: The central explanatory claim—that MFT improves reasoning by increasing long-distance dependency on questions and earlier steps—is suggestive but not convincingly established as causal.; Several empirical comparisons are not fully controlled: MFT is often trained for more epochs than SFT (e.g., 10 vs 3 on small datasets, 5 vs 3 on larger ones), which confounds attribution of gains to masking alone.; The dependency metric is unconventional and under-validated; the paper does not sufficiently justify that perturbing one numeric token and observing changes in another reliably measures reasoning dependency rather than local instability.; Some evaluations rely on small-scale or proxy analyses (e.g., 200 samples for false positives, 300 samples for dependency counts, GPT-4-based judging), limiting confidence in the mechanistic conclusions.; The OOD generalization evidence is mixed and sometimes negative (e.g., MetaMathQA-Mistral), so claims about generalization should be more restrained.; The method is evaluated mainly on answer accuracy for math benchmarks; there is limited rigorous assessment of step-level correctness beyond small GPT-4-judged subsets.; The paper occasionally overstates novelty relative to prior token-noising/data-noising regularization literature; the main novelty is the application to chain-of-thought reasoning rather than a fundamentally new objective.

Methodological Flaws: The main SFT vs MFT comparisons are confounded by unequal training budgets. According to page 4 and Appendix C (page 18), MFT commonly uses more epochs than SFT (e.g., 10 vs 3 on GSM8K/MATH for Llama-2-7B, 5 vs 3 on larger datasets). Although the paper argues SFT does not benefit from longer training, the primary tables still do not present a fully budget-matched comparison, weakening causal claims about MFT itself.; The dependency-learning argument is not rigorously validated. The proposed metric perturbs one numeric token and checks whether another token changes, but this does not cleanly identify semantic dependency or attention to premises; it may reflect decoding sensitivity, lexical coupling, or instability. No statistical significance tests or robustness analyses are reported for these dependency histograms.; The paper informally equates MFT to a special case of RL/process supervision in Appendix B, but this analogy is mathematically weak. Setting rewards to 1 for all ground-truth tokens and treating masked contexts as analogous to sampled trajectories does not establish equivalence to RL optimization; it is at best an intuition, not a derivation.; The false-positive and error-type analyses depend on GPT-4 judgments with ground-truth reasoning included in the prompt. This introduces evaluator bias and possible leakage toward favorable judgments, and the reported 92% accuracy of GPT-4 on the dataset is not enough to guarantee reliable fine-grained step-level annotation.; The comparison with other regularization methods in Table 5 may not be fully fair because hyperparameter tuning effort appears uneven and methods like dropout/NEFTune/scheduled sampling may require different schedules or placements to perform well. Thus the conclusion that 'minimal semantics at noisy positions' is the key principle is plausible but not definitively established.; The claim that MFT preserves or improves intermediate-step correctness is only weakly supported: Table 8 uses just 200 samples and shows very small absolute differences (e.g., false positives 2 vs 3, step-wise accuracy 0.516 vs 0.540), which may not be statistically meaningful.; There is an apparent typo/inconsistency in Appendix C, Table 9 (page 18), where the GSM8K-RFT-100 row for Llama2-7B lists the second method as 'SFT' with mask ratio 0.4, likely intended to be MFT. Such inconsistencies reduce confidence in experimental reporting.
