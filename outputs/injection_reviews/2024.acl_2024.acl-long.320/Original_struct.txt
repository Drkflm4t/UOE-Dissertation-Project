Summary: This paper proposes Masked thought Fine-Tuning (MFT), a simple modification to supervised fine-tuning for mathematical reasoning: randomly mask or replace some tokens in the chain-of-thought during training while keeping the original target reasoning as supervision. The authors argue that this acts as a regularizer that reduces over-reliance on nearby reasoning tokens and encourages stronger dependence on the original question and earlier steps. Across Llama-2 and Mistral base models and several math training datasets (GSM8K, MATH, GSM8K-RFT, MathInstruct, MetaMathQA), MFT consistently improves over standard SFT, with especially notable gains on smaller datasets and on the distractor-heavy GSM-IC benchmark. The paper also compares MFT to other noise/regularization methods, presents dependency analyses and case studies, and argues that MFT is complementary to explicit data augmentation methods such as RFT and GPT-generated math instruction datasets.

Strengths: The core idea is very simple, easy to implement, and low-cost compared with methods requiring larger teacher models, self-sampling, or human annotation.; Empirical gains are consistent across multiple datasets, two model families, and several training regimes; the main tables show improvements over SFT in most settings.; The method appears complementary to existing augmentation pipelines such as RFT, MathInstruct, and MetaMathQA rather than replacing them.; The GSM-IC results are particularly interesting, suggesting improved robustness to irrelevant context and better use of question premises.; The paper includes useful ablations over noise type and placement; Table 5 provides evidence that partial masking/replacement in reasoning steps is more effective than several alternative regularization schemes.; The dependency analysis offers a plausible mechanistic hypothesis for why the method helps, namely shifting reliance toward longer-range dependencies and the question text.; The paper is generally well organized and includes implementation details, limitations, and qualitative examples of both wins and failures.

Weaknesses: The main methodological comparison may not be fully fair because MFT is often trained for more epochs than SFT (e.g., 10 vs 3 on small datasets, 5 vs 3 on larger ones), so some gains may partly reflect additional optimization rather than masking alone.; The central mechanistic claim about dependency transfer is suggestive but not rigorously established as causal; the analyses are correlational and based on probing/perturbation heuristics.; Evaluation is concentrated on math reasoning, and the paper itself shows little to no benefit on general instruction tuning data, limiting the scope of the contribution.; Some reported OOD generalization results are mixed, including slight degradation for MetaMathQA-Mistral on science reasoning benchmarks, which weakens claims of broad transfer.; The method relies on chain-of-thought supervision during training, which may limit applicability in settings where such rationales are unavailable or undesirable.; Several analyses depend on GPT-4-based judging of intermediate-step correctness and error types, which introduces evaluator noise and potential bias.; The paper does not deeply analyze sensitivity to hyperparameters such as mask ratio across mixed datasets or provide a principled way to choose them.

Soundness Issues: A key soundness concern is the unequal training budget between SFT and MFT. Since MFT is trained longer by design, the paper should more carefully control for total optimization steps/tokens or provide matched-budget comparisons as a primary result.; The dependency metric based on perturbing numeric tokens and checking whether later tokens change is intuitive but somewhat narrow; it may not fully capture reasoning dependencies beyond numeric-token interactions.; Claims that MFT achieves performance comparable to self-sampling/RFT without extra resources are partly true empirically, but RFT and MFT differ in both data volume and training setup, so the comparison should be interpreted cautiously.; The recommendation to leave questions unmasked is empirically motivated, but the paper does not fully explore whether selective masking strategies conditioned on step type or token type would outperform the fixed heuristic.; False-positive and error analyses are based on relatively small samples (e.g., 200 or 300 examples), so conclusions about reasoning quality preservation are indicative rather than definitive.
