This paper proposes Masked thought Fine-Tuning (MFT), a very simple training modification for mathematical reasoning: randomly mask some tokens in the chain-of-thought during supervised fine-tuning while keeping the original target reasoning as the label. The central claim is that this lightweight perturbation improves reasoning performance, especially by encouraging the model to rely less on fragile local continuation patterns and more on longer-range dependencies, particularly the original question. Empirically, the paper reports consistent gains over standard SFT across GSM8K-, MATH-, and augmentation-based training settings, with additional robustness improvements on GSM-IC and some evidence of complementarity with existing data augmentation pipelines.

Overall, I found the paper interesting, well-motivated, and surprisingly strong for such a simple idea. The main appeal is precisely that the intervention is minimal yet appears to produce meaningful gains. In a literature where many reasoning improvements depend on expensive data generation, larger teacher models, or RL-style pipelines, the paper makes a compelling case that a cheap regularization-style modification can recover a nontrivial fraction of those benefits. I think this is a valuable contribution, both practically and conceptually.

The strongest aspect of the paper is the empirical simplicity-to-impact ratio. On page 4, Table 1 shows fairly consistent improvements from MFT over SFT across two base models and multiple training datasets. For example, with Llama-2-7B trained on GSM8K, accuracy rises from 41.6 to 47.1; with Mistral-7B on GSM8K, from 60.9 to 64.0; with MathInstruct, gains persist for both backbones. These are meaningful improvements for a method that requires only token masking during training and no external supervision. The robustness result on GSM-IC is also notable: page 8, Table 7 reports much larger gains on distractor-heavy inputs than on standard GSM8K, which supports the authors’ claim that MFT encourages better use of relevant premises from the question rather than over-reliance on nearby reasoning tokens.

I also appreciated the comparison to more expensive alternatives. Page 5, Table 2 is particularly effective in positioning the method: MFT on the original 7K GSM8K data reaches performance comparable to SFT trained on much larger RFT-augmented datasets, and combining MFT with RFT data improves further. This makes the paper practically relevant. Even if MFT does not fully replace stronger data generation or RL pipelines in all settings, it appears to be a highly attractive add-on because of its low implementation cost and favorable compute/data tradeoff.

Another strength is that the paper goes beyond reporting gains and tries to explain them. The dependency analysis in Figures 1, 7, 8, and 9 is one of the more interesting parts of the paper. The authors argue that MFT shifts the model toward longer-distance dependencies and stronger dependence on the question. The charts on page 2 show this most clearly for Llama-2-7B on GSM8K: compared with SFT, MFT reduces short-range dependence within the chain while increasing dependence on more distant tokens and on question tokens. The case studies on pages 8 and 20 are also helpful in making this intuition concrete. Whether or not one fully accepts the causal story, the paper does a good job of connecting the empirical effect to a plausible mechanism rather than leaving the method as a pure black-box trick.

The regularization comparison is another positive. On pages 6–7, the paper situates MFT within a broader noise-injection framework and compares against dropout, NEFTune, scheduled sampling, and different masking/replacement variants. Table 5 is useful because it suggests two practical principles: preserve some clean context, and make corrupted positions semantically uninformative. This is a nice distillation. It also strengthens the paper’s claim that the gains are not just due to “adding any noise,” but depend on where and how the corruption is applied.

That said, there are several limitations and open questions that should be discussed more carefully.

First, the fairness of some comparisons is not ideal because MFT is trained for more epochs than SFT. On page 4 and in the implementation details on page 18, SFT is typically trained for 3 epochs while MFT is trained for 5 or 10 epochs. The authors do acknowledge that MFT needs more steps to converge and show sample-efficiency curves on page 5, but this still complicates attribution: some portion of the gain may come from longer optimization rather than the masking itself. The paper partially addresses this by stating that extending SFT training does not yield better results, but this point would be much stronger with a systematic compute-matched comparison in the main tables. For example, reporting SFT at its best validation checkpoint under the same total token budget or wall-clock budget would make the empirical claim cleaner.

Second, while the dependency analysis is intriguing, it remains somewhat correlational. The paper is careful in the limitations section on page 10, but the main text occasionally reads more causally than the evidence warrants. The disturbance-based dependency metric over numeric tokens is clever, yet it is still a proxy. It would help to more clearly separate “observed association” from “established mechanism.” For instance, one could imagine that masking improves robustness through a more generic denoising or anti-overfitting effect, with dependency shifts being a consequence rather than the primary cause. I do not think this undermines the paper’s empirical contribution, but the mechanistic claims should be framed a bit more cautiously.

Third, the OOD generalization story is mixed. Page 5, Table 3 shows that MFT helps in some multi-source settings, especially MathInstruct with Mistral, but hurts in at least one case (MetaMathQA with Mistral: 51.4 to 50.1 average). The authors offer a plausible explanation that diverse multi-task data may be needed for transfer, but this section would benefit from a more systematic analysis. As written, the paper’s strongest claims are clearly about in-domain math reasoning and distractor robustness, while broader reasoning generalization remains tentative.

Fourth, the method seems somewhat sensitive to hyperparameters and dataset characteristics. On page 6, the authors note that GSM8K tolerates a relatively high mask ratio, whereas MATH requires smaller ratios. This is reasonable, but it suggests that the method may need dataset-specific tuning. Since one of the selling points is simplicity, it would be useful to provide a clearer default recipe or heuristic for selecting mask ratio based on measurable dataset properties, beyond qualitative statements about formula density.

Fifth, the paper’s evaluation is centered on final-answer accuracy, with only limited process-level validation. The false-positive analysis on pages 9 and 21–23 is a good start, and I appreciate that the authors checked whether MFT merely increases lucky answer matching. However, the analysis is small-scale and relies on GPT-4 as judge. A somewhat larger human-verified or rule-based step-level evaluation would strengthen confidence that MFT improves reasoning quality rather than only answer calibration. Similarly, the error taxonomy on page 26 is informative, but again based on a relatively small sample.

There are also a few presentation issues. Some terminology could be tightened: the paper sometimes uses “source” and “target” token noise in ways that are not immediately intuitive, and the notation in Eq. (1) could be explained more carefully. A few tables would benefit from clearer highlighting of whether comparisons are compute-matched. The discussion relating MFT to RL/RFT in Appendix B is interesting, but the claim that MFT can be regarded as a special case of RL feels more metaphorical than formal; I would avoid overstating that connection.

Despite these concerns, I think the paper is a strong submission. The idea is simple, novel in this specific reasoning-finetuning context, easy to reproduce, and empirically effective across multiple settings. Importantly, the paper does more than present a trick: it offers a coherent hypothesis about why the trick works, supported by several complementary analyses, including the dependency histograms on pages 2 and 19, the robustness results on page 8, and the regularization ablations on page 7. Even where the mechanistic story is not fully nailed down, the evidence is thoughtful and useful.

If accepted, I would encourage the authors in a revision to strengthen three points: add stricter compute-matched baselines for SFT versus MFT; temper causal language around dependency transfer unless further evidence is added; and expand the discussion of when MFT helps or fails, especially across heterogeneous datasets and non-math instruction tuning. None of these issues are fatal, and all seem addressable.

In summary, this is a compelling paper with a surprisingly effective and practical idea. It makes a meaningful empirical contribution to reasoning fine-tuning, offers useful insight into the role of dependency structure in chain-of-thought learning, and should be of broad interest to researchers working on LLM reasoning, training efficiency, and regularization. I lean positive and would support acceptance.
