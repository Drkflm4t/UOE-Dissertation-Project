This paper proposes Masked thought Fine-Tuning (MFT), a very simple modification to supervised fine-tuning for mathematical reasoning: randomly mask or replace some tokens in the chain-of-thought during training while keeping the original target reasoning as supervision. The central empirical claim is that this perturbation improves reasoning accuracy, especially on math datasets, without requiring external data generation or reward models. The paper further argues that MFT works by shifting the model away from short-range dependencies within the chain-of-thought and toward longer-range dependencies, especially dependence on the original question.

Overall, I found the paper interesting and surprisingly strong for such a simple idea. The main appeal is its simplicity: unlike many recent reasoning-improvement methods that rely on expensive synthetic data generation, rejection sampling, or RL-style pipelines, this method changes only the training input and appears to yield consistent gains across several datasets and two model families. The empirical improvements on GSM8K are meaningful, and the comparison to RFT is particularly compelling from a cost-effectiveness perspective. The paper is also commendable for going beyond pure benchmarking and attempting to analyze mechanism through dependency probes, distractor robustness, and error analysis. At the same time, I have several concerns about experimental fairness, the strength of the causal claims behind the proposed mechanism, and some methodological ambiguities that should be addressed before I would fully trust the conclusions.

The strongest aspect of the paper is the empirical consistency of the gains in the main math setting. In Table 1 on page 4, MFT improves over standard SFT across GSM8K, MATH, GSM8K-RFT-U33, MathInstruct, and MetaMathQA for both Llama-2-7B and Mistral-7B, with especially notable gains on smaller or weaker-data settings. For example, on Llama-2-7B trained on GSM8K, the gain is from 41.6 to 47.1 on GSM8K test; on Mistral-7B trained on GSM8K, from 60.9 to 64.0. The gains persist even when training data already comes from stronger augmentation pipelines such as MathInstruct and MetaMathQA. This supports the claim that MFT is complementary rather than merely a substitute for synthetic-data augmentation. Table 2 on page 5 is also persuasive: MFT on the original 7K GSM8K data reaches 47.1, nearly matching SFT trained on 46K RFT-100 data at 47.6, and combining MFT with RFT data improves further. That is a practically important result.

I also appreciated the robustness-oriented evaluation. The GSM-IC results in Table 7 on page 8 are striking: for Llama-2-7B, MFT improves from 48.0 to 59.2, substantially larger than the gain from RFT-100. If valid, this suggests the method is not merely memorizing local reasoning templates but may indeed help the model attend more effectively to relevant premises under distraction. This is one of the more convincing pieces of evidence in favor of the authors’ interpretation.

The paper’s second major contribution is the proposed explanation in terms of dependency learning. Figures 1 and 7–9, together with the case studies on pages 8 and 20, suggest that MFT reduces reliance on nearby tokens in the chain-of-thought and increases reliance on the question and earlier steps. This is an intuitively plausible story. In reasoning, over-reliance on recent generated text can amplify exposure bias and local hallucinations; masking intermediate tokens during training could force the model to recover from partial corruption by consulting more global context. I think this is a valuable hypothesis, and the paper deserves credit for trying to operationalize it rather than stopping at benchmark gains.

That said, I am not fully convinced by the mechanistic claims as currently supported. The dependency analysis is suggestive but not yet definitive. The probing method perturbs one numeric token and checks whether another changes, which is a reasonable heuristic, but it is still far from a causal decomposition of what the model has learned. In particular, the paper often moves from “MFT correlates with increased long-distance dependency” to “this is why performance improves.” That leap is too strong. It remains possible that the gains arise from more generic regularization, improved robustness to corrupted prefixes, or a training objective that better matches partial-information inference, without the specific dependency-shift being the primary driver. The limitations section acknowledges this, which is good, but the main text still occasionally overstates the explanation.

A more serious concern is fairness of the main SFT versus MFT comparisons. On page 4 and in Table 9 on page 18, MFT is trained for substantially more epochs than SFT: typically 10 epochs versus 3 on small datasets, and 5 versus 3 on larger ones. The authors argue that MFT needs more steps to converge and that equal sample budgets still favor MFT, with Figure 2 on page 5 showing better sample efficiency. However, the headline comparisons in Tables 1 and 2 are not strictly controlled for either epochs or total optimization steps. Since MFT sees the same data more times, one cannot cleanly attribute all gains to masking alone. The paper does mention that extending SFT epochs does not help and refers to Table 9 for convergence details, but this is not enough. What is needed is a direct matched-compute or matched-token-update comparison in the main paper: for example, SFT and MFT trained for the same number of steps, and perhaps also best-of-budget curves. Without that, the practical claim remains strong, but the scientific claim that masking itself is the key factor is somewhat weakened.

Relatedly, the hyperparameter tuning burden appears asymmetric. MFT uses dataset-specific mask ratios, warmup schedules, and longer training durations. The paper states that GSM8K uses 0.4 while MATH uses 0.1–0.2, and warmup occupies roughly two-thirds of training steps. This may be reasonable, but it raises the question of whether SFT baselines were equally tuned. If MFT receives more careful optimization and schedule selection than SFT, some gains may reflect tuning effort rather than method superiority. The paper should clarify exactly how much tuning was done for each baseline and whether the same validation protocol was used.

The comparison with other regularization methods in Table 5 on page 7 is interesting but somewhat difficult to interpret cleanly. The authors compare against dropout, NEFTune, scheduled sampling, and masked/random token replacement. The conclusion is that effective regularization requires retaining some clean tokens while making noisy positions minimally semantic. This is plausible, and the results do support the idea that stronger corruption at selected target positions works better than mild embedding noise. However, the baselines are not all equally natural or equally optimized for this setting. For example, NEFTune is known to help instruction tuning but may require careful scale selection and perhaps different placement than used here; scheduled sampling for transformers is notoriously tricky and often unstable. The paper’s conclusion that MFT’s design principles are broadly necessary may therefore be too broad given the limited baseline tuning. I would phrase this more cautiously as an empirical observation within the tested setup.

I also have some concerns about the exact implementation and terminology. The method is called “Masked thought Fine-Tuning,” but the best GSM8K result in Table 5 comes from random token replacement rather than literal [mask] insertion, and Table 9 indicates that random tokens are used for GSM8K while [mask] is used for other datasets. This is not a fatal issue, but it makes the naming slightly misleading and the method definition less crisp. Is the core contribution “masking,” “discrete corruption of reasoning tokens,” or “partial target-side noising”? The paper would benefit from a cleaner abstraction and perhaps a more neutral name if random replacement is often as good or better.

Another issue is the use of chain-of-thought supervision itself. The method assumes access to gold reasoning traces and perturbs them during training. This is standard in the literature the paper compares against, but it limits applicability. The paper positions itself partly as avoiding external resources, which is true relative to synthetic augmentation or RL, but it still depends on supervised rationales. It would be useful to discuss more explicitly whether the method is expected to help in settings with only final-answer supervision, or with noisy/weak rationales.

The OOD generalization results in Table 3 on page 5 are mixed and should be discussed more candidly. On MathInstruct, MFT helps average OOD performance, especially for Mistral. But on MetaMathQA with Mistral, it hurts by 1.3 points on average. This weakens any broad claim that MFT improves reasoning in a general sense. The authors do acknowledge that diverse multi-source data may be necessary for transfer, which is a fair interpretation. Still, the paper should sharpen its scope: the strongest evidence is for in-domain mathematical reasoning and distractor robustness, not universal reasoning generalization.

The false-positive and error analyses are useful but not fully rigorous. Table 8 on page 9 and Tables 11–12 in the appendix rely on GPT-4 to segment and judge reasoning steps. This is acceptable as a proxy, but the sample size is small and the reported differences are tiny. Likewise, the error taxonomy in Table 15 on page 26 suggests MFT reduces semantic/incoherent errors while slightly increasing calculator or one-step-missing errors, which is consistent with the paper’s story. But again, this is based on only 300 samples and automated judgment. I would treat these as anecdotal support rather than strong evidence.

I did appreciate the appendix experiment on Alpaca (page 27), which shows that MFT does not help much on general instruction tuning and does not obviously hurt either. This is actually a strength because it narrows the claim appropriately: the method seems particularly suited to reasoning data with rich long-range dependencies. That specificity makes the paper more believable.

There are also several presentation issues. Some writing is repetitive and occasionally imprecise. For example, the paper sometimes conflates “dependency,” “attention,” and “causal reliance,” which are not the same. The notation in Equation (1) is serviceable but could be clearer about source versus target tokens and whether masking is applied only to the target-side chain-of-thought or also elsewhere. Table 9 on page 18 appears to contain a likely typo: for GSM8K-RFT-100 under Llama2-7B, the second row says “SFT” with mask ratio 0.4, which presumably should be MFT. There are also minor grammatical issues throughout. None of these are major, but they do affect polish.

For strengthening the paper, I would most strongly recommend the following additional experiments or clarifications. First, provide matched-compute comparisons between SFT and MFT, ideally with accuracy versus training steps/tokens for both, and report best validation-selected checkpoints under equal budgets. Second, clarify the hyperparameter tuning protocol and ensure baselines are comparably tuned. Third, test whether masking only earlier steps, only later steps, or masking contiguous spans versus random tokens changes the effect; this would directly probe the dependency hypothesis. Fourth, include at least one stronger contemporary baseline beyond RFT-style augmentation, if feasible, or explain why not. Fifth, better separate the claims “MFT improves math reasoning” and “MFT works by increasing long-distance dependency,” making clear that the latter is a hypothesis supported by evidence rather than a proven mechanism.

In terms of significance, I think the paper has real practical value. A method that is this simple, cheap, and broadly compatible with existing reasoning fine-tuning pipelines is worth attention, especially if it consistently improves GSM8K-like tasks and robustness to distractors. The idea is also conceptually interesting because it runs against the intuition that reasoning supervision should always be made more precise rather than partially corrupted. That said, the current version falls short of fully establishing the causal mechanism and of making the fairest possible baseline comparisons.

My overall assessment is positive but somewhat cautious. I believe this is a worthwhile paper with a neat idea, solid empirical promise, and useful analysis, but it needs stronger control experiments and more careful framing of its explanatory claims. If accepted, it would likely stimulate follow-up work on reasoning-specific regularization and target-side corruption objectives. If revised, it could become substantially stronger.

Recommendation-wise, I lean toward acceptance at a venue that values practical empirical contributions and simple, reproducible methods, but not as an unqualified strong accept. The main reasons are the consistent gains, low implementation complexity, and interesting robustness results; the main reservations are fairness of comparison, incomplete mechanistic evidence, and some overstatement in interpretation.
