This is a strong, timely, and well-executed paper on an increasingly important problem: how users can audit black-box LLM APIs to determine whether they are actually serving the model distribution they claim to serve, or whether that distribution has changed due to quantization, watermarking, finetuning, implementation differences, or silent updates. The paper’s central contribution is to formalize this as a two-sample hypothesis testing problem, “Model Equality Testing,” and to show that a simple MMD-based test with an appropriate string kernel is surprisingly effective in practice. The work is both conceptually clean and practically consequential. It addresses a real gap in the current LLM ecosystem, where users often rely on third-party inference providers without any principled way to verify distributional faithfulness or temporal stability.

The framing is excellent. The paper clearly motivates two concrete auditing questions: whether an API has modified a reference model’s distribution, and whether an API has drifted over time. Casting both under a unified statistical testing lens is elegant and useful. I especially appreciated that the authors focus on the sampling setting rather than only greedy decoding or benchmark accuracy. This is a major strength: many practical applications depend on the full output distribution, not just top-1 behavior, and the paper makes a convincing case that existing benchmark-based audits are too narrow for this purpose. The problem formulation in Section 2 is crisp, and the distinction between sample access and log-probability access is handled thoughtfully.

Methodologically, the paper is simple in the best sense. The use of MMD is natural, but the key insight is that kernel choice determines both semantics and power. The comparison between the one-hot kernel, all-substrings kernel, and the proposed Hamming-style string kernel is very informative. The Hamming kernel is computationally cheap, interpretable, and empirically powerful, even though it is only a pseudometric rather than a fully characteristic metric. I found this tradeoff well justified. The paper does not oversell universality; instead, it argues persuasively that in the small-sample regime, a non-universal but better-biased feature map can be much more useful than a theoretically stronger but practically weak alternative. This is exactly the kind of pragmatic statistical design that often matters in real ML systems work.

The empirical evaluation is a major strength. The simulation suite is broad and well chosen: quantization, watermarking, finetuning, and model substitution are all realistic distortions. The headline result that the Hamming MMD test achieves median power of 77.4% with roughly 10 samples per prompt is impressive, especially given the high-dimensionality of the completion space. The plots on pages 6–7 are particularly compelling: the sample complexity comparison in Figure 2, the robustness to longer completions, and the finetuning detection results all support the paper’s claims well. I also appreciated the analysis of pairwise distances between models in Figure 3 on page 7. The observation that models cluster more by family than by size is interesting in its own right and shows that the framework is useful not only for hypothesis testing but also for exploratory model comparison.

The real-world audit of commercial Llama endpoints is the paper’s most impactful component. Applying the method to 31 endpoints across nine providers and four Llama variants makes the work much more than a methodological exercise. The finding that 11 of 31 endpoints differ from Meta’s released reference distributions is important and likely to attract substantial attention. The tables and figures in Section 5 are clear and informative. In particular, Table 1 on page 9 gives a concise summary of which providers fail under which tasks, while Figure 4 adds useful effect-size context by showing that some deviations are as large as swapping in an entirely different model family. This is a striking result. The qualitative examples in Box 2 and Appendix D also help ground the statistical findings in observable behavioral differences.

Another notable strength is the paper’s attention to practical usability. The authors release code, a Python package, and a large dataset of 1.1M completions. This substantially increases the paper’s value to the community. The appendices are also unusually thorough. I found the discussion of p-value estimation, permutation testing, composite nulls, asymmetric sampling costs, and approximate goodness-of-fit comparisons to be careful and useful. The paper does a good job of anticipating likely reviewer questions and addressing them empirically.

I also want to highlight the paper’s clarity. The writing is polished, the motivation is easy to follow, and the figures are generally strong. The visual summary in Figure 1 on page 2 is effective, and the progression from formalization to simulations to deployment is well structured. The paper is accessible to both ML systems and statistics audiences without sacrificing rigor.

There are a few limitations, but they are relatively minor and mostly point to future work rather than undermining the current contribution. First, the strongest empirical results depend on a particular kernel choice and prompt distribution design. While the authors do test across multiple prompt sets and tasks, the method’s sensitivity to prompt selection remains important in practice. Figure 8 in the appendix suggests that power improves with more prompts, which is useful, but it would be helpful to have a bit more guidance in the main text on how an end user should choose prompts for a custom audit and how robust conclusions are to prompt mismatch. That said, the paper already goes farther than most prior work in evaluating prompt variation.

Second, the Hamming kernel’s non-injectivity is acknowledged, but the practical implications could be discussed a bit more explicitly in the main paper rather than mostly in Appendix A. In principle, there are distribution shifts that this kernel will miss. The empirical evidence suggests this is not a serious issue for the distortions studied here, but a short discussion of plausible failure modes would strengthen the presentation. Relatedly, the weak performance against fp16 in some settings is interesting and deserves a slightly more developed interpretation. The paper notes that fp16/fp32 differences may not be well captured by Hamming distance; expanding on what kinds of shifts are “orthogonal” to this kernel would be valuable.

Third, the API audit methodology necessarily faces confounds from provider-side prompt templating, decoding implementation details, caching, and time-varying backend changes. The authors are careful about matching chat templates and documenting collection dates in the appendix, which I appreciated. Still, because the paper’s conclusions are about endpoint distributions rather than necessarily intentional provider modifications, the wording should remain precise throughout. In most places it is, but a few claims could perhaps more consistently emphasize that the test detects distributional inequality, not its cause. This is not a flaw in the method, just an interpretational nuance.

Fourth, the composite null in Section 5 is sensible but somewhat conservative, and the decision to threshold average rejection rate over ten samples at 0.5 is pragmatic rather than fully formal. I understand why this was done to reduce variance and cost, and the appendix provides supporting detail, but a bit more statistical justification for this aggregation rule would improve the end-to-end audit story. Even so, the main conclusions appear robust, especially given the large effect sizes for several flagged endpoints.

A smaller presentation issue is that some appendix examples and tables are dense, and a few qualitative samples are more anecdotal than diagnostic. This is not a major concern, since the core quantitative evidence is strong, but the paper could perhaps streamline a few appendix elements or better connect them to the main claims.

Overall, I found this paper highly compelling. It identifies an important and underexplored problem, proposes a principled and practical solution, validates it extensively, and demonstrates real-world impact through a substantial audit of commercial APIs. The combination of statistical formalization, empirical depth, and practical relevance is excellent. This feels like exactly the kind of work that can shape how the community thinks about auditing and reproducibility for black-box LLM services.

My recommendation is to accept. The paper is novel, technically sound, empirically strong, and broadly useful. It should be of interest to researchers in LLM evaluation, trustworthy ML, ML systems, and applied statistics, and I expect it to stimulate follow-up work on stronger tests, adaptive prompt selection, and auditing beyond text generation.
