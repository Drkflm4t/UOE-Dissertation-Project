This paper studies an important and timely problem: how users can audit black-box LLM APIs to determine whether an endpoint is actually serving the claimed model distribution, or whether it has been altered through quantization, watermarking, finetuning, implementation differences, or silent updates. The paper formalizes this as a two-sample hypothesis testing problem, “Model Equality Testing,” and proposes kernel MMD-based tests, with a particular emphasis on a simple Hamming string kernel over completions. The empirical study is extensive: the authors compare several candidate tests in simulation, examine robustness across distortions and prompt distributions, and then audit 31 commercial endpoints serving Llama-family models. The headline findings are strong and practically relevant: the Hamming-MMD test appears substantially more sample-efficient than several natural baselines, and the audit flags a nontrivial fraction of commercial endpoints as distributionally different from Meta’s released weights.

Overall, I found the paper interesting, well-motivated, and potentially impactful. The problem formulation is clean and useful, and the paper makes a compelling case that benchmark-style auditing is too narrow for many real users. The empirical results are also striking, especially the API audit in Section 5 and the distance visualizations in Figures 3–4 and Appendix C.8. The work is also commendably practical: it releases code and a large completion dataset, and the methodology only requires sample access rather than logprobs. I think this paper has the ingredients of a strong conference contribution. At the same time, there are several methodological and interpretational issues that should be clarified or strengthened before I would be fully convinced by the strongest claims.

The main strength of the paper is the problem framing. Casting API auditing as a distribution equality test is elegant and general. It unifies several practically important questions: faithfulness to open weights, drift over time, and substitution with another model. This is a better fit than accuracy-only auditing for long-form generation, code, and other settings where users care about the full output distribution rather than just greedy correctness. I also appreciated the discussion in Section 3 that kernel choice determines semantics: this is exactly the right lens for understanding why a coarse feature map may miss meaningful differences while a universal kernel may be too sparse in the small-sample regime.

A second major strength is the empirical comparison of tests. The paper does not simply propose MMD in the abstract; it compares Hamming-MMD against one-hot MMD, all-substrings MMD, and classical multinomial-style tests such as L1 and chi-squared. The sample complexity plots on page 6 are persuasive: the Hamming kernel seems to dominate in the low-sample regime that matters for practical auditing. The robustness-to-length result is also important. Since completion spaces explode combinatorially with length, showing that the Hamming kernel degrades more gracefully than one-hot or multinomial tests is a meaningful contribution.

The API audit itself is the most compelling part of the paper. Table 1 on page 9 and the summary in Figure 1 show that 11 of 31 endpoints are flagged. This is a concrete, policy-relevant result. The paper goes beyond binary rejection and estimates effect sizes via MMD, which is valuable because statistical significance alone can be misleading at scale. The observation that some provider deviations are as large as swapping in a different model family is especially notable. The qualitative examples in Box 2 and Appendix D help ground these findings, and the provider-to-provider distance plots in Figure 4 and Appendix C.8 are informative.

That said, my main concern is that the paper’s strongest empirical conclusions depend heavily on one particular kernel whose statistical meaning is limited. The authors are transparent that the Hamming-MMD is only a pseudometric, not a metric, and Appendix A.1 explicitly shows that it only captures stacked token-position marginals rather than the full joint distribution. This is not a minor technicality. It means the test can fail to distinguish distributions that differ in higher-order dependencies while sharing the same per-position marginals. In other words, the method is powerful for some distortions but not a general solution to model equality. The paper acknowledges this, but the framing and conclusions sometimes read more broadly than warranted. I think the paper should more clearly distinguish “detecting many practically relevant distortions” from “testing equality of model distributions” in the full statistical sense. As written, the title and some claims risk overstating what the chosen test can certify.

Relatedly, the kernel selection is almost entirely empirical. That is acceptable to some extent, but the paper would be stronger with more theory or at least more structured analysis of when Hamming-MMD should be expected to work. For example, quantization and watermarking often perturb local token preferences, so a positionwise kernel is plausible. But for finetuning or implementation changes, why should Hamming similarity be the right inductive bias? The fp16 results in Figure 2 already show a failure mode: the test has much lower power there. This suggests the method is sensitive to the geometry of the distortion, not just its magnitude. A more principled discussion of the alternatives the kernel is and is not suited for would improve the paper.

A second concern is the calibration and interpretation of the API audit. In Section 5, the authors define a composite null over fp32 and fp16 reference distributions and reject only if both pairwise tests reject. This is conservative, which is good for false positives, but then they add another layer of aggregation: they repeat tests over ten samples and “fail endpoints if the average rejection rate is at least 0.5.” This latter rule is much less standard statistically, and I did not see a formal justification for its operating characteristics. What false positive rate does this induce overall? How sensitive are the conclusions to the 0.5 threshold, the number of repeats, or the choice of alpha? Since the paper’s most visible claim is that 11/31 endpoints differ from reference weights, the decision rule deserves a more careful treatment. At minimum, I would like confidence intervals over endpoint failure rates, or a sensitivity analysis showing how many endpoints are flagged under nearby thresholds.

More broadly, the audit results may conflate provider-side model changes with other sources of mismatch. The paper notes some of these issues, but they remain important. First, matching chat templates is tricky; Appendix B.1 mentions that the authors used the Llama-3 template for Llama-3.1 because it better matched API token counts at the time. This is reasonable, but it also means the “reference distribution” is partly an inferred implementation choice rather than a canonical ground truth. Second, APIs may differ in undocumented system prompts, stop conditions, tokenizer handling, safety filters, or post-processing. Third, the data collection spans multiple dates for some providers, sometimes over weeks, as shown in Tables 2–6. If an endpoint drifts during collection, the sampled distribution is itself a mixture. Since the paper’s framing includes temporal drift as a target phenomenon, this is not fatal, but it complicates interpretation of a rejection as “this provider modified the model.” The paper should be more explicit that the audit detects endpoint-level distribution mismatch, not necessarily weight-level modification alone.

I also have some reservations about the prompt distributions used in the simulation study. The Wikipedia continuation task is a reasonable long-form benchmark, but it is still a fairly narrow slice of behavior. The prompts are 100-character snippets from multilingual Wikipedia, and the main simulation uses only 25 prompts per distribution. The authors do test HumanEval and UltraChat in the API audit, which helps, but the core kernel comparison in Section 4 is mostly on Wikipedia continuation. Since the paper argues for user-customized auditing, it would be useful to see more diversity in the simulation study itself: dialogue, summarization, instruction following, safety-sensitive prompts, or prompts with stronger semantic constraints. The appendix result in Figure 8 suggests power improves with more prompts, which is encouraging, but it also raises the question of how prompt selection affects detectability. Are there prompt distributions under which the Hamming kernel performs poorly even for meaningful distortions?

Another issue is the baseline selection. The paper compares against several classical tests, but some stronger modern baselines seem absent. For example, one could imagine embedding-based two-sample tests using sentence embeddings, hidden-state features from a reference model, or n-gram feature maps beyond exact positionwise matches. Even within kernel methods, there are many string kernels between one-hot and Hamming. The all-substrings kernel is included but is computationally expensive and perhaps not tuned for this setting. Since the paper’s main methodological claim is that a simple Hamming kernel is especially effective, it would be useful to compare against at least one semantic or representation-based baseline. Otherwise, it is hard to know whether the observed gains are due to MMD per se, the low-sample regime, or simply the weakness of the chosen alternatives.

The goodness-of-fit comparison in Appendix C.3 is interesting but underdeveloped. The finding that approximate goodness-of-fit tests can underperform two-sample tests despite privileged probability access is surprising and potentially important. However, because this section is relegated to the appendix and uses approximations that introduce bias, it currently reads more like an exploratory aside than a solid conclusion. I would either downplay it further or expand it with clearer explanation and perhaps a more principled approximation scheme.

I appreciated the qualitative examples, but some of them also highlight a subtle issue: the test may detect stylistic or entropy differences that are not necessarily harmful, while missing semantically important but distributionally subtle changes. Figure 4 on page 9 shows only moderate correlation between MMD and HumanEval accuracy differences. The authors correctly note that there are multiple ways to be wrong, and MMD captures more than accuracy. Still, from a user perspective, it matters whether a detected difference is consequential. The paper would benefit from a more explicit discussion of how users should interpret a rejection. Does a large Hamming-MMD imply degraded utility, or just different phrasing? The effect-size analysis helps, but some calibration to downstream impact would strengthen the practical story.

On clarity, the paper is generally well written. The exposition of MMD is concise and accessible, and the figures are informative. I especially liked Figure 3 right, which clusters models by estimated pairwise distances, and the appendix heatmaps comparing providers. One minor issue is that some notation and estimator definitions could be tightened. For example, Equation 4 uses equal sample sizes only, with unequal sizes deferred to the appendix; given the practical setting, unequal sizes seem common enough to deserve at least a brief main-text formula. Also, the distinction between token-space and character-space testing is important and should perhaps be emphasized earlier, since it materially affects power, as seen in Table 8.

I also want to note the conflict-of-interest disclosure is appropriate and appreciated, especially given that one author is affiliated with a provider evaluated in the paper. The paper’s release of code and data is another strong positive for reproducibility.

In terms of significance, I think this work could have substantial impact. Auditing black-box LLM APIs is a real need, and the paper offers a concrete, low-cost tool that many practitioners could use. Even if the Hamming-MMD test is not a universal solution, the framing itself is valuable and likely to stimulate follow-up work on stronger tests, better kernels, and broader modalities. The paper’s empirical finding that many commercial endpoints differ from released weights is also likely to attract attention.

My main suggestions for improvement are as follows. First, sharpen the claims around what is and is not being tested. The current method is best described as a powerful practical detector for many common distortions, not a definitive equality test for full sequence distributions. Second, provide a more rigorous treatment of the endpoint-level decision rule in Section 5, including sensitivity analyses for the repeated-test aggregation threshold and perhaps confidence intervals. Third, expand discussion of confounders in the API audit, especially chat templates, system prompts, safety layers, and temporal drift during collection. Fourth, add at least one stronger baseline based on semantic or learned representations, or justify more clearly why such baselines are out of scope. Fifth, broaden the simulation study or at least discuss more explicitly how prompt distribution choice affects power and user-facing applicability.

To summarize my assessment: this is a strong and timely paper with a compelling problem formulation, useful methodology, and impactful empirical findings. Its main weakness is that the chosen test statistic is not fully aligned with the breadth of the “model equality” framing, and some of the audit conclusions rely on somewhat ad hoc aggregation choices and potentially confounded endpoint comparisons. Still, I think the paper makes a meaningful contribution and is close to publishable quality. With clearer positioning and some additional robustness analysis, it would be substantially stronger.

My recommendation is positive but not without reservations.
