Summary: This paper formalizes auditing black-box LLM APIs as a two-sample hypothesis testing problem called Model Equality Testing: given samples from a reference distribution and an API, test whether they induce the same prompt-completion distribution. The authors propose kernel two-sample tests based on Maximum Mean Discrepancy, and empirically find that a simple Hamming-style string kernel over completions is the most sample-efficient among several alternatives. In simulations across quantization, watermarking, finetuning, and model-swap perturbations, the method achieves strong power with relatively few samples, especially for long-form generation. The paper then audits 31 commercial endpoints serving Llama models and reports that 11 endpoints differ from Meta reference weights under their testing setup. The work also uses estimated MMD as an effect-size measure to compare models and providers.

Strengths: Timely and practically important problem: auditing black-box LLM APIs for undisclosed distribution shifts is highly relevant for users, benchmarking, and reproducibility.; Clear problem formulation as a statistically grounded two-sample testing task, with explicit hypotheses and Type-I error control goals.; Methodologically simple and appealing: MMD with a string kernel is easy to implement, does not require log-probability access, and naturally applies to sampled long-form outputs.; Strong empirical evaluation breadth: the paper studies quantization, watermarking, finetuning, model swaps, prompt distributions, completion lengths, asymmetric sampling costs, and composite nulls.; Useful practical findings: the Hamming-kernel MMD appears substantially more sample-efficient than one-hot and classical multinomial-style tests in the low-sample regime.; Compelling real-world case study auditing 31 commercial Llama endpoints across multiple providers and tasks.; The paper goes beyond binary testing by estimating pairwise distances/effect sizes between models and APIs, which is a valuable extension.; Good transparency and reproducibility: code, package, and a large completion dataset are released; appendices provide many implementation details and additional plots.; The discussion of character-space comparison for models/APIs with different tokenizers is pragmatic and broadens applicability.

Weaknesses: The core empirical validation is almost entirely simulation- and benchmark-driven; there is limited theoretical analysis of power/sample complexity for the proposed Hamming-kernel test in this setting.; The preferred Hamming MMD is only a pseudometric, so it can miss distribution differences by construction; the paper acknowledges this but does not deeply characterize failure modes.; Many experiments rely on a narrow family of prompt distributions, especially Wikipedia continuation, which may not reflect realistic downstream applications or adversarially chosen prompts.; The audit conclusions about commercial providers are suggestive but not definitive: observed differences could arise from undocumented prompt templating, safety layers, decoding-stack differences, tokenizer/rendering mismatches, or time drift rather than intentional model substitution/modification.; The composite-null audit rule is conservative and appears to reduce power substantially; some negative results may therefore be hard to interpret.; The decision to average rejection rates over 10 repeated samples and fail endpoints when the average rejection rate is at least 0.5 is somewhat ad hoc and not fully justified statistically.; Some comparisons in character space may conflate semantic/model differences with superficial formatting differences, especially for code tasks and APIs that prepend boilerplate.; The paper emphasizes strong power at roughly 10 samples per prompt, but this still implies hundreds of API calls overall; for some users/tasks this may not be as lightweight as suggested.; The real-world audit lacks ground-truth confirmation from providers, so claims about what modifications occurred remain speculative.

Soundness Issues: The hypothesis-testing framework itself is sound, and the paper is careful about Type-I error control for the basic and composite-null settings. However, the practical audit pipeline introduces extra heuristics (e.g., repeated runs and thresholding average rejection rate at 0.5) whose statistical properties are not fully analyzed.; Kernel choice is empirically motivated rather than theoretically justified. Since the Hamming kernel is not characteristic, failure to reject does not strongly support equality beyond the chosen feature space.; Power estimates are based on sampling from a pre-collected dataset of completions rather than fresh online generation in most experiments; this is reasonable for Monte Carlo study but may understate operational variability.; The API audit may be sensitive to hidden confounders such as provider-side chat templates, system prompts, stop conditions, or output post-processing. The authors attempt to match templates and token counts, but residual mismatch remains a plausible alternative explanation for some detected differences.; Using simulated null distributions from local reference models assumes the local implementation faithfully matches the intended reference distribution; any local inference-stack differences could affect conclusions.; The correlation analysis between MMD and HumanEval accuracy is informative but modest (R^2 about 0.39), so MMD should not be overinterpreted as a direct proxy for capability differences.
