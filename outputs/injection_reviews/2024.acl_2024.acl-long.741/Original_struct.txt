Summary: The paper studies user-side privacy protection for online self-disclosures. It introduces: (1) a 19-category taxonomy of self-disclosure spans and a Reddit corpus of 2.4K posts with 4.8K annotated spans; (2) span-level detection models for identifying disclosures; (3) an HCI interview study with 21 Reddit users assessing perceived usefulness; (4) a new task of self-disclosure abstraction, where detected spans are rewritten into less specific alternatives while preserving utility; and (5) an importance-rating task to decide which disclosures should be removed, abstracted, or kept. Empirically, the best detection model reaches about 65.7 partial span F1 on the multi-class setting, abstraction models are evaluated mainly by human judgments showing moderate privacy increase and high utility preservation, and importance rating reaches about 80% accuracy under a permissive matching criterion. The paper is timely, practically motivated, and combines NLP with user-centered evaluation, but several methodological choices weaken the strength of the claims, especially around annotation reliability, evaluation design, and the validity of the abstraction/importance metrics.

Strengths: Addresses an important and underexplored problem: protecting user-side privacy in voluntary online self-disclosures rather than only masking classic PII.; Introduces a reasonably broad taxonomy of 19 disclosure categories spanning both demographic attributes and personal experiences (Table 1, page 2), which is broader than much prior work.; Creates a new span-level annotated corpus with nontrivial scale (2.4K posts, 4.8K spans), which is valuable for future research.; Uses span-level detection rather than sentence/document classification, which is better aligned with actionable privacy assistance.; Includes an HCI user study with real Reddit users, adding practical perspective beyond benchmark metrics.; The abstraction task is novel and well motivated by user feedback; the examples in Figure 1/page 1 and Table 11/page 17 clearly illustrate the intended behavior.; Compares multiple fine-tuning strategies for abstraction generation and reports both automatic and human evaluations.; The paper is generally transparent about limitations and ethical considerations, including restricted release and IRB approval.

Weaknesses: Annotation agreement for the core detection dataset is only moderate overall (Krippendorff's alpha 0.54 on page 3 / Table 6), with several important categories substantially lower, raising concerns about label consistency and upper-bound performance.; The detection performance, while promising, is still modest for a deployment-oriented privacy tool: average partial span F1 is about 65.7, and some categories such as relationship status, pet, health, and family are much weaker (Tables 2 and 8, pages 3 and 16).; The user study is small (21 participants), qualitative, and potentially subject to selection bias; claims about real-world applicability are therefore suggestive rather than conclusive.; The abstraction training data is generated by GPT-3.5/GPT-4 rather than humans, so the model is largely distilled from another model's preferences and failure modes.; Human evaluation for abstraction has only fair agreement (e.g., alpha 0.37 on z-scores; page 7), which limits confidence in fine-grained ranking among methods.; The importance-rating task is highly subjective, yet the reported accuracy uses a permissive criterion that counts a prediction as correct if it matches any one of three annotations, likely inflating performance.; The paper does not evaluate the full end-to-end pipeline (detection -> importance -> abstraction), even though this is the practical use case.; Restricted release of data/models may limit reproducibility and community verification, even if ethically understandable.

Methodological Flaws: The scientific validity of the importance-rating results is weakened by the evaluation metric. Counting a prediction as correct if it matches any one of three annotators is much easier than matching a gold consensus label, especially when disagreement is high (Krippendorff's alpha 0.29; page 7). Thus the reported ~80% accuracy is not directly comparable to standard classification accuracy and likely overstates practical performance.; The abstraction task is trained on synthetic labels produced by GPT-3.5 for train/dev and evaluated against GPT-4-generated references for automatic metrics (page 6). This creates a teacher-student setup where the target distribution is defined by proprietary LLM outputs rather than human gold abstractions, weakening claims that the model learns a generally valid notion of privacy-preserving abstraction.; The paper's main abstraction claim is based on human ratings of 'privacy increase' and 'utility preservation', but there is no objective privacy-risk model or adversarial re-identification evaluation. Therefore, the claim that privacy risks are reduced is only indirectly supported by subjective judgments, not by demonstrated reduction in inference or linkage risk.; Detection training/evaluation mixes singly annotated data for most training with only a smaller adjudicated subset for continual fine-tuning/evaluation (pages 3-4). Given moderate agreement, label noise may materially affect both training and reported performance; the paper does not quantify sensitivity to annotation uncertainty.; For rare classes (name/contact), the system is partly a hybrid pipeline using external NER/regex plus a sentence classifier rather than the same learned detector (page 4). This makes the overall task formulation and evaluation less uniform, and the paper does not provide integrated end-to-end results including these categories in the main tables.; All experimental results are from single runs (page 17), with no variance estimates or significance testing. Given small evaluation sets in several analyses and close scores among methods, some reported differences may not be statistically meaningful.; The user-study conclusion that the model 'helps users manage privacy risks' is stronger than what the evidence supports. The study measures perceptions and stated willingness to use, not actual reduction in risky disclosures or improved posting decisions in a controlled behavioral setting.; The comparison to GPT-4 for detection is not fully balanced methodologically: prompted GPT-4 is compared against supervised fine-tuned models trained on the task dataset. While useful as a practical baseline, it should not be interpreted as evidence of superior modeling without acknowledging the asymmetry in supervision.
