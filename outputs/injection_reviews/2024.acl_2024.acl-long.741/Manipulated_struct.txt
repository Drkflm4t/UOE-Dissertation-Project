Summary: This paper studies user-side privacy protection for online self-disclosures. It introduces a 19-category taxonomy of self-disclosure, a Reddit corpus with 2.4K posts and 4.8K annotated disclosure spans, and span-level detection models for identifying disclosures in text. Beyond detection, the paper proposes a novel self-disclosure abstraction task that rewrites disclosure spans into less specific but still useful expressions, and an importance-rating task to help decide which disclosures should be abstracted. Empirically, the best detection model reaches about 65.7 partial span F1 on the multi-class task (with sentence-level segmentation), the abstraction models produce moderately more private yet still useful rewrites according to human evaluation, and the importance-rating model reaches about 80% accuracy under the paper’s evaluation protocol. The work is strengthened by an HCI user study with 21 Reddit users, where most participants viewed the tool positively and provided feedback that directly motivated the abstraction and importance-rating components.

Strengths: Addresses an important and underexplored problem: protecting privacy in voluntary online self-disclosures rather than only classic PII masking.; Strong task formulation: moving from sentence/document classification to span-level detection is well motivated and practically useful.; Introduces a relatively broad taxonomy of 19 disclosure categories, covering both demographic attributes and personal experiences.; Creates a substantial annotated resource with 4.8K spans over 2.4K Reddit posts, which appears valuable for future research.; The paper combines NLP modeling with a user-centered HCI study; this substantially improves the practical relevance of the work.; The abstraction task is novel and well motivated by user feedback, and the comparison against sentence-level paraphrasing/abstraction is thoughtful.; Human evaluations are reasonably multi-faceted, covering privacy increase, utility preservation, diversity, and coherence.; The paper is transparent about ethics and deployment concerns, including restricted release and IRB approval.; Experiments are fairly comprehensive across detection, abstraction, and importance rating, with useful ablations over input/output formats and training strategies.; The paper includes qualitative examples and visual illustrations (e.g., Figure 1 on page 1 and Table 3 on page 5) that make the proposed use case concrete and easy to understand.

Weaknesses: Detection performance, while promising, is still moderate for a safety-critical application; many categories remain difficult, especially rarer or more subjective ones.; Inter-annotator agreement is only moderate/fair for several tasks (e.g., overall alpha 0.54 for span annotation, lower for abstraction/importance judgments), reflecting substantial subjectivity and possible label noise.; The user study is relatively small (21 participants) and limited to Reddit users, which constrains generalizability.; The abstraction evaluation relies heavily on human judgments and LLM-generated training targets, which may encode stylistic or normative biases about what counts as an appropriate abstraction.; The importance-rating evaluation uses a permissive correctness criterion (matching any of three annotations), so the reported ~80% accuracy may overstate practical reliability.; Some categories are extremely sparse (e.g., name/contact), requiring hybrid handling rather than a unified model, which weakens the claim of broad coverage.; The paper does not evaluate the full end-to-end pipeline in realistic deployment settings, where detection errors would propagate into abstraction.; There is limited discussion of subgroup effects or whether abstraction quality differs across sensitive populations or disclosure types.; The restricted release policy is understandable ethically, but it may reduce reproducibility and broader community validation.; Comparisons to stronger modern baselines for generation and editing could be more extensive, especially given the rapid evolution of instruction-tuned LLMs.

Soundness Issues: The methodology is generally sound, but several core evaluations depend on subjective annotations with only fair agreement, especially for importance rating (Krippendorff’s alpha 0.29) and some abstraction dimensions. This makes conclusions directionally credible but somewhat noisy.; The abstraction training data is generated automatically by GPT-3.5/GPT-4 rather than by humans. This is pragmatic, but it means the model is partly learning to imitate another model’s notion of privacy-preserving rewriting rather than a validated human gold standard.; The importance-rating metric counts a prediction as correct if it matches any annotator label. Given frequent disagreement, this evaluation may inflate performance relative to a stricter majority-label or calibrated ordinal metric.; The user study provides useful qualitative evidence, but the paper does not quantify whether model outputs actually reduce risky disclosures in real posting behavior over time.; For detection, the choice of partial span F1 is reasonable, but because the task is intended for user-facing highlighting, more analysis of precision/recall tradeoffs and false-positive burden would strengthen the practical claims.; The paper’s claim that failures tend toward 'overprotection' may not always hold in practice; missed disclosures could still create meaningful privacy risk, and over-highlighting may reduce usability.
