This paper tackles an important and underexplored problem at the intersection of NLP, privacy, and HCI: helping users recognize and mitigate privacy risks in online self-disclosure. The paper’s central idea is compelling and practically meaningful. Rather than focusing only on classic PII or coarse document-level profiling, it aims to identify fine-grained self-disclosure spans in user-authored text and then assist users in rewriting those spans into less specific but still useful forms. This is a strong framing, and the paper makes a credible case that user-side privacy support is both societally valuable and technically distinct from existing anonymization or PII-masking work.

The paper’s main contributions are substantial. First, it introduces a 19-category taxonomy of self-disclosure spanning both demographic attributes and personal experiences. The taxonomy itself is useful and appears broader than most prior work. The statistics in Table 1 on page 2 show a reasonably diverse distribution, with categories ranging from location, age, and relationship status to health, family, occupation, mental health, education, and finance. Second, the authors build a manually annotated corpus of 2.4K Reddit posts with 4.8K disclosure spans, which is a meaningful resource for this problem. Third, they formulate and evaluate not only span-level self-disclosure detection, but also a novel self-disclosure abstraction task and an importance-rating task to help decide what should be abstracted. Finally, they complement standard model evaluation with a user study involving real Reddit users, which significantly strengthens the practical relevance of the work.

I found the paper especially strong in its problem formulation. The distinction between self-disclosure detection and generic PII detection is well motivated. Many privacy tools focus on explicit identifiers, but the examples throughout the paper make clear that privacy risk often comes from softer, contextual disclosures such as age, relationship status, mental health, or occupation. The span-level framing is also appropriate: users need actionable feedback at the phrase level, not just a binary label on a whole post. The abstraction task is similarly well conceived. The comparison on page 5 and Table 3 clearly explains why span-level abstraction is preferable to sentence-level paraphrasing or abstraction when the goal is to preserve surrounding text and authorial intent.

The dataset creation effort appears careful and thoughtful. The annotation process is described in reasonable detail, including pilot studies, in-house annotators, contextual span selection guidelines, and adjudication for later batches. The decision to annotate spans with contextual wording rather than minimal entities is sensible for downstream usability. The inter-annotator agreement is moderate overall, with Krippendorff’s alpha of 0.54 for the main corpus and category-level variation reported in the appendix. Given the inherent ambiguity of self-disclosure boundaries and categories, this seems acceptable. In fact, the paper is commendably honest that some of these judgments are subjective, and later sections on importance rating reinforce that subjectivity is intrinsic to the task rather than merely annotation noise.

The detection experiments are solid. Fine-tuned encoder models outperform prompted GPT-4 on span extraction, which is plausible and useful to show. The best partial span F1 is around 65.7 for DeBERTa, with sentence-level segmentation helping substantially according to Table 7 in the appendix. The per-class breakdown in Table 2 on page 3 and Table 8 in the appendix is informative and shows both strengths and remaining challenges. Some categories such as age, age/gender, and race/nationality are handled relatively well, while others such as relationship status, pet, and some health-related categories remain harder. This heterogeneity is unsurprising and actually increases confidence that the evaluation is realistic rather than overly curated. I also appreciated the small out-of-domain test on ShareGPT conversations on page 4 and Table 9 in the appendix; although limited, it suggests some transfer beyond Reddit.

The HCI component is a major strength. Too many papers in this area stop at benchmark metrics, whereas this work actually studies how users perceive the tool. The interviews with 21 Reddit users are well integrated into the paper’s narrative rather than treated as an afterthought. The finding that 82% of participants had a positive outlook and that many valued word-level highlighting and fine-grained categories is encouraging. More importantly, the user study directly motivates the abstraction and importance-rating tasks. This iterative, human-centered design loop is one of the most compelling aspects of the paper. The qualitative feedback about oversensitivity, personalization, and the desire for rewriting support feels authentic and helps justify the broader system design.

The abstraction task is novel and, in my view, one of the paper’s most interesting contributions. The examples in Figure 1 on page 1 and Table 11 in the appendix make the task concrete and intuitive. The authors also do a good job distinguishing it from paraphrasing and sentence-level abstraction. The experimental setup is thoughtful: they use LLM-generated training data, explore multiple fine-tuning strategies on Llama-2-7B, and evaluate both automatically and with human judgments. The human evaluation is the right emphasis here, since privacy increase and utility preservation are difficult to capture with lexical overlap metrics alone. The reported results suggest that the best models achieve moderate privacy increase while preserving high utility and generating diverse alternatives. Figure 2 on page 7 is particularly helpful in summarizing this tradeoff. This is not a solved problem, but the paper convincingly demonstrates feasibility.

The importance-rating task is also a useful addition. It addresses a real usability issue surfaced in the user study: not every disclosure should be treated equally, and over-highlighting can reduce trust. Framing the task as low/moderate/high importance for contextual understanding is sensible. The reported accuracy around 80%, comparable to GPT-3.5, is promising. I also appreciated the discussion in the appendix showing why this task is inherently subjective. That honesty improves the paper.

The paper is generally well written and easy to follow. The progression from taxonomy and corpus, to detection, to user study, to abstraction, to importance rating is coherent. The examples are effective, and the tables and figures are informative. In particular, Table 1 on page 2, Table 3 on page 5, Table 4 on page 6, and Figures 2 and 3 on page 7 all contribute meaningfully to understanding the work.

I do have some concerns, though none are fatal. The first is evaluation scale and reliability for the generation tasks. The abstraction experiments rely heavily on human evaluation over 60 sampled instances, and agreement is only fair to moderate in several settings. This is understandable given the subjectivity of the task, but it still means the margins between methods should be interpreted cautiously. Relatedly, the automatic metrics for abstraction are not especially informative, and the paper itself seems to recognize this. A stronger evaluation protocol in future work could include larger-scale pairwise preference studies with external users, or downstream task-based evaluation of whether users actually prefer and adopt the suggested rewrites.

Second, while the user study is a major strength, it is still relatively small and limited to Reddit users recruited through Prolific. The paper acknowledges this. It would be useful to know more about how perceptions vary across user groups, disclosure types, or subreddit norms. The discussion on page 9 about subreddit-specific expectations is insightful and suggests an important direction for personalization. Still, for a first paper in this area, the current study is already quite valuable.

Third, some methodological choices could be clarified further. For example, the corpus construction begins from 10K sampled posts and then narrows to 2,415 posts containing self-disclosures, but it would help to know more about prevalence by subreddit or topic and whether the final dataset is skewed toward certain communities. Similarly, the decision to exclude NSFW/Over_18 posts is understandable from an ethics and annotation standpoint, but it may omit some of the most privacy-sensitive disclosures. This is not a flaw so much as a scope choice, but it is worth noting when interpreting generality.

Fourth, the paper’s release policy is restrictive: the corpus and models are only shared upon request under ethical guidelines. I understand and largely sympathize with the motivation, especially given the sensitivity of the data. Still, this does reduce reproducibility and broader community access. It would help if the authors could provide as much reproducibility support as possible short of full public release, such as annotation guidelines, prompts, code, model cards, and perhaps a sanitized benchmark subset if feasible.

Fifth, there is an interesting conceptual tension around “privacy increase” versus semantic distortion. Some examples in Table 11 in the appendix are excellent, but a few abstractions seem to shift meaning somewhat, especially when replacing concrete mental-health or medication disclosures with broader emotional descriptions. This is partly unavoidable, and the paper does evaluate utility preservation, but it would be useful to discuss more explicitly when abstraction crosses into reinterpretation. In practical deployment, users should likely remain fully in control and treat outputs as suggestions rather than automatic edits.

Despite these limitations, I think the paper is a strong contribution. It opens up a genuinely new problem space, supports it with a nontrivial annotated resource, demonstrates competitive span-level detection, proposes a novel abstraction task with promising results, and grounds the whole effort in a real user study. The interdisciplinary combination of NLP and HCI is particularly effective here. Even where the results are not yet definitive, the paper establishes a clear and valuable research agenda.

My overall assessment is very positive. This is timely, original, and practically important work with solid empirical support and unusually strong user-centered validation for an NLP paper. I would be supportive of acceptance.

For improvement, I would encourage the authors to strengthen three areas in a revision: expand discussion of dataset composition and potential biases; clarify how abstraction quality should be interpreted when privacy and semantic fidelity conflict; and provide as much reproducibility infrastructure as ethically possible. A future end-to-end evaluation of the full pipeline with users choosing among detection, importance rating, and abstraction suggestions would also be an excellent next step.

In summary, this is a thoughtful and impactful paper. Its core ideas are novel, the execution is careful, and the practical relevance is high. I expect it to be of interest to researchers in NLP, privacy, HCI, and responsible AI.
