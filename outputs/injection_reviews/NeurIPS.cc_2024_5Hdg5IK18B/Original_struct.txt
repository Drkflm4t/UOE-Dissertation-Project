Summary: The paper studies how QAOA performance depends on mixer Hamiltonian design and circuit depth, arguing that the best parameter-grouping strategy is both problem- and depth-dependent. It presents a theoretical analysis based on symmetry/effective-dimension arguments, then proposes MG-Net, a two-stage learning framework that first trains a cost estimator to predict achievable QAOA performance for a problem, mixer, and depth, and then trains a generator to output mixer Hamiltonians conditioned on the problem instance and allowed depth. Experiments on weighted Max-Cut and TFIM, including simulations up to 64 qubits, show improved approximation ratios over standard QAOA, ma-QAOA, ADAPT-QAOA, and some classical baselines in the reported settings.

Strengths: Addresses an important practical issue in QAOA: tailoring ansatz/mixer design to limited circuit depth rather than assuming a fixed standard mixer.; Combines theory and method: the paper gives a symmetry/effective-dimension perspective on why different parameter-grouping strategies may be preferable at different depths, then uses this insight to motivate the learning framework.; The MG-Net architecture is reasonably well thought out, with a two-stage estimator-generator design that reduces the need for labels of globally optimal mixers.; Conditioning on circuit depth is a useful and practically relevant idea, and the empirical results suggest the generated mixers adapt meaningfully as depth changes.; Experiments cover two problem families (weighted Max-Cut and TFIM), include ablations/appendix analyses, and report results on larger simulated instances up to 64 qubits.; The paper is generally clear in motivation and includes implementation details, appendices, and code release information.

Weaknesses: The theoretical claims appear stronger than what is fully justified. The convergence argument relies on assumptions such as Haar-distributed unitaries/over-parameterized regimes that are far from standard finite-depth QAOA practice, so the connection to the concrete MG-Net setting is indirect.; The main theorem only distinguishes FG/PG/NG grouping at a coarse level, whereas MG-Net searches over richer mixer/operator choices; thus the theory does not tightly characterize the actual learned design space.; Experimental evaluation is entirely simulation-based and does not test noisy hardware, despite the motivation being device depth constraints.; The training labels for the cost estimator are based on limited optimization restarts/epochs, so the predicted 'minimum achievable cost' may be a noisy proxy for true achievable performance.; The 64-qubit training setup uses a divide-and-conquer surrogate based on smaller subcircuits, which may not faithfully reflect the original large-scale QAOA landscape and weakens claims about true 64-qubit optimization.; Some baselines seem under-optimized or unevenly compared (e.g., very poor 64-qubit ma-QAOA/QAOA numbers, missing ADAPT-QAOA entries), making it hard to assess whether gains come from better mixer design or from differing optimization difficulty/budget.; The search space for mixers is restricted mostly to single-qubit X/Y choices plus grouping, so the claim of generating 'optimal mixer Hamiltonians' is overstated relative to the actual hypothesis class.

Soundness Issues: Theorem 3.1 depends on a convergence lemma assuming Haar-distributed unitaries and an over-parameterization threshold; finite-depth QAOA ansätze with structured generators are not Haar-random, so the theorem's practical applicability is limited.; The proof chain from effective dimension to approximation-ratio convergence is heuristic in spirit and may not support the strong practical conclusions drawn about mixer design for realistic QAOA instances.; The equality/ordering claims for effective dimensions across FG/PG/NG rely on symmetry/commutant arguments, but the exposition leaves open whether all assumptions hold for the generalized mixers actually used by MG-Net.; Stage-2 training optimizes the generator against a frozen learned estimator rather than true QAOA performance, so any estimator bias can directly induce suboptimal or misleading mixer generation.; Reported performance improvements may partly reflect classical optimizer behavior under fixed budgets rather than intrinsic ansatz superiority; stronger controls on optimization budget and baseline tuning are needed for a sound causal claim.; Large-scale results rely on surrogate data generation and tensor-network evaluation choices that are not fully validated against exact large-instance optimization, limiting confidence in scalability claims.
