This paper studies how to customize the mixer Hamiltonian in QAOA as a function of both the problem instance and the available circuit depth, and proposes MG-Net, a learned framework that predicts mixer structure from the problem graph and depth budget. The paper combines a theoretical discussion based on symmetry/effective-dimension arguments with a two-stage neural architecture that first learns a cost estimator for QAOA performance and then trains a generator to output mixer/operator-grouping choices that minimize the estimated cost. Empirically, the method is evaluated on weighted Max-Cut and TFIM instances, including simulations up to 64 qubits, and is reported to outperform standard QAOA, multi-angle QAOA, ADAPT-QAOA, and classical baselines on the tested settings.

Overall, I found the paper interesting, ambitious, and potentially impactful. The central idea is compelling: rather than fixing a single mixer family or manually engineering symmetry-aware parameter sharing, the method attempts to learn the appropriate mixer/operator grouping conditioned on the instance and depth. This is a meaningful problem in the QAOA literature, especially because the usefulness of additional parameters is known to depend strongly on depth and problem structure. The paper’s framing of a “depth-aware customization” problem is well motivated, and the proposed estimator-generator decomposition is a sensible way to avoid requiring labels of globally optimal mixers, which would be prohibitively expensive to obtain.

A major strength is the breadth of the contribution. The paper is not merely an empirical architecture-search paper, nor merely a theoretical note on symmetry. It tries to connect the two. The discussion in Sections 2–3 about symmetry, parameter grouping, and effective dimension gives a useful conceptual lens for why fully grouped, partially grouped, and non-grouped parameterizations may behave differently as depth changes. In particular, the message that the best grouping strategy is both problem-dependent and depth-dependent is important and aligns with practical experience in variational quantum algorithms. Even if some parts of the theory are somewhat stylized, the paper succeeds in extracting a design principle that directly motivates the learning framework.

The MG-Net framework itself is also thoughtfully designed. The two-stage training pipeline shown in Figure 2 on page 5 is clear and well motivated: first train a surrogate cost estimator from problem/mixer/depth triples, then freeze it and optimize a generator against that surrogate. This is a practical workaround for the combinatorial search space over operator types and parameter-sharing patterns. I also appreciated the decomposition of mixer generation into two subproblems—operator-type prediction and parameter-group prediction—and the graph-based encodings of both the problem and the mixer. The use of a DAG encoding for the problem unitary and a graph encoding for the mixer Hamiltonian is a reasonable attempt to obtain a unified representation across tasks.

The empirical section contains several encouraging results. The plots on pages 8–9 suggest that the learned cost estimator tracks actual QAOA performance reasonably well, and the qualitative transition in the generated number of parameters as depth increases is one of the most interesting findings in the paper. The behavior in Figure 5(a,b) on page 9—where MG-Net tends toward more expressive/non-grouped structures at small depth and more grouped structures at larger depth—nicely matches the paper’s theoretical narrative. The convergence curves in Figure 5(c) and the additional curves in Appendix E.4 on pages 27–28 are also useful, because they show not only final approximation ratios but optimization dynamics and gradient norms. The Max-Cut comparison in Table 1 on page 9 is striking, especially at 16 and 64 qubits, and the appendix results for TFIM and additional graph families further support the claim that the method is not narrowly tuned to a single toy setting.

That said, there are also several issues that should be addressed for the paper to be fully convincing.

My main concern is the strength and precision of the theoretical claims. Theorem 3.1 is presented as a convergence theorem for QAOA, but the assumptions and conclusions seem weaker and more indirect than the presentation suggests. In Appendix B, the argument relies on results for over-parameterized PQCs under Haar-like assumptions and then transfers these to QAOA via effective dimension. However, actual QAOA ansätze with structured generators are far from Haar-random, and the theorem ultimately gives only an ordering statement under sufficiently large depth and an unspecified threshold. Moreover, the equality deff(AFG)=deff(APG) while claiming improved convergence intuition from partial grouping is somewhat subtle and could confuse readers. The paper’s real theoretical contribution seems more like a qualitative principle relating symmetry/commutants/effective dimension to grouping choices, rather than a sharp predictive theorem for practical QAOA. I would encourage the authors to moderate the wording around “rigorous theoretical analysis” and clarify exactly what is proved versus what is heuristic extrapolation.

Relatedly, the notion of “effective dimension” used here is not the standard statistical learning notion but a symmetry-restricted reachable-subspace dimension. That is fine, but the exposition could be clearer about how this quantity is computed in practice for the reported experiments, especially for larger systems. Figure 5(b) on page 9 compares effective dimensions of circuits achieving high approximation ratio, but the computational procedure for obtaining these values is not sufficiently transparent in the main text. Since this quantity is central to the paper’s narrative, more explicit methodology would help.

A second major concern is evaluation fairness and reproducibility of the QAOA baselines. The reported baseline numbers in Table 1 are surprisingly poor for standard QAOA and especially ma-QAOA at 16 and 64 qubits, with ma-QAOA reported as 0.0 at 64 qubits. This may reflect optimization difficulty under the authors’ training budget, but then the comparison is partly between “which ansatz is easiest to optimize under this specific optimizer/initialization/epoch budget” rather than intrinsic representational quality. Since MG-Net is explicitly designed to improve trainability, this is not illegitimate, but the paper should be much more careful in stating that the gains are under a fixed optimization protocol. In particular, all QAOA variants appear to be optimized for only 40 epochs with Adam at learning rate 0.15. For difficult nonconvex landscapes, this may be insufficient or poorly tuned for some baselines. A stronger comparison would include more robust baseline tuning, multiple optimizers, or parameter initialization heuristics known in the QAOA literature. Without that, the very large margins over QAOA/ma-QAOA are harder to interpret.

A third concern is the training-data construction and possible leakage of computational effort. The paper emphasizes that MG-Net avoids collecting labels of optimal mixers, which is true, but Stage 1 still requires labels equal to the minimum cost achievable by a QAOA instance after repeated optimization over parameters. According to Appendix D.1, each label is obtained by running the same QAOA circuit 10 times for 40 epochs and taking the minimum. This is not cheap, and it means the method shifts substantial cost into offline surrogate construction. That may still be a good tradeoff, but the paper should quantify it more explicitly. For example, how many candidate mixers per problem/depth are sampled? What is the total offline cost relative to simply running a stronger baseline search online? The claim of efficiency would be much stronger with a wall-clock or query-complexity accounting.

The 64-qubit experiments also deserve more scrutiny. The paper states in Appendix D.1 that the training data for 64-node Max-Cut are built using a divide-and-conquer strategy inspired by QAOA-in-QAOA, where 64-node graphs are partitioned into eight 8-node subgraphs and labels are derived from independent 8-qubit QAOAs, while testing uses tensor-network simulation of the original 64-qubit QAOA. This is an interesting engineering choice, but it introduces a mismatch between the training surrogate and the true target problem. It would be helpful to know how sensitive the results are to this approximation, and whether the strong 64-qubit gains persist when training and evaluation are aligned on smaller sizes where exact simulation is possible. As written, the 64-qubit results are promising but somewhat indirect.

There are also some modeling choices that seem under-justified. The mixer search space in the main experiments is restricted to single-qubit X/Y operators with parameter grouping, later extended in the appendix to include XX/YY. This is reasonable as a first step, but the paper sometimes speaks broadly about “optimal mixer Hamiltonians,” which overstates what is actually searched. The initial state is fixed to |+>^N even when the predicted mixer includes Y terms or more general structures; Appendix D.4 acknowledges that this may not be the ground state of the mixer and appeals to counterdiabatic intuition. This may work empirically, but it weakens the connection to standard QAOA/alternating-operator design principles and deserves more discussion.

I also found some presentation issues. The paper occasionally conflates “cost,” “loss,” and “approximation ratio,” sometimes with sign changes that are not always easy to track. For example, the estimator predicts “minimum loss” while the main QAOA objective earlier is framed as maximizing expectation value. The notation around Eqns. (6)–(7) and the figures would benefit from a cleaner, consistent convention. There are also several grammatical issues and a few statements that are too strong relative to the evidence, especially in the abstract and contributions list. These are fixable, but tightening the exposition would improve readability.

Despite these concerns, I think the paper has substantial merit. The problem is important, the proposed framework is novel in the QAOA context, and the empirical evidence—while not yet airtight—suggests a genuinely useful phenomenon: the best mixer/parameter-sharing structure changes systematically with depth, and a learned generator can exploit this. I especially like that the paper goes beyond simply adding more parameters and instead tries to learn when to add or remove parameter correlations. That is a more nuanced and practically relevant contribution than many prior “larger ansatz” proposals.

To strengthen the paper, I would recommend the following concrete revisions. First, clarify the theorem statements and temper claims so that the theory is presented as motivation plus partial justification, not as a complete convergence characterization of practical QAOA. Second, provide a more careful baseline protocol, ideally with stronger tuning and perhaps warm-start or Fourier/interpolation heuristics for standard QAOA and ma-QAOA. Third, quantify the offline computational cost of Stage 1 dataset construction and compare it to alternative search procedures. Fourth, explain more explicitly how effective dimension is computed in experiments and how the 64-qubit divide-and-conquer training labels relate to the final evaluation target. Fifth, if space permits, include an ablation on the importance of operator-type prediction versus parameter-group prediction, since it is currently unclear which component contributes most of the gain.

In summary, this is a creative and timely paper that tackles an important bottleneck in QAOA design with a technically interesting combination of symmetry analysis and learned mixer generation. While I have reservations about the sharpness of the theory and the fairness/interpretation of some empirical comparisons, I believe the core idea is strong and the results are sufficiently promising to merit serious consideration. With clearer positioning and somewhat stronger experimental validation, this could become a valuable contribution at the intersection of quantum optimization and machine learning. My overall assessment is positive.
