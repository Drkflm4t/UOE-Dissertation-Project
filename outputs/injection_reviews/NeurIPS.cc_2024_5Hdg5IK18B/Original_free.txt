This paper studies how to customize QAOA mixer Hamiltonians as a function of both the problem instance and the allowed circuit depth, and proposes MG-Net, a learned generator that predicts a mixer design intended to improve approximation quality and trainability. The paper combines a theoretical discussion centered on symmetry, parameter grouping, and effective dimension with a two-stage learning framework: first train a cost estimator that predicts the achievable QAOA objective for a given problem, depth, and mixer; then train a generator against this frozen estimator to output a mixer for unseen instances. Experiments on weighted Max-Cut and TFIM, including simulations up to 64 qubits, suggest improved approximation ratios over standard QAOA and several baselines.

Overall, the paper is ambitious and addresses an important problem. The practical tension between expressive QAOA variants and hardware-limited depth is real, and the idea of making mixer design depth-aware is interesting. The combination of a learned surrogate objective with a generator is also a sensible way to avoid exhaustive labeling of optimal mixers. The empirical results are promising at a high level, especially the qualitative trend that the learned mixer becomes less parameter-rich as depth increases, which matches the paper’s intended narrative. That said, I have substantial concerns about the rigor of the theoretical claims, the fairness and clarity of the empirical evaluation, and the exact scope of what has been demonstrated. In its current form, I find the paper intriguing but not yet fully convincing.

The main strength of the paper is the problem formulation. Rather than proposing yet another fixed QAOA variant, the authors ask a more general question: given a problem class and a depth budget, can one automatically choose a mixer structure that balances expressivity and trainability? This is a worthwhile direction. The framework in Figure 2 on page 5 is conceptually clean: a cost estimator is trained in Stage 1, then a mixer generator is optimized in Stage 2 using the estimator as a differentiable proxy. The decomposition of mixer design into operator-type prediction and parameter-grouping prediction is also reasonable and gives the method some interpretability. The examples in Table 4 on page 27, where the predicted mixer transitions from largely ungrouped at small depth to more grouped at large depth, are intuitive and align with the motivating hypothesis.

A second positive aspect is that the paper attempts to connect the method to theory rather than presenting it as a purely heuristic neural architecture search procedure. The discussion around symmetry and effective dimension is potentially valuable. In particular, the claim that parameter grouping can reduce effective dimension and thereby improve convergence in sufficiently overparameterized regimes is plausible and consistent with prior literature on symmetric ansätze and trainability. The empirical plots in Figure 5(a,b) on page 9 qualitatively support the intended story: at small depth the learned circuits use more parameters, while at larger depth they move toward more grouping.

However, the theoretical section is currently much weaker than the paper claims. Theorem 3.1 is presented as a convergence theorem for QAOA, but the actual statement is narrow and relies on assumptions that are either unrealistic or insufficiently justified. Most importantly, Lemma B.5 in the appendix assumes the unitary operator follows the Haar distribution over special unitaries, and then imports a convergence result from overparameterized VQEs. This is a very strong assumption that does not resemble the structured QAOA ansätze considered here. QAOA circuits with grouped or ungrouped parameters are highly constrained and certainly not Haar-random in any meaningful sense. As a result, the theorem does not really establish convergence behavior of practical QAOA instances; it instead gives a heuristic transfer of an existing result under assumptions that are disconnected from the actual model class. The paper should be much more careful in how it frames this contribution. Calling it a “rigorous theoretical analysis on the convergence of QAOA” overstates what is proved.

Relatedly, the equality and inequality statements about effective dimensions in Theorem 3.1 and Lemma B.6 are not fully convincing as written. The claim that fully grouped and partially grouped ansätze have equal effective dimension, while non-grouped has larger or equal effective dimension, depends delicately on the invariant subspace actually reached from the chosen initial state and on the exact symmetry structure of the problem Hamiltonian. The appendix argument proceeds through commutants and representation decompositions, but several steps are too compressed and appear to conflate dimension of Lie algebra, dimension of representation, and effective dimension of the reachable invariant subspace. Equation (27) on page 18 is especially problematic: it asserts an ordering of effective dimensions from an inclusion relation between commutants, but the mapping from commutant size to the specific invariant subspace dimension relevant to optimization is not established carefully enough. At minimum, the theorem should be weakened, or the proof substantially expanded with precise assumptions and examples.

There is also a conceptual mismatch between the theory and the method. The theory discusses three parameter-grouping regimes over essentially fixed operator sets: fully grouped, partially grouped, and non-grouped. MG-Net, however, searches over both grouping and operator types, including X/Y choices and, in an appendix experiment, even XX/YY operators. The theoretical analysis does not cover this broader search space. Thus the theory does not really justify the full method, only a restricted intuition about one component of it. That is acceptable if stated honestly, but the paper currently presents the theory as stronger support than it actually provides.

On the empirical side, the results are promising but difficult to interpret confidently because several evaluation choices are underexplained or potentially unfair. The most serious issue is the comparison protocol for baselines. Table 1 on page 9 reports extremely poor performance for standard QAOA and ma-QAOA on 64-qubit Max-Cut, including 0.19 for QAOA and 0.0 for ma-QAOA, while the proposed method achieves 0.96. Such a dramatic gap is surprising and demands very careful scrutiny. Yet the optimization budget appears fixed at only 40 epochs with Adam and 10 random restarts, as stated on pages 8 and 25. This may be far from sufficient for strong baselines, especially ma-QAOA with many parameters. If the paper’s central claim is that MG-Net improves trainability under a fixed optimization budget, that should be stated explicitly and evaluated as such. If instead the claim is that it improves the best achievable approximation ratio, then the baselines need much stronger tuning and perhaps problem-specific initialization strategies. As written, it is unclear whether the comparison reflects intrinsic ansatz quality or simply optimization difficulty under a limited budget.

The treatment of ADAPT-QAOA is also not fully fair. The paper notes in Appendix C.2 that ADAPT-QAOA incurs higher measurement cost due to gradient evaluation over an operator pool, which is true, but then compares only final approximation ratios without a careful accounting of search/optimization cost. Moreover, the operator pool used for ADAPT-QAOA is not clearly specified in the main text, and the table even omits the 64-qubit result with a dash. Since MG-Net itself requires a nontrivial offline training phase and a labeled dataset for the cost estimator, a fair comparison should distinguish offline training cost, online inference cost, and online QAOA optimization cost for all methods.

A related concern is that the paper repeatedly emphasizes “efficiency,” but the actual computational burden of MG-Net may be substantial. Stage 1 requires constructing a labeled dataset where each label is the minimum cost found by repeatedly optimizing a QAOA instance over multiple random initializations. Appendix D.1 states that for each sample the circuit is run 10 times and optimized for 40 epochs, and that the training set contains 100 instances per task with multiple sampled mixers. This is expensive. For 64-qubit training, the paper further uses a divide-and-conquer surrogate based on QAOA-in-QAOA over 8-node subgraphs rather than direct simulation. This is a pragmatic choice, but it means the training labels for large instances are not the true 64-qubit QAOA performance. The paper should be much more explicit that the 64-qubit training regime relies on an approximation pipeline, and should discuss how errors in this surrogate affect the learned generator.

The 64-qubit evaluation itself needs clarification. Appendix D.1 says that for testing on unknown graphs, tensor network simulations are used to estimate the performance of the original 64-qubit QAOA. But the training labels for 64-qubit graphs come from decomposed 8-qubit subproblems with at least 8 independent parameters. This creates a train-test mismatch: the generator is trained against a surrogate objective that may not faithfully reflect the true large-scale circuit landscape. The paper should quantify this mismatch, for example by correlating surrogate labels with true tensor-network-evaluated performance on a subset of 64-qubit instances.

The cost estimator is central to the method, but its evaluation is not yet sufficient. Figure 4 on page 8 shows scatter plots of predicted versus minimum cost and depth-wise trends, which is useful, but the paper does not report standard regression metrics such as R^2, Spearman rank correlation, or calibration error. Since Stage 2 depends entirely on the estimator as a surrogate objective, ranking quality is arguably more important than MSE. The ranking loss in Equation (6) is a good idea, but the paper should report whether the estimator preserves ordering among candidate mixers for the same problem and depth. More importantly, there should be an ablation showing what happens if the estimator is weaker, or if Stage 2 is trained with only MSE versus MSE plus ranking loss. Without this, it is hard to know whether the success comes from the architecture, the surrogate quality, or simply the restricted search space.

The search space itself is narrower than the framing suggests. In the main method, the mixer Hamiltonian is restricted to sums of single-qubit X or Y operators with learned grouping. This is a meaningful extension over standard QAOA, but still far from “optimal mixer Hamiltonians” in any broad sense. The appendix briefly considers an extended pool with XX and YY operators in Figure 13 on page 28, but this is not integrated into the main evaluation. Since many powerful QAOA variants rely on problem-informed multi-qubit mixers or constrained alternating operators, the paper should be more precise in describing the scope: MG-Net learns within a predefined operator pool, not over general mixers. This does not invalidate the contribution, but the claims should be narrowed.

I also have concerns about the problem encoder and invariance claims. The paper states that challenge C-1 requires a universal representation that retains essential properties such as permutation invariance. Yet the proposed problem encoder converts the problem Hamiltonian into a DAG representing a quantum circuit, with edges encoding temporal order of gate execution (pages 22–23). This representation depends on the chosen decomposition and ordering of terms in the Hamiltonian. It is not obvious that it is permutation-invariant or even canonical. Two isomorphic graphs or equivalent Hamiltonians with different term orderings could map to different DAGs. Since the method’s generalization hinges on the encoder, the paper should either prove or empirically test invariance/equivariance properties, or at least explain how a canonical ordering is enforced.

There are also several methodological details that need clarification. The initial state is always fixed to |+>^N even when the learned mixer is not the standard transverse-field mixer. Page 25 acknowledges that this is not the ground state of the predicted mixer and appeals to counterdiabatic intuition. This is interesting, but it also means the method is not really learning the full QAOA ansatz design, only the mixer used in the alternating evolution while keeping the initialization fixed. Since the initial state and mixer are usually coupled in alternating operator ansätze, this design choice deserves more discussion and ideally an ablation comparing fixed |+>^N against initializing in the ground state of the learned mixer when feasible.

The presentation of approximation ratio for TFIM is somewhat confusing. In Section 5, TFIM is treated analogously to Max-Cut, but TFIM is a ground-state energy problem rather than a combinatorial optimization problem with a natural nonnegative objective. The paper should define clearly how the reported “approximation ratio” is computed for TFIM, especially since the Hamiltonian includes both ZZ and X terms and may have negative energies. The main text defines approximation ratio as C(z)/C_max for nonnegative objectives, which does not directly transfer. This ambiguity affects interpretation of Table 3 in Appendix E.1.

Some reported numbers also raise questions. In Table 1 on page 9, the standard deviation for the proposed method at 16 qubits is 0.152, which is quite large relative to the mean 0.95 and larger than some baselines, while at 6 qubits it is tiny. It would help to know whether this variance is across problem instances, random seeds, or both. Likewise, the 64-qubit greedy baseline is reported as a single number 0.79 without uncertainty, unlike the smaller cases. The evaluation protocol should be standardized across methods and sizes.

The paper would benefit from stronger ablations. At minimum, I would like to see: an ablation on operator-type learning versus grouping-only learning; an ablation on Stage 2 versus simply selecting the best mixer from a sampled candidate pool using the cost estimator; an ablation on the ranking loss term; an ablation on depth embedding; and an ablation on the problem encoder representation. The appendix includes only a small depth-embedding comparison in Table 5 on page 29. Given the complexity of the framework, more component-wise evidence is needed.

I also think the paper should compare against simpler heuristics inspired by its own theory. For example, if the main insight is that small depth prefers less grouping and large depth prefers more grouping, then a hand-designed schedule that interpolates between NG and FG as a function of p would be a natural baseline. Similarly, a symmetry-aware partially grouped ansatz from prior work should be included more prominently, since the theory explicitly discusses PG but the main experiments compare mostly FG, NG/ma-QAOA, and ADAPT-QAOA. Without a strong PG baseline, it is hard to assess whether the neural generator is necessary or whether a simpler symmetry-based rule would suffice.

The writing is generally understandable, but there are places where terminology is imprecise or inconsistent. The paper sometimes refers to “minimum cost” and elsewhere to maximizing the QAOA objective; signs appear to flip between sections. Figure 4 on page 8 discusses “estimated loss” while earlier sections define maximizing F_p. The theorem section also mixes convergence in overlap, expectation value, and approximation ratio. These are related but not interchangeable. Tightening notation and sign conventions would improve readability and reduce confusion.

In summary, I think the paper contains a genuinely interesting idea and some encouraging empirical evidence, but the current version overclaims on theory and underdocuments key experimental choices. The strongest contribution is the depth-aware learned mixer design framework, not the convergence theorem. If the authors reposition the theory as motivation rather than rigorous characterization, strengthen the empirical protocol, and add ablations and fairer baselines, this could become a solid contribution.

My main suggestions for improvement are as follows. First, substantially tone down or revise the theoretical claims. Clearly state the assumptions under which the convergence result holds, explain why they are only heuristic for QAOA, and separate proven statements from intuition. Second, improve baseline tuning and fairness, especially for ma-QAOA and symmetry-aware grouped variants, and report optimization budgets transparently. Third, quantify the quality of the cost estimator with standard metrics and show that better estimator quality translates to better generator performance. Fourth, clarify the 64-qubit training/evaluation pipeline and validate the surrogate labels used in training. Fifth, add ablations isolating the contributions of operator-type prediction, parameter grouping, ranking loss, and depth conditioning. Sixth, clarify the definition of approximation ratio for TFIM and standardize reporting of uncertainty.

If these issues are addressed, the paper could make a meaningful contribution at the intersection of quantum optimization and learned architecture design. As it stands, I view it as promising but not yet fully mature.
