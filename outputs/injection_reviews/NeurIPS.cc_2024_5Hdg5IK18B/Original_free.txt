Summary

This paper studies how mixer Hamiltonian design in QAOA should depend on both the problem instance and the allowed circuit depth. The authors first present a convergence-oriented analysis relating parameter grouping strategies in QAOA to symmetry/effective dimension considerations. Motivated by this, they propose MG-Net, a learned framework that takes a problem instance and a target depth p as input and outputs a customized mixer Hamiltonian, including both operator types and parameter-sharing/grouping structure. The method is trained in two stages: first a cost estimator predicts achievable QAOA performance for a given problem/mixer/depth triple; then a generator is trained against the frozen estimator to produce good mixers without requiring labels of optimal mixers. Experiments on weighted Max-Cut and TFIM, including instances up to 64 qubits, show improved approximation ratios over standard QAOA and several baselines.

Overall assessment

The paper addresses an important and timely problem in variational quantum optimization: how to adapt ansatz structure to hardware depth constraints and problem structure. The central idea—learning depth-aware mixer design rather than hand-crafting it—is interesting and potentially impactful. The paper also tries to connect the empirical method to a theoretical perspective based on symmetry and effective dimension, which is a strength.

That said, I have significant reservations about the rigor of the theoretical claims, the fairness and completeness of the empirical evaluation, and the extent to which the reported gains demonstrate practical usefulness beyond the studied simulation setting. In its current form, I find the paper promising but not fully convincing.

Strengths

1. Important problem setting
The paper targets a real bottleneck for QAOA: finite circuit depth on realistic devices. The framing that “good” mixer design should depend jointly on the task and the available depth is well motivated.

2. Interesting methodological idea
The estimator-generator decomposition is sensible. It avoids requiring labels of optimal mixers, which would be prohibitively expensive. This is a practical design choice and one of the more compelling aspects of the work.

3. Depth-aware customization
Conditioning the generator on p is a nice idea. The qualitative trend shown in Figure 5 and Table 4 (pages 9 and 27) is intuitive: shallow circuits favor less grouping / more expressivity, while deeper circuits move toward more grouped structures.

4. Broad empirical scope within simulation
The paper evaluates both Max-Cut and TFIM, includes some asymmetric and 2D variants in the appendix, and attempts larger-scale experiments up to 64 qubits.

5. Clear high-level presentation
The main pipeline is easy to follow. Figure 2 on page 5 and Figure 3 on page 7 communicate the training/inference workflow and model structure clearly.

Main concerns

1. The theoretical claims appear overstated and in parts questionable

The paper claims a “rigorous theoretical analysis” of QAOA convergence and uses this as a major motivation for MG-Net. However, Theorem 3.1 and its proof rely on assumptions and steps that seem too weak or mismatched to support the stated conclusions.

1.1 Reliance on Haar-random/unitary assumptions
Lemma B.5 assumes U(theta) follows the Haar distribution over special unitaries. This is a very strong assumption and is not representative of QAOA circuits, especially structured shallow QAOA with constrained generators and parameter sharing. The resulting convergence statement therefore does not directly characterize the actual optimization behavior of QAOA.

1.2 Equality of effective dimensions for FG and PG
Theorem 3.1 states deff(A_FG) = deff(A_PG) <= deff(A_NG). This is a strong and somewhat surprising claim. Intuitively, partial grouping can preserve symmetry while changing reachable submanifolds and optimization geometry. The proof seems to argue via commutants and invariant subspaces, but it is not clear that the equality of effective dimensions follows generally from the presented reasoning.

1.3 Convergence conclusion does not distinguish FG and PG
Equation (4) states T_PG = T_FG <= T_NG once p exceeds a threshold. But one of the paper’s motivating points is that PG can outperform FG in practice. If the theory predicts equal convergence iterations for FG and PG in the overparameterized regime, then the theory does not really explain the empirical advantage of customized grouping except indirectly through depth dependence and threshold effects. This weakens the claimed explanatory power.

1.4 Approximation ratio derivation is loose and possibly sign-confused
In Appendix B.2, the derivation connecting overlap error epsilon to approximation ratio uses lambda_1 < 0 and writes r = <psi|H_C|psi>/lambda_1. This is unusual because in the main text the approximation ratio is defined for maximization problems as F_p/C_max. For TFIM the objective is different again. The paper seems to mix minimization and maximization conventions, and it is not clear that the same ratio notion is used consistently across tasks and proofs.

1.5 “Sufficient circuit depth” threshold is not operational
The theorem introduces a deff-dependent threshold C but does not provide a usable characterization for QAOA instances. Since the practical question is exactly how to choose grouping at finite p, a theorem that only applies once p exceeds an unspecified threshold has limited actionable value.

Overall, I think the theory is better viewed as heuristic motivation than as rigorous support for the main claims. The paper should tone down the wording substantially.

2. Experimental comparisons are not fully fair or sufficiently detailed

2.1 Baseline tuning is unclear
The paper compares against QAOA, ADAPT-QAOA, and ma-QAOA, but there is not enough detail to assess whether these baselines were tuned comparably. For example:
- Were all methods given the same optimization budget?
- Were learning rates tuned per method?
- Were initialization strategies matched?
- For ADAPT-QAOA, what operator pool and stopping criteria were used?
- For ma-QAOA, were parameter-sharing reductions such as those in ref. [38] considered?

Given how sensitive QAOA performance is to optimizer settings, this matters a lot.

2.2 Some baseline results look suspiciously poor
In Table 1 on page 9, standard QAOA gets 0.35 on 16 qubits and 0.19 on 64 qubits for Max-Cut, while ma-QAOA gets 0.0 on 64 qubits. These are extremely weak numbers, especially relative to classical heuristics and to common QAOA behavior on regular graphs. This raises concerns that the optimization setup may be unfavorable to the baselines or that the evaluation metric/setup differs from standard practice.

2.3 Comparison to classical methods is limited
Only greedy and GW are included for Max-Cut. For random weighted 3-regular graphs, stronger classical heuristics are available. If the claim is practical utility, stronger classical baselines should be included, or at least the scope of the claim should be narrowed.

2.4 64-qubit training/evaluation protocol is indirect
The 64-qubit training data are constructed via decomposition into 8 subgraphs and 8-qubit QAOA simulations (Appendix D.1, page 22), while testing uses tensor network simulation. This is an interesting workaround, but it means the model is not trained on true 64-qubit optimization outcomes. The paper should discuss more carefully what exactly is being learned and whether this introduces bias.

2.5 No noise/hardware-aware evaluation
The paper motivates hardware depth constraints, but all experiments appear noiseless and use classical simulation. Since the generated mixers may involve Y operators and altered parameter-sharing patterns, hardware compilation cost and noise sensitivity could differ substantially from standard QAOA. Without even a simple noisy simulation, the practical relevance is limited.

3. Search space and ansatz design choices are restrictive

The generalized mixer in Eq. (5) only allows single-qubit X/Y operators with grouping. This is a meaningful space, but still narrow relative to the broader mixer design literature. The paper mentions possible extension to two-qubit operators in the appendix, but the main method is evaluated mostly in a restricted operator pool. Therefore “optimal mixer Hamiltonian” is too strong; it is only optimal within a limited learned search space and according to the estimator.

4. The cost estimator may be a bottleneck, but its errors are under-analyzed

The whole second stage depends on the estimator being accurate enough not just in regression but in ranking and extrapolation over the mixer space. Figure 4 on page 8 shows correlation, but:
- quantitative metrics such as R^2, Kendall/Spearman rank correlation, calibration, or top-k retrieval are missing;
- errors on out-of-distribution mixers are not analyzed;
- there is no ablation showing how generator performance depends on estimator quality.

Since the generator optimizes against the estimator, estimator bias could easily be exploited. This is a central issue and deserves deeper analysis.

5. Generalization claims are stronger than the evidence

The paper repeatedly emphasizes generalization from limited data to broad problem classes. But the training and test distributions are quite close:
- weighted 3-regular graphs for Max-Cut,
- 1D TFIM with sampled couplings/fields,
- same size regime family.
The appendix adds asymmetric graphs and 2D TFIM, which is helpful, but still limited. There is no evidence of transfer across graph families, densities, weight distributions, or substantially different Hamiltonian structures. So the generalization claim should be moderated.

Specific comments and questions

1. Definition/notation consistency
The paper alternates between maximizing objective values and minimizing “cost/loss,” and the notation around F_p, y, and approximation ratio is sometimes confusing. Please standardize this carefully.

2. Initial state choice
Appendix D.4 states the initial state is always |+>^N even when it is not the ground state of the predicted mixer. This is an important deviation from standard QAOA intuition. It would be useful to include an ablation comparing:
- fixed |+>^N,
- ground state of predicted mixer when efficiently preparable,
- perhaps a learned initial state.
This could clarify whether gains come from the mixer form itself or from a broader ansatz redesign effect.

3. Effective dimension computation
How exactly is deff computed in the experiments in Figure 5(b)? Is it exact, estimated, or derived from symmetry classes? For 64 qubits, exact computation seems infeasible. More detail is needed.

4. Parameter count versus trainability
Figure 5(a) and (c) suggest MG-Net balances expressivity and trainability. It would help to compare against simpler heuristics, e.g.:
- interpolate between FG and NG by a hand-designed grouping schedule as a function of p;
- random grouping with matched parameter count;
- symmetry-respecting grouping only.
This would show whether the learned structure is truly necessary.

5. Cost of data generation
The paper claims reduced label cost because optimal mixers are not needed, but Stage 1 still requires many QAOA optimizations to estimate achievable minima. Please report the actual computational cost of dataset construction and training, and compare it to direct search baselines.

6. ADAPT-QAOA comparison
ADAPT-QAOA is usually adaptive in operator selection and can be expensive, but the paper should specify the operator pool and whether the comparison uses the same mixer search space as MG-Net. Otherwise the comparison is hard to interpret.

7. Statistical reporting
Most results are averages over 100 instances, but confidence intervals or standard deviations are inconsistently reported. Some entries in Table 1 have no variance, and ADAPT-QAOA has missing values at 64 qubits. Please clarify.

8. Figure 4 interpretation
The text says the cost estimator predicts “high likelihood of finding the most accurate solution” for FG at p=92. This wording is vague. The figure seems to show regression of estimated versus achieved minimum cost, not a probability of exact solution.

9. Table 4 examples
The examples on page 27 are interesting, but only for 6-qubit cases. It would be more convincing to show representative learned mixers for larger instances and whether they exhibit interpretable patterns tied to graph structure.

10. Relation to prior QAS work
The related work section contrasts MG-Net with quantum architecture search methods by emphasizing generalization. This is fair, but some classical neural architecture search methods also use predictors and amortization. The paper could position itself more precisely as amortized ansatz/mixer design for QAOA.

Suggestions for improvement

1. Reframe the theory more modestly
Present the theoretical section as motivation or qualitative guidance rather than a rigorous explanation of MG-Net’s empirical success. Clarify assumptions and limitations explicitly.

2. Strengthen empirical rigor
- Tune baselines carefully and report tuning details.
- Add stronger classical baselines for Max-Cut.
- Include simple heuristic grouping baselines.
- Report wall-clock/data-generation cost.
- Add noisy simulations or compilation-aware metrics.

3. Analyze the estimator more deeply
Provide quantitative regression/ranking metrics, ablations on estimator architecture/loss, and evidence that the generator is not merely exploiting estimator artifacts.

4. Clarify the 64-qubit protocol
Explain more carefully how subgraph-based training relates to full-instance inference and what assumptions are being made.

5. Moderate claims of optimality and generalization
Replace “optimal mixer Hamiltonian” with “predicted/customized mixer within the considered search space,” and narrow generalization claims to the tested distributions unless broader evidence is added.

Recommendation

Weak Reject

Rationale

The paper has a good core idea and some promising empirical results, but I am not yet convinced by the theoretical grounding or the experimental evidence as presented. The method could become a strong contribution with a more careful and transparent evaluation, better baseline treatment, and toned-down theoretical claims. In its current form, the gap between the ambition of the claims and the strength of the evidence is too large for me to recommend acceptance.

Confidence

Medium-high. I am reasonably confident in the concerns about theory/experimental rigor, though less certain about how much of the empirical weakness could be resolved by additional details not included in the current version.
