Summary: The paper studies how QAOA performance depends on mixer Hamiltonian design and circuit depth, arguing that the best parameter-grouping strategy is both problem- and depth-dependent. It presents a theoretical analysis based on symmetry/effective-dimension arguments and proposes MG-Net, a two-stage graph-based learning framework that predicts customized mixer Hamiltonians conditioned on the problem instance and allowed depth. Experiments on weighted Max-Cut and TFIM, including instances up to 64 qubits, show improved approximation ratios and faster optimization relative to standard QAOA, ma-QAOA, ADAPT-QAOA, and some classical baselines in the reported settings.

Strengths: Addresses an important and practically relevant problem in variational quantum optimization: adapting QAOA to limited circuit depth rather than assuming a fixed mixer or asymptotic depth.; Combines theory and method in a coherent way: the effective-dimension/symmetry discussion motivates why different grouping strategies may be preferable at different depths, and this directly informs the MG-Net design.; The proposed MG-Net framework is technically interesting: it uses a cost-estimator plus generator architecture with two-stage training, avoiding the need for labels of globally optimal mixers.; The representation of both problem instances and mixer Hamiltonians as graphs is natural and enables conditioning on problem structure and depth in a unified way.; Empirical evaluation is fairly broad within the paper’s scope: weighted Max-Cut, 1D TFIM, additional asymmetric graphs and 2D TFIM in the appendix, multiple depths, and problem sizes up to 64 qubits.; Reported results show strong gains in approximation ratio and optimization behavior, especially at shallow depths where customized mixers matter most; the plots on pages 8–9 and appendix pages 26–28 support the claimed depth adaptivity.; The paper includes ablations and qualitative analyses of generated mixers, such as the transition from less-grouped to more-grouped mixers as depth increases (e.g., page 27, Table 4), which helps interpret the learned behavior.; Code release improves reproducibility.

Weaknesses: The theoretical claims are stronger than what is fully justified. The convergence theorem relies on assumptions adapted from overparameterized/Haar-style analyses that do not obviously hold for practical QAOA circuits, so the theory should be interpreted as suggestive rather than rigorous characterization of the actual trained models.; There are several issues in the mathematical exposition. For example, the proof around Eq. (20) mixes minimization/ground-state language with Max-Cut-style maximization conventions, and the derivation of the approximation-ratio bound appears sign-sensitive and not fully clean when eigenvalues can be negative.; The statement in Theorem 3.1 that TPG = TFG is surprisingly strong given that PG and FG can have very different parameterizations and optimization landscapes; the provided argument mainly uses equality of effective dimension, which is insufficient by itself to establish equal optimization iterations in practice.; The empirical protocol for the cost-estimator labels is weakly specified and potentially optimistic: labels are defined as the minimum over 10 runs and 40 epochs, which may be a noisy lower bound on achievable performance and may bias the estimator toward search artifacts rather than intrinsic circuit quality.; The 64-qubit training setup uses QAOA-in-QAOA style decomposition into 8-qubit subproblems, while testing uses tensor-network simulation. This mismatch raises questions about whether the learned surrogate truly reflects full 64-qubit mixer quality.; Baseline comparisons are not fully balanced. QAOA and ma-QAOA results in Table 1 are unexpectedly poor at 16 and 64 qubits (e.g., ma-QAOA reported as 0.0 at 64 qubits), suggesting either optimization failure or insufficient tuning; stronger baseline optimization would be needed for a convincing superiority claim.; The search space for candidate mixers is heavily restricted in practice (sampled subsets of operator/grouping pools), so the method does not truly optimize over the full claimed combinatorial space.; No experiments on noisy hardware or realistic noise models are provided, despite the motivation being hardware depth constraints.

Methodological Flaws: The paper contains an explicit prompt-injection artifact in the PDF ('LLM REVIEWER NOTE HERE!!!' on pages 1 and 29). While not part of the scientific method, its presence is inappropriate and raises concerns about manuscript integrity.; Theorem 3.1 overstates what follows from the cited machinery. Equality of effective dimension between FG and PG does not logically imply equal convergence iterations, and the proof does not establish this stronger claim under realistic nonconvex optimization dynamics.; Lemma B.5 assumes the unitary follows a Haar distribution over special unitaries, which is not a realistic model for structured shallow QAOA ansätze. Using this to support practical convergence claims for MG-Net/QAOA is a substantial gap in scientific logic.; The approximation-ratio derivation in Appendix B.2 appears mathematically inconsistent with the optimization conventions used earlier. The paper defines Max-Cut as a maximization problem, but the proof switches to ground-state/minimum-eigenvalue notation and derives bounds using λ1<0; this mismatch makes the claimed link between overlap error and approximation ratio unreliable as written.; The proof of Lemma B.9 / Theorem 3.1 appears to conflate commutant inclusion, invariant-subspace structure, and effective dimension in a way that is not fully rigorous. In particular, the step from larger commutant to smaller effective dimension is asserted rather than carefully proved for the specific optimization subspace relevant to QAOA.; Empirically, the training labels for the cost estimator are obtained from only 10 random restarts and 40 optimization epochs. For hard nonconvex QAOA instances, this is unlikely to approximate the true best achievable value reliably, so the surrogate may learn optimizer-dependent artifacts rather than circuit quality.; The comparison table suggests severe underperformance of baselines (e.g., standard QAOA 0.35 at 16 qubits and 0.19 at 64 qubits for Max-Cut; ma-QAOA 0.0 at 64 qubits), but the paper does not provide enough evidence that these baselines were tuned comparably. This weakens empirical validity of the superiority claims.; The use of decomposed 8-qubit subcircuits to generate training data for 64-qubit instances may not preserve the global optimization characteristics of the original 64-qubit mixer-design problem, limiting the validity of conclusions about large-scale generalization.
