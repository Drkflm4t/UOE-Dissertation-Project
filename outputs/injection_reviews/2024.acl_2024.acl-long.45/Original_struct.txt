Summary: This paper studies whether large language models for automated theorem proving can benefit from learning from failed proof attempts, not just successful proof paths. The authors introduce PropL, a Lean-formalized dataset of intuitionistic propositional logic theorems with proofs generated by a focused proof search procedure that records trial-and-error and backtracking information. They train TRIALMASTER on these proof trees and compare it against a conventional LLM-based prover trained only on correct proofs and used with DFS search. On in-distribution data both methods nearly saturate performance, while on an out-of-distribution split TRIALMASTER achieves higher success (88.7%) with lower search cost than the best DFS baselines. Ablations suggest that including failed search paths materially improves performance.

Strengths: The paper addresses a clear and important mismatch between training and inference in neural theorem proving: models are typically trained only on successful trajectories but used in search settings that heavily involve failure and backtracking.; The central idea is intuitive and well motivated, and the paper tests it in a controlled formal setting where correctness can be checked by Lean.; PropL appears to be a useful contribution: a scalable Lean-formalized benchmark for intuitionistic propositional logic with explicit trial-and-error traces, which are largely absent from existing open datasets.; The methodology is clearly illustrated, especially the contrast between conventional DFS-based proving and TRIALMASTER in Figures 1, 2, and 4.; The empirical results on the OOD split are compelling within the chosen domain: TRIALMASTER outperforms the DFS baseline in success rate while using fewer Lean calls than the strongest DFS configurations.; The ablation comparing training with and without trial-and-error information is strong and directly supports the paper’s main claim.; The paper is transparent about limitations, including context-length truncation and the restricted domain of propositional logic.

Weaknesses: The evaluation domain is narrow: intuitionistic propositional logic is much simpler and more structured than general theorem proving in Lean, so it is unclear how well the conclusions transfer to richer mathematical domains.; The main baseline may not be fully competitive. Comparing against only a DFS system trained on correct proofs leaves open whether stronger search methods, richer prompting, or training with error signals/backtracking-aware decoding could close the gap.; The comparison somewhat conflates training signal and inference procedure: TRIALMASTER uses a different decoding/search paradigm than the DFS baseline, making it harder to isolate whether gains come from trial-and-error supervision, the inference setup, or both.; The proof-generation procedure is not fully complete due to an added assumption that forbids certain implication reuse; although the authors say this affects only about 1 in 1000 provable theorems, it weakens the claim of completeness.; The dataset split is based on proof-length quantiles derived from generated proofs, which is reasonable, but may induce a somewhat artificial notion of OOD that is still tightly coupled to the proof generator.; The paper claims representativeness via uniform sampling over propositions using a bijection, but after filtering/splitting by proof length the effective training/test distributions may no longer be uniformly representative in a strong sense.; Some empirical details are limited: there is little discussion of variance across runs, statistical significance, or sensitivity to the choice of sampled training proofs.

Soundness Issues: The core experimental logic is sound for the stated claim within this restricted setting: Lean verifies proof steps, and the ablation on trial-and-error supervision supports the conclusion that failed-path information helps.; However, the headline comparison to DFS is not a pure apples-to-apples test of supervision because the systems differ both in training data and inference mechanics. This weakens causal attribution of gains solely to learning from failure.; The search-cost metric based on Lean calls is sensible, but it favors TRIALMASTER’s explicit backtrack tokens because they incur no Lean call; this is acceptable but should be interpreted as a system-level metric rather than a pure model-efficiency metric.; The added incompleteness in proof generation introduces a mild methodological caveat, though the reported practical impact appears small.; Claims about the model learning backtracking are supported mainly by format correctness of generated backtrack instructions and end-task performance; stronger analysis of whether backtracking decisions are semantically appropriate would strengthen the argument.
