Summary: The paper studies whether large language models for automated theorem proving benefit from training on trial-and-error trajectories rather than only successful proof paths. The authors introduce PropL, a Lean-formalized dataset of intuitionistic propositional logic theorems with proofs generated by a focused proof search procedure that includes backtracking information. They fine-tune Llama-2-7B in two settings: a conventional tactic generator trained only on correct proofs and used with DFS search, and TRIALMASTER, trained on full proof histories including failed branches and explicit backtrack instructions, and used greedily without an external search controller. On the proposed out-of-distribution split, TRIALMASTER reportedly achieves higher proof success (88.7%) with lower Lean-call search cost than the strongest DFS baselines, while both methods are near-saturated on the in-distribution split.

Strengths: The paper addresses a clear and important mismatch in theorem proving systems: training on successful traces only, while inference requires exploration and recovery from failures.; The proposed idea is intuitive and practically relevant: incorporating failed attempts and backtracking history into training data for tactic generation.; The dataset contribution is meaningful within its scope. PropL is Lean-checkable, includes trial-and-error trajectories, and is generated systematically rather than via ad hoc collection.; The experimental setup compares not only success rate but also search cost measured by number of Lean calls, which is an appropriate efficiency metric for this setting.; The ablation comparing TRIALMASTER to a model trained on correct paths only but evaluated with the same greedy inference protocol is useful and shows a large gap (88.7% vs 59.3%).; The paper is generally well motivated, and figures such as the workflow comparison on page 3 and the proof examples on pages 5 and 15 help clarify the method.

Weaknesses: The empirical scope is narrow: all results are on synthetic intuitionistic propositional logic theorems generated by the authors' own procedure, so it is unclear how much transfers to broader theorem proving domains.; The main baseline is not fully matched to the proposed method. TRIALMASTER changes both training data and inference/search procedure, while the baseline uses DFS with multi-sample search; this makes attribution of gains less clean.; The claim that PropL is 'complete, scalable, and representative' is overstated. The dataset is representative only relative to the authors' formula-generation scheme and subsequent filtering by proof length, not necessarily of realistic theorem-proving distributions.; The paper provides limited statistical rigor: no variance across random seeds, no confidence intervals, and no significance testing despite stochastic proof generation and model training.; The training-data construction appears somewhat selective: TRIALMASTER is trained on two of the shortest trial-and-error proofs among ten candidates, which may materially affect performance and complicates fairness relative to baselines.; The comparison omits stronger or more diverse baselines, such as training a model with identical data but using explicit search, or a baseline augmented with failure/error tokens but not full trajectories.; The paper does not deeply analyze failure modes, types of backtracking learned, or whether gains come from avoiding repeated failed tactics versus learning better tactic semantics.

Methodological Flaws: There is a notable confound in the central comparison: TRIALMASTER differs from the DFS baseline in both supervision and inference algorithm. Because one model is trained on trial-and-error and decoded greedily with explicit backtrack tokens, while the other is trained on successful traces and decoded with DFS over sampled tactics, the reported gains cannot be attributed solely to learning from failure.; The proof-generation procedure is not fully complete as acknowledged on page 5: the additional assumption that once an implication is picked, it cannot be used to show its premise introduces incompleteness. This weakens claims that the dataset/proof search is complete and may bias the generated trajectories.; The representativeness argument is scientifically weak. Although there is a bijection between formulas and natural numbers for fixed size, the actual train/test distribution is heavily conditioned by fixing 16 internal nodes and then filtering by proof-length quantiles. This is not equivalent to uniform or representative sampling over theorem-proving difficulty.; The OOD split is defined by proof length under the authors' own proof generator, not by semantic or structural novelty. This may overstate generalization, since test examples are still drawn from the same synthetic generation process and theorem size regime.; The stopping criteria are asymmetric in effect. TRIALMASTER is terminated on the first Lean error, whereas DFS can continue exploring alternatives after wrong tactics at a state. This makes the inference protocols qualitatively different and complicates interpretation of success/cost tradeoffs.; The evidence for 'learning backtracking capability' is weak. Showing that generated backtrack instructions have the correct format and refer to existing states does not establish that the model performs logically meaningful backtracking beyond pattern imitation.; The paper lacks mathematical validation of the claimed bijection and uniformity beyond algorithm sketches in the appendix. No formal proof is provided that the encoding/decoding procedures are mutually inverse under all stated conditions, yet this claim underpins the dataset-generation argument.
