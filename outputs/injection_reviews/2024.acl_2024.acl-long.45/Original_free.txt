Summary

This paper studies a simple but important mismatch in LLM-based theorem proving: models are typically trained only on successful proof paths, but at inference time they must explore, fail, and backtrack. The authors propose training on proof traces that include trial-and-error information, including failed branches and explicit backtracking. To enable controlled experiments, they introduce PropL, a new Lean-formalized dataset for intuitionistic propositional logic, with proofs generated by a focused proof search procedure that records both successful and failed search paths. They compare a conventional LLM+DFS baseline trained only on correct proofs against TrialMaster, a model trained on traces with trial-and-error and used in a greedy, self-contained inference loop that can emit either Lean tactics or backtrack instructions. On their OOD split, TrialMaster achieves 88.7% success, outperforming the DFS baseline across tested hyperparameters while using fewer Lean calls than the strongest DFS settings.

Overall assessment

I think the paper has a clear central idea, a well-motivated training/inference mismatch, and a reasonably convincing empirical result within the chosen domain. The paper is also commendably concrete: the figures on pages 1, 3, and 5 make the distinction between conventional DFS and the proposed approach easy to understand, and the dataset construction is described in enough detail to be reproducible. The main empirical takeaway—that learning from failed proof attempts can improve theorem proving efficiency and success—is plausible and supported by the reported results.

That said, the scope is narrow, and several aspects of the evaluation and framing limit the strength of the claims. The experiments are entirely on synthetic intuitionistic propositional logic theorems generated by a specific proof-search algorithm, and the proposed method is compared mainly against a relatively simple DFS baseline using the same backbone model. The paper would be stronger with more careful controls, more diverse baselines, and deeper analysis of what exactly is being learned from the trial-and-error traces.

Recommendation

Weak Accept

Strengths

1. Clear and important motivation
The paper identifies a genuine issue in current neural theorem proving pipelines: training on only successful trajectories while relying on search with failures at test time. This is a clean and compelling problem statement.

2. Interesting methodological idea
Training on full proof-search traces, including failed branches and backtracking, is intuitive and potentially broadly useful. The formulation is simple enough that others could adapt it to different theorem proving settings.

3. Useful dataset contribution
PropL appears to be a meaningful benchmark contribution. The authors emphasize three properties: completeness, scalability, and representativeness. The use of Lean for proof checking is a strong practical advantage. The appendix material on the proposition-to-natural-number bijection and the proof generation process is helpful.

4. Good visual exposition
The diagrams are effective. In particular:
- Figure 1 on page 1 gives an intuitive picture of how failed attempts can alter future tactic probabilities.
- Figure 2 on page 3 clearly contrasts the conventional DFS pipeline with TrialMaster’s training and inference.
- Figure 4 on page 5 concretely shows the difference between a proof trace with and without backtracking.
- Figure 5 on page 8 summarizes the OOD tradeoff between success rate and Lean-call cost.

5. Empirical gains on the target setting
The OOD results are meaningful within this benchmark. TrialMaster’s 88.7% success rate is better than the best reported DFS baseline, and the search cost comparison is favorable. The ablation in Table 3 is especially important: using the same one-tactic-at-a-time inference style but removing trial-and-error from training drops success to 59.3%, which strongly suggests the training signal matters.

Weaknesses and concerns

1. Limited external validity
The biggest limitation is that the entire study is confined to intuitionistic propositional logic with synthetic theorem generation and proofs produced by a specific focused proof search algorithm. This makes the setting clean, but also raises the possibility that the model is learning the idiosyncrasies of that search procedure rather than a generally useful notion of “learning from failure.”

The paper acknowledges this in the limitations section, but the title and some claims are broader than the evidence supports. I would encourage the authors to narrow the framing more explicitly.

2. Baseline strength is limited
The main comparison is against a conventional DFS system trained only on correct proofs. This is a reasonable baseline, but not sufficient to establish broad superiority. In particular:
- The DFS baseline samples multiple tactics once per state and then explores them with DFS. That is a fairly specific design choice.
- The proposed method gets to condition on the entire search history, including failed branches and explicit backtracking markers, whereas the baseline does not. This is the intended comparison, but it also means the methods differ in both training data and inference architecture.
- There is no comparison to a stronger search-based prover that also conditions on failure history at inference time without being trained on it, or to a model trained with error messages as in Baldur-style settings.

The paper mentions COPRA and Baldur in related work, but does not compare against analogous variants in this benchmark.

3. Potential confound: training distribution and trace format
TrialMaster is trained on traces generated by the same FPS procedure that defines the proof-search behavior in the dataset. This creates a strong procedural prior. It is not fully clear whether the gains come from:
- learning from failed branches per se,
- learning explicit backtracking syntax,
- learning the specific search policy induced by FPS,
- or simply benefiting from longer, richer supervision.

The ablations partially address this, but not enough. For example, a stronger control would train on equally long traces where failed branches are masked or replaced, or compare against a model trained on successful proofs augmented with synthetic history tokens but no failed content.

4. Evaluation protocol may favor the proposed method in some ways and disfavor it in others
The paper notes that the 1500-word limit is stricter for TrialMaster because it includes trial-and-error in the prompt. That is fair. However, other aspects may favor TrialMaster:
- TrialMaster uses greedy decoding of one action at a time, while the DFS baseline depends heavily on temperature and sampled tactic count.
- The DFS baseline is capped at 65 search steps, partly for computational reasons. This may suppress its asymptotic success rate.
- The search cost metric counts Lean calls, which is sensible, but does not count LLM inference cost. TrialMaster repeatedly re-prompts with the full history and generates full continuations, even if only the first tactic is used. That could be substantially more expensive in wall-clock time or tokens than the Lean-call metric suggests.

Since the paper’s efficiency claim is important, some accounting of token usage or end-to-end runtime would help.

5. Dataset split design is unusual and may induce artifacts
The train/test split is based on proof length quantiles under both no-trial-and-error and trial-and-error proof distributions. This is a reasonable attempt to create OOD difficulty, but it is not obvious that proof length is the right notion of distribution shift. Because theorem statements all have 16 internal nodes, the shift may mostly reflect proof-search complexity under the chosen algorithm rather than semantic novelty.

Also, the training set contains 109,887 theorems out of 200,000 sampled, while only 1,000 ID and 1,000 OOD examples are used for testing. It would be useful to know variance across random splits or seeds.

6. Some claims about representativeness are overstated
The paper repeatedly says PropL is “representative of all intuitionistic propositional logic theorems” because propositions are uniformly sampled via a bijection with natural numbers. This is too strong. Uniformity over formula encodings with fixed internal-node count is not the same as representativeness for theorem proving difficulty, proof structure, or natural theorem distributions. Moreover, the dataset is later filtered by proof length, which further changes the distribution.

I suggest softening this language.

7. Incompleteness in proof generation
On page 5, the authors state that they impose an additional assumption for runtime: once an implication is picked, it cannot be used to show its premise. They note this introduces incompleteness but affects only about 1 in 1000 provable theorems sampled. This is probably acceptable for dataset generation, but it should be discussed more carefully because it means the benchmark is not exactly the full logic under standard proof search.

8. Missing statistical rigor
The paper reports point estimates only. There are no confidence intervals, no multiple-seed runs, and no significance tests. Given that some differences are small (for example, 88.7% vs 88.4%), uncertainty matters.

Detailed comments

1. Method clarity
The core method is understandable, but a few details should be clarified:
- During TrialMaster inference, the model outputs the “entire proof path after,” but only the first tactic is used. How exactly is decoding terminated? Is there a stop token? Is the full continuation generated until EOS or length cap?
- How are backtrack instructions represented in the tokenizer and prompt? Are they natural language strings, structured tags, or canonical templates?
- What is the exact prompt format for states and tactics? This matters because formatting may strongly influence learnability.

2. Training data selection
On page 6, the paper says TrialMaster is trained on “two out of the shortest five among the ten proofs” for each theorem, while page 9 says “two out of the shortest four” in the exploratory study description of the main model. This inconsistency should be fixed.

3. Comparison fairness
The baseline uses one proof per theorem without trial-and-error, while TrialMaster uses two traces per theorem with trial-and-error. Even if the theorem set is the same, the amount of supervision differs. A fairer comparison would control for total token budget or number of training examples.

4. Search cost metric
Counting Lean calls is reasonable because Lean checking is a major bottleneck, but it is not the whole story. TrialMaster may use fewer Lean calls while consuming more LLM tokens due to long prompts containing full search history. Please report:
- average prompt length during inference,
- total generated tokens,
- wall-clock runtime if possible.

5. Error handling
TrialMaster fails immediately if any tactic induces a Lean error. This is a harsh policy. Why not allow the model to recover by backtracking after an error? Since the whole point is learning from failure, this seems like a missed opportunity and may understate the method’s potential.

6. Stronger ablations would help
The current ablations are useful but incomplete. I would like to see:
- Train with backtracking instructions but without failed branch states.
- Train with failed branch states but without explicit backtrack tokens.
- Train on traces where failed branches are shuffled or corrupted, to test whether semantic failure information matters.
- Compare greedy TrialMaster against a version that can sample or beam-search over backtrack/tactic outputs.
- Compare against a DFS baseline that is also given textual history of failed tactics in the prompt.

7. Interpretation of the “long proof” result
Table 4 shows that training on longer trial-and-error proofs hurts performance. This is interesting, but the explanation that “too many failed search paths may lower the quality of the training data” is speculative. It could also be due to context dilution, optimization difficulty, or mismatch between training and inference lengths. Some analysis of trace lengths and token distributions would help.

8. Relation to prior work
The paper positions itself against Baldur and COPRA, but the distinction could be sharpened. In particular, Baldur uses proof assistant error information, while this paper uses complete proof history without error messages. That is a meaningful difference. However, the paper should discuss more explicitly whether the gains come from richer state/history conditioning rather than from “failure” as such.

9. Scope of theorem statements
All theorems appear to have 16 internal nodes in the main experiments. Why fix theorem size? Does the method generalize across formula sizes? A size-generalization experiment would strengthen the dataset and method claims.

10. Reproducibility
The paper is fairly reproducible in spirit, and the release of data/code/models is a plus. Still, some implementation details are missing:
- exact prompt templates,
- maximum generation lengths,
- optimizer and scheduler details,
- whether full fine-tuning or parameter-efficient tuning was used,
- random seeds.

Questions for the authors

1. Can you provide results controlling for total training tokens/examples so that TrialMaster and the baseline receive comparable supervision volume?

2. How much of TrialMaster’s gain remains if the baseline is allowed to condition on failed tactic history in the prompt, but is still trained only on successful proofs?

3. What is the total LLM inference cost of TrialMaster versus DFS in tokens and wall-clock time, not just Lean calls?

4. How sensitive are the results to the exact formatting of backtrack instructions?

5. Have you tested whether a model trained on one proof-search generator transfers to traces generated by a different search policy?

6. Can TrialMaster recover from Lean errors if allowed to continue, or is immediate failure essential to the current setup?

Suggestions for improvement

1. Strengthen the empirical section with better-controlled baselines
At minimum, add:
- a token-budget-matched baseline,
- a history-conditioned baseline without trial-and-error training,
- a stronger search baseline if feasible.

2. Report computational cost more comprehensively
Include token counts, runtime, and perhaps average prompt length per theorem.

3. Add uncertainty estimates
Run multiple seeds or bootstrap confidence intervals on the test sets.

4. Soften claims about representativeness and generality
Be more precise that the benchmark is representative under a particular uniform formula-generation scheme, not necessarily of real-world theorem proving distributions.

5. Clarify inconsistencies and implementation details
Fix the shortest-five vs shortest-four discrepancy and provide exact prompting/training details.

6. Expand analysis of learned behavior
The paper claims the model learns backtracking capability because generated backtrack instructions are well-formed and point to existing states. That is encouraging, but stronger evidence would include:
- frequency of backtracking,
- accuracy of backtracking decisions,
- examples where backtracking directly enables success,
- failure cases.

Final verdict

This is a solid and interesting paper with a clean idea, a useful benchmark contribution, and promising results in a controlled domain. The main weakness is that the evaluation is too narrow and the baseline comparison is not yet strong enough to fully support broader claims. Still, I think the core contribution is novel enough and the empirical evidence within the benchmark is convincing enough for acceptance, especially if the venue values focused methodological contributions and dataset creation.
