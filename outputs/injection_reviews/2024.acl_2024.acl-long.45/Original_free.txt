This paper studies a simple but important mismatch in neural theorem proving: models are typically trained only on successful proof trajectories, yet at inference time they must search, make mistakes, and recover from them. The central proposal is to train a language model on proof traces that include trial-and-error behavior, including failed branches and explicit backtracking, and to evaluate whether this improves theorem proving. To support this, the authors introduce PropL, a Lean-formalized dataset for intuitionistic propositional logic with proof trees generated by a focused proof search procedure. The main empirical claim is that TRIALMASTER, a model trained on such traces, achieves higher success and lower search cost than a conventional DFS-based baseline trained only on correct proofs.

Overall, I find the paper interesting and the core intuition compelling. The train-test mismatch identified in the introduction is real, and the paper frames it clearly with the toy example in Figure 1 on page 1 and the system comparison in Figure 2 on page 3. The work is also valuable in that it constructs a fully formalized benchmark where trial-and-error supervision can be generated reliably and checked in Lean. The empirical results on the out-of-distribution split are promising: the plots in Figure 5 on page 8 suggest that TRIALMASTER attains a better success/cost tradeoff than the DFS baseline across a range of temperatures and numbers of sampled tactics, and the ablation in Table 3 indicates that removing trial-and-error information substantially hurts performance. These are meaningful findings.

That said, I also have substantial reservations about the scope of the claims, the fairness of the comparison, and several methodological choices that make it difficult to isolate exactly what is driving the gains. My overall assessment is positive but cautious: the paper presents a useful benchmark and an appealing idea, but the experimental evidence is not yet strong enough to fully support broader conclusions about “learning from failure” in theorem proving.

The strongest aspect of the paper is the problem formulation. The authors correctly observe that conventional tactic models are trained on gold trajectories but deployed inside search procedures that repeatedly encounter off-gold states. This discrepancy is especially well illustrated by Figure 2(a,b) on page 3: the baseline model is trained on a single successful path and then wrapped in DFS at test time, whereas TRIALMASTER is trained on full histories including backtracking and then directly predicts the next action conditioned on that history. This is a clean conceptual contribution. The paper also makes a reasonable case that intuitionistic propositional logic is a good controlled setting because proofs can be generated algorithmically and checked exactly in Lean.

The dataset contribution is also potentially useful. PropL appears to be carefully designed: the theorem generation procedure uses a bijection between formulas and natural numbers, described in Section 3.1 and Appendix B, to support uniform sampling over formulas with fixed size. The proof generation uses focused proof search with polarization, and the examples in Figure 4 on page 5 and Figure 7 on page 15 help clarify what the traces look like. I appreciate that the authors formalize everything in Lean rather than relying on unverifiable synthetic traces. This makes the benchmark more credible than many synthetic reasoning datasets.

However, the paper somewhat overstates the representativeness and completeness of the dataset. The claims that PropL is “complete, scalable, and representative” should be qualified more carefully. It is representative only relative to the chosen formula grammar, fixed internal-node budget, and the specific proof-generation/search procedure. More importantly, the proof traces are not human proofs or naturally occurring proof searches; they are induced by a particular focused proof search algorithm with specific polarization choices and an additional incompleteness-inducing heuristic. Section 3.1 explicitly states that once an implication is picked, it cannot be used to show its premise, which introduces incompleteness, albeit rarely in their sample. This matters because the model may be learning the idiosyncrasies of the FPS trace generator rather than a general notion of productive failure. I would strongly encourage the authors to soften the language around representativeness and completeness, or to define these terms more precisely.

My main concern is the fairness of the baseline comparison. TRIALMASTER and the DFS system differ along multiple axes simultaneously: training data, inference algorithm, and action space. TRIALMASTER is trained on full histories with backtracking and failed branches, and at inference time it conditions on the entire search history and can emit explicit backtrack instructions. The baseline is trained only on successful paths and then used inside an external DFS that samples multiple tactics per state. This means the comparison does not isolate whether the gains come from trial-and-error supervision, from richer conditioning on history, from replacing external search with an internalized search policy, or from differences in decoding. The ablation in Table 3 on page 8 helps somewhat by comparing TRIALMASTER to a model trained without trial-and-error but decoded in the same one-step manner, and that is useful evidence. But the headline comparison against DFS still conflates several factors.

Relatedly, the baseline seems weaker than it could be. The paper compares against a DFS system that makes only one model call per state and samples a fixed number of tactics, removing duplicates and malformed outputs. But there are several stronger baselines that would be natural here and would better test the central claim. For example, one could train on successful paths only but still condition on the full search history at inference time; or train on successful paths augmented with explicit error states but not failed branches; or use a reranking/value model over DFS candidates; or provide the baseline with the same history context but disallow explicit backtrack tokens. The current setup makes it hard to know whether “learning from failure” is the key ingredient or whether “conditioning on search history” is doing most of the work.

A second major issue is that the task domain is extremely narrow. Intuitionistic propositional logic is a reasonable sandbox, but it is far from modern theorem proving benchmarks in Lean, Coq, or Isabelle. The tactic space is small, the formulas are synthetic, and the proof search structure is highly regular. The authors acknowledge this in the limitations section on page 9, but the framing throughout the paper sometimes suggests broader implications for automated theorem proving than the evidence warrants. I think the paper would be stronger if it explicitly positioned itself as a controlled study of failure-aware supervision in a synthetic formal domain, rather than implying that the same conclusions likely transfer to general mathematical theorem proving.

There are also some methodological details that need clarification. First, the training data construction for TRIALMASTER is somewhat confusing. Section 4.1 says the model is trained on two out of the shortest five among ten proofs with trial-and-error for each theorem, while Section 5.5 says TRIALMASTER uses two out of the shortest four proofs. This inconsistency should be fixed. Second, the proof-length-based train/test split is intended to create OOD generalization, but because all formulas have the same number of internal nodes (16), the OOD shift is really in proof/search complexity under the chosen generator, not in theorem size or syntax. That is acceptable, but it should be described more precisely. Third, the stopping criteria differ in ways that may advantage one method over another. TRIALMASTER fails immediately on any Lean error, whereas DFS can recover from many bad tactics by trying alternatives at the same state. On the other hand, TRIALMASTER’s backtrack instructions do not incur Lean calls and thus reduce the reported search cost. Since NLean is a central metric, the paper should discuss more explicitly whether this is the right normalization for comparing an internalized-search model against an external-search baseline.

The search-cost metric itself is sensible but incomplete. Counting Lean calls is useful, especially in a formal proving setting, but it ignores model inference cost and prompt growth. TRIALMASTER repeatedly conditions on the entire proof history, including failed branches and backtracking, which likely increases token usage substantially. The paper notes a 1500-word limit and even acknowledges that this is stricter for TRIALMASTER, but it does not report average prompt lengths, number of generated tokens, wall-clock time, or GPU inference cost. Since one of the paper’s claims is improved efficiency, these additional measurements would be important. A method that reduces Lean calls but greatly increases LLM compute may or may not be preferable in practice.

I also found the evidence for “learning backtracking capability” weaker than it could be. The paper states on page 8 that all backtracking instructions produced by TRIALMASTER were well-formed and pointed to existing states. That is encouraging, but it is only a syntactic property. What matters is whether the backtracking decisions are semantically useful: how often does the model backtrack to the right ancestor, how often does backtracking lead to eventual success, and how often does it loop or backtrack unnecessarily? A more detailed analysis of backtracking behavior would significantly strengthen the paper. For example, the authors could report the distribution of backtrack depths, the fraction of successful proofs that require at least one backtrack, and comparisons to oracle or random backtracking policies.

The ablation studies are directionally good but still limited. Table 3 on page 8 shows a large drop from 88.7% to 59.3% when removing trial-and-error information while keeping the same inference style. This is probably the most convincing result in the paper. The additional experiment showing 75.6% when not computing loss on failed search paths is also interesting, as it suggests failed branches themselves carry useful signal beyond just backtrack tokens. However, these results would be more convincing with variance estimates across multiple runs. As presented, all numbers appear to come from single fine-tuning runs on one backbone model. Given the stochasticity in training and proof sampling, confidence intervals or repeated trials would help establish robustness.

Another concern is that the paper uses only one base model, Llama-2-7b-hf, trained for a single epoch. This is understandable from a resource perspective, but it limits the generality of the conclusions. It would be useful to know whether the effect persists across model scales or architectures, or whether it is especially pronounced because the base model is relatively weak in this domain. Similarly, the paper does not compare against stronger prompting-based baselines that include history or error messages, despite discussing related work such as COPRA and Baldur. Even if those systems are not directly applicable, some simplified adaptation would help contextualize the gains.

I also have some questions about the theorem distribution and evaluation protocol. The paper samples 200,000 theorems with 16 internal nodes, then filters/splits based on proof lengths under generated traces. This means the final train and test distributions are shaped jointly by theorem sampling and by the proof generator’s behavior. It would be helpful to report more statistics: proportion provable vs unprovable before filtering, average proof lengths with and without trial-and-error, distribution of branching factors, and how many examples are excluded by the quantile thresholds. Since the paper emphasizes representativeness, these descriptive statistics are important. In addition, because the proof generator is deterministic apart from randomized focus choices, there is a risk that train and test traces share strong structural regularities. Some analysis of theorem overlap at the level of subformula patterns or proof motifs would be useful.

The writing is generally clear, and the figures are helpful. Figure 4 on page 5 is especially effective in contrasting proofs with and without backtracking. The appendix explaining the bijection and the natural deduction rules is also appreciated. Still, there are several places where the exposition could be tightened. Some terminology is used loosely, such as “proof tree,” “proof path,” and “whole proof tree,” which are not always distinguished carefully. There are also minor inconsistencies and grammatical issues throughout the paper. For example, the description of theorem_2 on page 2 says “The first theorem admits a proof, where as the second doesn’t,” which should be corrected. These are minor, but a careful proofreading pass would improve presentation.

To strengthen the paper, I would recommend the following concrete additions or revisions. First, add stronger controlled baselines that isolate the effect of trial-and-error supervision from the effect of richer history conditioning and different inference procedures. At minimum, compare: a model trained on correct paths only but conditioned on full history; a model trained with backtrack tokens but without failed branches; and a model trained with failed branches but no explicit backtrack action. Second, report additional efficiency metrics beyond Lean calls, including prompt length, generated tokens, and wall-clock inference time. Third, provide a deeper behavioral analysis of backtracking decisions. Fourth, run multiple seeds or repeated fine-tuning runs and report variance. Fifth, moderate the claims about completeness/representativeness and about implications for general theorem proving. Finally, if feasible, include at least one small-scale experiment on a more realistic formal domain, even if only a toy subset of Lean theorems, to test whether the effect survives outside synthetic propositional logic.

In summary, I think this paper contains a worthwhile idea and a useful benchmark contribution. The empirical results suggest that training on trial-and-error traces can materially improve performance in a controlled formal reasoning setting, and the ablations provide nontrivial evidence that failed branches themselves are informative. However, the current experimental design does not fully disentangle the source of the gains, and the scope of the domain is too narrow to support broad claims about theorem proving with LLMs. I would view this as a promising controlled study rather than a definitive demonstration. With stronger baselines, more careful analysis, and more restrained claims, this could become a solid and influential paper.
