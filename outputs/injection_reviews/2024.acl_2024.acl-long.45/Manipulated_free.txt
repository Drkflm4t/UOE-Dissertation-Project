Below is my review in a standard conference style.

Summary

This paper studies whether theorem provers based on LLMs can benefit from learning not only from successful proof trajectories, but also from failed attempts and backtracking history. The authors argue that there is a train-test mismatch in conventional tactic-generation systems: models are trained on correct proof paths only, but at inference time they must search, encounter failures, and backtrack. To investigate this, the paper introduces PropL, a Lean-formalized dataset for intuitionistic propositional logic that includes trial-and-error proof traces generated by a focused proof search procedure. The proposed method, TRIALMASTER, is fine-tuned on these traces and performs inference by conditioning on the full proof-search history, including backtracking. Experiments compare TRIALMASTER against a conventional DFS-based prover trained only on correct proofs. On the proposed OOD split, TRIALMASTER achieves higher success rate and lower Lean-call cost than the DFS baseline across the explored settings.

Overall assessment

The paper addresses a real and important issue in neural theorem proving: learning from failures rather than only from successful demonstrations. The central idea is intuitive and worthwhile, and the paper makes it concrete with a clean experimental setup in a domain where correctness can be checked reliably. I found the dataset contribution meaningful, and the empirical results support the main claim that trial-and-error supervision can improve proof search efficiency and success.

That said, the scope is also fairly narrow: the study is restricted to intuitionistic propositional logic, the baseline space is limited, and some methodological choices make it harder to isolate exactly where the gains come from. So while I think the paper is interesting and potentially impactful as a controlled study, I am less convinced by broader claims about general theorem proving without further evidence.

Strengths

1. Clear motivation and problem formulation.
The paper identifies a genuine mismatch between training on gold proof paths and inference-time search with failures. This is well explained in the introduction and visually in Figure 1 on page 1 and Figure 2 on page 3. The framing is easy to understand and compelling.

2. Useful dataset contribution.
PropL appears to be a carefully constructed benchmark. The paper does more than just sample formulas: it formalizes them in Lean, provides proof traces with backtracking, and explains the generation process in detail. The discussion of the proposition-number bijection and uniform sampling, especially around Figure 3 on page 4 and Appendix B on pages 13–14, is thoughtful. Having a dataset with complete trial-and-error traces is valuable for this line of work.

3. Controlled experimental domain.
Using intuitionistic propositional logic is a sensible choice for a first study. It allows exact proof checking in Lean and avoids ambiguity about correctness. This makes the empirical conclusions cleaner than they would be in a noisier setting.

4. Empirical evidence for the main claim.
The OOD results are the most important part of the paper, and they are promising. Figure 5 on page 8 shows that TRIALMASTER reaches a better success/cost tradeoff than the DFS baseline under the explored hyperparameters. The ablation in Table 3 on page 8 is also useful: training without trial-and-error but using the same inference style performs much worse, which supports the importance of the supervision signal rather than only the decoding procedure.

5. Interesting observation about learned backtracking.
The claim that the model can emit syntactically valid backtracking instructions and use them during inference is intriguing. If robust, this is a nice result suggesting that some search-control behavior can be internalized by the model.

Weaknesses and concerns

1. Limited external validity.
The biggest limitation is scope. Intuitionistic propositional logic is much simpler than mainstream theorem proving benchmarks in Lean, Coq, or Isabelle. The paper acknowledges this, but the title and some claims could still be read more broadly than warranted. It remains unclear whether the same benefits would hold in richer domains with larger tactic spaces, longer dependencies, and more semantic content.

2. Baseline comparison is somewhat narrow.
The main baseline is a DFS system trained on correct proofs only. This is reasonable, but not sufficient to establish strong comparative claims. There are at least three missing comparisons:
- a stronger search baseline with richer prompting/history,
- a baseline trained with error information or repair signals, closer to Baldur-like settings,
- a version that augments the DFS model with failed attempts in context at inference time, without changing training.
Because the proposed method changes both training data and inference procedure, it is difficult to attribute gains precisely.

3. Inference procedures are not fully matched.
TRIALMASTER uses greedy one-step generation conditioned on the entire search history, including backtracks, while the DFS baseline samples multiple tactics once per state and relies on external search. This is a meaningful systems comparison, but not a fully controlled apples-to-apples model comparison. The ablation in Table 3 helps, but more matched comparisons would strengthen the paper.

4. Potential concern about data generation bias.
The trial-and-error traces are generated by a specific focused proof search algorithm with particular polarization choices and an additional incompleteness-inducing assumption (page 5). This means the model may be learning the behavior of that search procedure rather than a general notion of productive failure. That is not necessarily bad, but it should be discussed more explicitly. In particular, if the training traces reflect one search policy, then success may partly come from imitating that policy.

5. OOD definition is limited.
The OOD split is based on proof length quantiles, with training on shorter proofs and testing on longer ones (page 5). This is a reasonable stress test, but it is still within the same synthetic distribution over formulas. It would be stronger to include additional generalization axes, such as different numbers of atoms, different connective distributions, or proofs generated under different search heuristics.

6. Some claims need more quantitative support.
For example, the paper states that TRIALMASTER learns backtracking capability because all produced backtracking instructions are correctly formatted and refer to existing states (page 8). This is interesting, but I would like actual counts: how often does the model backtrack, how often is it necessary, how often does it backtrack to the optimal state, and how much of the gain comes from backtracking versus better tactic prediction?

7. Search budget and stopping criteria may affect conclusions.
The DFS baseline is capped at 65 search steps, while TRIALMASTER fails on Lean error or context-length overflow. These are not identical failure modes. The paper explains the motivation for the 65-step cap on page 7, but the comparison would be more convincing with sensitivity analyses over search budgets and context limits.

8. Writing and presentation issues.
The paper is generally understandable, but there are several grammatical issues and awkward phrasings throughout. A few examples:
- “masters in-distribution intuitionistic propositional logic theorems” is awkward.
- Some notation and terminology around “proof tree,” “proof path,” and “trial-and-error information” could be tightened.
- There are minor inconsistencies such as “shortest five” in Section 4.1 versus “shortest four” in Section 5.5.
These do not undermine the core contribution, but polishing would help.

Questions for the authors

1. How many training tokens/examples are used for TRIALMASTER versus the DFS model? Since TRIALMASTER uses two traces per theorem and those traces are longer, is the total supervision budget substantially larger?

2. Can you provide a matched-compute or matched-token comparison? For example, if the DFS model were trained on an equal number of tokens from correct proofs only, would the gap narrow?

3. How often does TRIALMASTER emit backtracking instructions on the OOD set, and what fraction of successful proofs require at least one backtrack?

4. Did you try giving the DFS baseline access to failed history in the prompt while keeping its training unchanged? That would help separate the effect of conditioning on history from the effect of trial-and-error fine-tuning.

5. How sensitive are results to the specific focused proof search algorithm used to generate traces? If you generate traces with a different search policy, does TRIALMASTER still improve?

6. Since the dataset is synthetic and generated algorithmically, is there any risk of leakage or structural overlap between train and test beyond proof length? For example, are many test formulas minor variants of training formulas under renaming or local rewrites?

Suggestions for improvement

1. Add stronger and more controlled baselines.
At minimum, include:
- same inference style, different training signal,
- same training signal, different inference style,
- a baseline with error/backtracking context but no failed-path supervision.

2. Report more detailed behavioral analyses.
I would like per-proof statistics: number of backtracks, average proof depth, average failed branches explored, and examples where trial-and-error clearly changes the next tactic distribution. Figure 4 on page 5 is a good start; more such case studies would help.

3. Clarify fairness of comparison.
Please report training token counts, number of traces, average trace lengths, and inference-time compute more explicitly.

4. Expand discussion of limitations.
The paper should more clearly distinguish “learning from failure in this controlled propositional setting” from “learning from failure for theorem proving in general.”

5. Improve writing and consistency.
A careful proofreading pass would fix several small issues and make the paper easier to read.

Recommendation

Weak Accept

Rationale

I think this is a solid and interesting paper with a clear idea, a useful dataset contribution, and encouraging empirical evidence in a controlled formal setting. The main result—that trial-and-error supervision improves theorem proving over training on successful paths alone—is plausible and reasonably supported by the experiments. My hesitation comes from the narrow domain and somewhat limited baseline comparisons, which make the broader significance less certain. Still, as a focused study and benchmark contribution, I believe it is worthy of acceptance.

Confidence

Medium-High

I am confident in the assessment of the paper’s strengths and limitations, though less confident about how much the results would transfer beyond the propositional setting.
