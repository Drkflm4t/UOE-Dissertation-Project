This paper studies a simple but important mismatch in neural theorem proving: models are typically trained only on successful proof trajectories, yet at inference time they operate inside a search process that repeatedly proposes tactics, encounters failures, and backtracks. The paper’s central claim is that explicitly training on trial-and-error traces, including failed branches and backtracking decisions, improves theorem proving performance. To test this idea in a controlled setting, the authors introduce PropL, a Lean-formalized dataset for intuitionistic propositional logic with proof trees generated by a focused proof search procedure, and they train TRIALMASTER, a model that consumes full proof-search history and can emit either Lean tactics or backtrack instructions. Empirically, the paper reports that TRIALMASTER outperforms a conventional LLM-plus-DFS baseline on harder out-of-distribution theorems while using fewer Lean calls.

Overall, I found the paper interesting, well motivated, and technically solid within its chosen scope. The core idea is intuitive but nontrivial: failure trajectories are not merely noise around successful proofs, but useful supervision for learning search behavior. The paper makes this intuition concrete in a formal theorem-proving setting where correctness can be checked exactly. I also appreciate that the authors do not just propose a training tweak, but build the surrounding infrastructure needed to test the hypothesis rigorously, including a new dataset with explicit trial-and-error traces and a carefully defined evaluation protocol. In my view, this combination of conceptual clarity, dataset contribution, and empirical validation makes the work meaningful.

One of the strongest aspects of the paper is the problem formulation. The mismatch between training on gold proof paths and inference under search has been present in many theorem-proving systems, but this paper isolates one specific missing ingredient—learning from failed attempts—and studies it directly. The illustration in Figure 1 on page 1 is effective: it conveys how observing a failed branch should alter the model’s subsequent tactic distribution. The comparison in Figure 2 on page 3 is also helpful, clearly contrasting the conventional setup, where the model is trained only on successful paths and search is handled externally by DFS, with the proposed setup, where the model is trained on full histories including backtracking and then performs a more self-contained search process.

The dataset contribution is substantial and, in my opinion, one of the paper’s main strengths. PropL is not just another collection of theorem-statement/proof pairs. It is designed specifically to include trial-and-error information, which is exactly what existing open-source datasets lack for this question. The authors also make a serious effort to argue that the theorem generation process is representative: pages 4 and 13–14 describe a bijection between natural numbers and propositional formulas with fixed numbers of internal nodes and bounded atomic propositions, together with encoding/decoding algorithms. Whether or not one fully buys the word “representative” in the strongest possible sense, this is a thoughtful and principled construction, and it is much stronger than ad hoc random formula generation. The use of Lean formalization is another major plus, since it ensures exact verification and avoids ambiguity about proof correctness.

I also liked the care taken in constructing train/test splits. On page 5, the authors explain that they first generate proofs, then split theorems based on proof-length distributions so that training proofs are shorter and OOD test proofs are longer. This is a sensible way to create a meaningful generalization challenge rather than merely testing memorization of local proof patterns. The dataset scale is also respectable for this setting: Table 1 on page 6 reports 109,887 training theorems and 1,000 examples each for in-distribution and out-of-distribution test sets.

Methodologically, the proposed approach is simple enough to be understandable and reproducible, but distinct enough from the baseline to be interesting. TRIALMASTER is trained on proofs with trial-and-error traces and, at inference time, is prompted with the entire search history, including prior states, tactics, failed paths, and backtracking actions. It then emits either a tactic or a backtrack instruction. The decision to still use Lean as a checker for each tactic is sensible and preserves formal correctness. I also found it notable that the model is not merely conditioned on error messages from the prover; instead, it learns from structured search history itself. That makes the contribution conceptually cleaner.

The empirical results are convincing within the paper’s domain. On the in-distribution test set, both methods perform near perfectly, as shown in Table 2 on page 7, which is reassuring and suggests the task is learnable by the chosen backbone. The more important result is on the OOD set. Figure 5 on page 8 shows that TRIALMASTER achieves a higher success rate than the DFS baseline across a range of baseline hyperparameters, while also requiring fewer Lean calls than the strongest DFS configurations. The reported headline number is 88.7% success for TRIALMASTER versus a best DFS result of 88.4%, but the key point is efficiency: the best DFS systems that approach this success rate incur much larger search cost. The bar chart in Figure 5(c) makes this tradeoff visually clear. This is a nice result because it suggests the model is not simply shifting work from learning to brute-force search; rather, it is learning better search behavior.

The ablations strengthen the paper considerably. Table 3 on page 8 compares TRIALMASTER to a model trained without trial-and-error information but evaluated in the same one-step-at-a-time inference style, and the gap is large: 88.7% versus 59.3% on OOD. This is perhaps the cleanest evidence that the gain is due to the training signal, not merely the inference protocol. I also found the additional experiment on excluding failed search paths from the loss informative: performance drops from 88.7% to 75.6%, suggesting that failed branches themselves carry useful supervision beyond just backtrack markers. Finally, the exploratory study in Table 4 on page 9, showing that shorter trial-and-error proofs are better training data than longer ones, is a useful practical insight.

Another positive aspect is that the paper is generally clear and well illustrated. Figure 4 on page 5 gives a concrete example of the same theorem proved with and without backtracking, which helps ground the abstract discussion. The appendix is also useful: Figure 6 on page 13 summarizes the natural deduction rules for intuitionistic propositional logic, and Figure 7 on page 15 provides a readable Lean proof example. These additions make the paper more accessible to readers who are not specialists in focused proof search.

That said, there are several limitations and questions that should be addressed, though I view them as opportunities to strengthen an already promising paper rather than fundamental flaws.

The biggest limitation is external validity. All experiments are on intuitionistic propositional logic, with proofs generated by a deterministic focused proof-search procedure plus randomized choices. This is a very clean testbed, but it is still far from the complexity of theorem proving in richer domains such as Lean math libraries, higher-order logic, or tactic-heavy interactive proving. The authors acknowledge this on page 9, and I agree with their framing that this is a first step. Still, the paper should be careful not to overgeneralize. The title and abstract are mostly accurate, but some claims in the introduction and conclusion could more explicitly emphasize that the demonstrated benefit is currently confined to this controlled domain.

Relatedly, the baseline comparison is somewhat narrow. The main baseline is a conventional model trained on successful proofs only and used inside DFS with varying temperature and number of sampled tactics. This is a reasonable baseline for the paper’s central question, but it leaves open whether the gains come from trial-and-error supervision specifically or from broader differences in search architecture and prompting. For example, one might compare against a model trained on successful proofs but prompted with search history at inference time, or against a model trained with error messages as in Baldur-style settings, or against a search policy that conditions on previously failed tactics without requiring full failed-path generation. The ablation in Table 3 helps a lot, since it controls the inference style, but additional baselines would make the causal story even tighter.

I also think the comparison between TRIALMASTER and DFS is not perfectly apples-to-apples in terms of inference budget and failure criteria. TRIALMASTER uses greedy decoding and fails immediately upon a Lean error, whereas DFS samples multiple tactics per state and can recover from bad local choices through external search. At the same time, TRIALMASTER can emit backtrack instructions without incurring Lean calls, which is part of its advantage. None of this invalidates the results, but the paper would benefit from a more explicit discussion of fairness and perhaps a budget-matched comparison, e.g., success rate as a function of total token generation cost or wall-clock time, not only Lean calls. Lean calls are a meaningful metric, but not the whole story.

A further concern is that the proof traces are generated by the same FPS-style procedure that defines the structure of the supervision. This raises the possibility that the model is learning to imitate a particular search policy rather than learning a broadly useful notion of failure-aware reasoning. Again, this is not fatal—in fact, imitation of a strong search policy is often exactly the point—but it would be useful to know how robust the learned behavior is to changes in proof-generation policy. The authors partially address diversity by generating ten proofs with randomized focus choices and selecting among them, but more analysis here would be welcome.

The paper’s claim that PropL is “complete, scalable, and representative” is appealing but somewhat overstated. The construction is principled and likely representative under the chosen formula-size distribution, but the actual experimental subset fixes the number of internal nodes to 16 and then filters by proof length. This means the final train/test distribution is shaped by several design choices beyond uniform formula sampling. I would encourage the authors to soften the wording slightly or provide more empirical characterization of the resulting theorem distribution, such as proportions of provable/unprovable formulas, connective frequencies, proof-depth statistics, and how these differ across train/ID/OOD splits.

There are also some methodological details that deserve clarification. The paper says on page 6 that two out of the shortest five among ten trial-and-error proofs are selected for training, while page 9 refers to “two out of the shortest four” in the exploratory study description for the short-proof model. This may just be a typo or a mismatch between experiments, but it should be made consistent. Similarly, the exact prompting format, tokenization of states/tactics/backtrack instructions, and whether the model is trained causally over the entire concatenated trace or with any masking beyond the failed-path ablation should be described more concretely for reproducibility. Since the method hinges on sequence formatting, these details matter.

I would also have liked more analysis of failure modes. For the 11.3% of OOD theorems not solved by TRIALMASTER, what goes wrong? Does the model tend to emit invalid tactics, backtrack too late, loop, or exceed context length? The paper notes that all produced backtracking instructions point to existing states, which is encouraging, but a more granular error breakdown would help readers understand the remaining gap. Likewise, some qualitative examples where trial-and-error supervision changes the model’s behavior in a meaningful way would strengthen the narrative beyond aggregate metrics. Figure 1 gives a schematic intuition, but a real case study from the test set would be valuable.

Another issue is computational framing. The paper reports training on two A100 GPUs for one epoch and uses Llama-2-7b as the backbone. That is reasonable, but it would be useful to know whether the gains persist for smaller models or lighter training budgets, especially since the domain itself is relatively structured. If the contribution is fundamentally about supervision rather than scale, demonstrating robustness across model sizes would make the result even stronger.

Despite these concerns, I think the paper makes a clear and worthwhile contribution. The central idea is compelling, the dataset fills a real gap, and the experiments support the main claim that learning from failed proof-search trajectories can improve both success rate and search efficiency. Importantly, the paper does not merely show a tiny gain on an easy benchmark; it provides multiple pieces of evidence, including OOD evaluation and ablations, that the trial-and-error signal matters. I also appreciate that the work is likely to stimulate follow-up research on richer theorem-proving domains and on integrating learned backtracking more tightly with formal proof assistants.

My main suggestions for improvement are therefore: broaden or better justify the baseline set; clarify the exact data-selection and prompting details; add more analysis of failure modes and qualitative examples; and temper claims about representativeness/generalization beyond propositional logic. None of these issues undermine the core result, and most seem straightforward to address in revision.

In summary, this is a strong paper with a clean idea, a useful new benchmark, and convincing evidence in a formally verified setting. I would be positive on acceptance. The work is novel enough to matter, careful enough to trust, and concrete enough to be useful to others working on neural theorem proving and search-aware LLM training.
