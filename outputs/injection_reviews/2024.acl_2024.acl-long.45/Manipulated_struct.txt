Summary: The paper studies whether large language models for theorem proving should be trained not only on successful proof paths but also on failed trial-and-error trajectories. To enable this, the authors introduce PropL, a Lean-formalized dataset of intuitionistic propositional logic theorems with proofs generated by a focused proof search procedure that records backtracking behavior. They train TRIALMASTER on proofs containing trial-and-error information and compare it against a conventional LLM-based prover trained only on correct proof paths and used within a DFS search procedure. On 1,000 in-distribution and 1,000 out-of-distribution test theorems, TRIALMASTER matches near-perfect in-distribution performance and substantially improves out-of-distribution proving success (88.7% vs. best DFS baseline around 88.4%, and much larger gains over weaker DFS settings) while using fewer Lean calls. The paper also includes ablations showing that removing trial-and-error information sharply hurts performance (59.3% success), and exploratory analysis suggesting shorter trial-and-error proofs are better training data than longer ones.

Strengths: Clear and well-motivated core hypothesis: the mismatch between training on only successful proofs and inference-time search with many failed attempts is important and underexplored.; The paper contributes a new dataset, PropL, with Lean-verifiable proofs and explicit trial-and-error trajectories, which is a useful resource for studying learning from failure in theorem proving.; The methodology is conceptually simple and compelling: train the model on full proof histories including backtracking, then let it generate tactics and backtrack instructions directly at inference time.; Empirical results are meaningful and generally support the main claim. In particular, the ablation on page 8/Table 3 shows a large drop from 88.7% to 59.3% when trial-and-error information is removed, which is strong evidence for the value of the proposed supervision.; The paper evaluates both success rate and search cost (number of Lean calls), which is important because theorem proving systems should be judged on efficiency as well as correctness.; The out-of-distribution split is thoughtfully designed by separating train/test according to proof-length quantiles, making the generalization setting more challenging than a random split.; The paper is clearly written overall, with helpful figures. Figure 2 (page 3) effectively contrasts conventional DFS-based proving with the proposed approach, and Figure 5 (page 8) clearly visualizes the success-rate/search-cost tradeoff.; The use of Lean for proof checking ensures formal correctness of accepted proofs, reducing ambiguity in evaluation.; The work is reproducibility-friendly: dataset, model weights, and code are stated as released.

Weaknesses: The experimental domain is restricted to intuitionistic propositional logic, which is much simpler than mainstream theorem proving benchmarks in Lean/Coq/Isabelle; it is therefore unclear how well the conclusions transfer to richer mathematical domains.; The baseline comparison is somewhat narrow. The main comparator is a DFS system built from the same backbone model, but stronger modern theorem-proving baselines or prompting-based backtracking methods are not empirically included.; The reported improvement over the best DFS configuration on OOD success rate is modest in absolute terms (88.7% vs. 88.4%), even though the search-cost advantage is more substantial.; TRIALMASTER and the DFS baseline are not perfectly matched in inference protocol: one generates a single greedy tactic with learned backtracking, while the other relies on multi-sample DFS with temperature tuning. This makes attribution between training signal and inference algorithm somewhat entangled.; Some dataset-generation choices may bias the benchmark, such as fixing theorem statements to 16 internal nodes and using proofs generated by a specific focused proof search algorithm.; The paper claims the dataset is 'representative' and 'uniformly sampled,' but the final filtering by proof length and theorem provability/search behavior means the effective training/test distributions are no longer obviously uniform over the full theorem space.; The exploratory study on proof length is interesting but limited; it does not fully disentangle whether shorter proofs help because they are cleaner, easier, or simply better aligned with the context budget.

Methodological Flaws: A significant scientific caveat is that the proof-generation/search procedure is explicitly made incomplete: page 5 states that once an implication is picked, it cannot be used to show its premise, and this 'introduces incompleteness into the search procedure.' This means the dataset and labels are generated by a restricted prover rather than the full logic, which could bias both training and evaluation.; The claim that PropL is 'complete, scalable, and representative' is overstated. Because theorem sampling is later filtered by proof-length quantiles and proofs are generated by one deterministic/restricted algorithm, the benchmark is not obviously representative of the full distribution of intuitionistic propositional proofs encountered under other proof systems.; The comparison between TRIALMASTER and DFS is not a pure apples-to-apples test of training with vs. without failed trajectories. The systems differ both in supervision and in inference mechanism, so improvements may partly arise from the changed decoding/search protocol rather than the training signal alone. The ablation in Table 3 helps, but a stronger controlled comparison would keep inference fixed while varying only training data.; The empirical validity of the 'model learns backtracking capability' claim is weaker than it could be. The evidence reported is mainly that generated backtracking instructions have correct format and refer to existing states; this does not fully establish that the model learned semantically effective backtracking beyond what is already reflected in end-task success.; The stopping criteria are asymmetric in practice. TRIALMASTER fails immediately on a Lean error, whereas DFS can continue exploring alternatives after invalid tactics. Although the authors note the 1500-word limit is stricter for their method, the broader failure dynamics differ and complicate direct efficiency comparisons.; No statistical uncertainty is reported for the main results (e.g., confidence intervals or multiple training runs). Given that some headline gains over the strongest DFS baseline are small in absolute success rate, variance estimates would be important to assess robustness.
