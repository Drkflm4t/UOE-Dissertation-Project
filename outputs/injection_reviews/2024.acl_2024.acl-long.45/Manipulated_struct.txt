Summary: The paper studies whether large language models for theorem proving can benefit from training on trial-and-error proof traces rather than only successful proof paths. The authors introduce PropL, a Lean-formalized dataset of intuitionistic propositional logic theorems with proofs generated by a focused proof search procedure that includes backtracking and failed branches. They fine-tune Llama-2-7B in two settings: a conventional tactic generator trained only on correct proofs and used with DFS search, and TRIALMASTER, trained on proof histories containing failed attempts and backtracking instructions and used greedily with full history. On their PropL benchmark, especially on an out-of-distribution split with longer proofs, TRIALMASTER achieves higher proof success rate and lower Lean-call search cost than the DFS baseline. Ablations suggest that including failed search paths materially improves performance.

Strengths: Clear and well-motivated problem: there is a genuine train-test mismatch in theorem proving systems trained only on successful trajectories but deployed with search over many failed attempts.; The paper contributes a new dataset, PropL, that includes trial-and-error proof traces and is formalized in Lean, enabling reliable proof checking.; The methodological idea is simple and interesting: encode backtracking and failed paths directly into the training data and inference context rather than relying only on external search.; Experimental results on the OOD split are compelling within the chosen domain: TRIALMASTER outperforms the DFS baseline in both success rate and search cost.; The ablation on removing trial-and-error information is useful and supports the central claim that failed paths help.; The paper is generally well illustrated; figures such as the workflow comparison on page 3 and the OOD tradeoff plots on page 8 make the setup and results easy to follow.; The dataset construction is thoughtfully designed to reduce train-test similarity by splitting according to proof length distributions and evaluating on longer OOD proofs.

Weaknesses: The empirical scope is narrow: all experiments are on intuitionistic propositional logic, a highly structured and relatively limited domain, so it is unclear how well the conclusions transfer to richer theorem proving settings.; The baseline comparison is limited. The main comparator is a DFS system trained only on correct proofs; stronger baselines using richer prompting, error feedback, or alternative search/training strategies are not evaluated.; The inference procedures are not perfectly matched: TRIALMASTER uses a different action space including explicit backtracking instructions, while the baseline relies on external DFS with sampled tactics, making attribution between training signal and inference design less clean.; The proof generation process is synthetic and tied to a specific focused proof search algorithm, so the learned behavior may partly reflect imitation of that generator rather than broadly useful theorem-proving skill.; The search/evaluation protocol may favor the proposed method in some respects and the baseline in others; for example, TRIALMASTER fails immediately on a Lean error, while DFS can continue exploring alternatives, making comparisons somewhat hard to interpret.; The paper does not report variance across runs, statistical significance, or sensitivity to random seeds, despite stochastic proof generation and model training choices.; Using only one backbone model (Llama-2-7B) limits understanding of whether the effect is robust across model scales or architectures.

Soundness Issues: A notable methodological caveat is the incompleteness introduced in dataset/proof generation by the assumption that once an implication is picked, it cannot be used to show its premise. The authors acknowledge this affects about 1 in 1000 provable theorems, but it still means the benchmark is not fully faithful to the underlying logic.; The claim that PropL is 'representative' because propositions are uniformly sampled via a bijection is somewhat overstated, since the final dataset is filtered/split by proof length and proof generation characteristics, which changes the effective distribution.; The comparison between TRIALMASTER and DFS conflates multiple factors: training on failed paths, conditioning on full proof-tree history, greedy one-step inference, and explicit generation of backtrack actions. This makes causal attribution less rigorous.; The evidence that the model truly 'learns backtracking' is limited; the paper mainly reports that generated backtracking instructions are well-formed and reference existing states, but does not deeply analyze whether these decisions are semantically optimal or necessary.; The exploratory study on proof length is suggestive but not conclusive, since it changes the training proof selection strategy without broader controls or repeated trials.
