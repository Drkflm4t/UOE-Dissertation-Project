Summary: The paper proposes Efficient Safe Policy Optimization (ESPO), an on-policy safe RL method that adaptively manipulates sample size based on the conflict between reward and safety gradients within a three-mode optimization framework inherited from PCRPO-style updates: reward-only, cost-only, and joint reward-cost optimization. The main claim is that increasing sample size under gradient conflict and decreasing it otherwise improves sample efficiency, optimization stability, and reward-safety trade-offs. The paper provides a tabular softmax-policy analysis with convergence and auxiliary propositions, and evaluates ESPO on Safety-MuJoCo and Omnisafe benchmarks against primal baselines (CRPO, PCRPO) and primal-dual baselines (PCPO, CUP, PPOLag), reporting improved reward, better or comparable safety, and reduced sample usage/training time.

Strengths: Addresses an important problem in safe RL: improving sample efficiency without sacrificing constraint satisfaction.; The core idea of adapting sample size using reward-cost gradient conflict is intuitive and novel in the safe RL context.; The three-mode optimization framework is practically meaningful and aligns well with the structure of constrained policy optimization.; The paper includes both theoretical analysis and empirical evaluation, which strengthens the overall contribution.; Empirical results on multiple tasks across two benchmarks show consistent gains in reward and efficiency, with reported sample reductions of roughly 25-29% and noticeable training-time savings.; Comparison against both primal and primal-dual baselines broadens the empirical relevance of the work.; Ablation studies on cost limits, sample sizes, and update styles provide some additional insight into the behavior of the method.; The paper is generally well motivated, and the figures/tables clearly communicate the claimed empirical improvements.

Weaknesses: The theoretical analysis is restricted to the tabular softmax setting, while the experiments are in deep RL continuous-control settings; the gap between theory and practice is substantial.; The sample-manipulation rule is heuristic in the practical algorithm, and the theoretical sample-efficiency proposition is qualitative rather than giving a sharp non-asymptotic bound on total sample complexity improvement.; There are several presentation inconsistencies and notation issues, including swapped case descriptions in Section 4.3, inconsistent naming of sets/modes, and some typographical/mathematical errors in the appendix.; The empirical evaluation uses a limited number of seeds (at least three), and no statistical significance tests or confidence intervals over final metrics are reported beyond shaded curves.; The comparison may not fully isolate whether gains come from adaptive sample sizing versus the underlying PCRPO-style gradient handling, since ESPO combines both ingredients.; Training-time comparisons across different implementations/frameworks may be affected by engineering details and are therefore harder to interpret as purely algorithmic gains.

Methodological Flaws: There is a scientific-logic inconsistency in Section 4.3: for soft-constraint violations, the text states that when the gradient angle is <= 90 degrees the policy is updated with Equation (3), and when the angle is > 90 degrees it is updated with Equation (4), which contradicts Section 4.1 where Equation (3) is the conflict case (> 90 degrees) and Equation (4) is the aligned case (<= 90 degrees). This raises ambiguity about the actual implemented algorithm.; Algorithm 1 on page 26 appears to repeat the same mismatch: line 19 uses theta_{r,c} <= 90 degrees followed by update Equation (3), again conflicting with the earlier method definition. If this is not a typo, the algorithmic logic is reversed in a key branch.; The proof of Proposition 4.3 does not establish a quantitative sample-complexity improvement in a rigorous standard sense. It relies on Assumption A.7, an informal relation delta_t = Y - alpha s_t^B, and concludes only that ESPO 'needs fewer number of samples' than a non-adaptive variant. This is not a formal theorem with explicit conditions and bounds, so the sample-efficiency claim is weaker than advertised.; The theoretical development contains notation/sign inconsistencies around the conflict set. In multiple places B_soft^conf is defined as the case of conflicting gradients, but equation (33) derives y_t^r >= x_t^r because cos(theta_rc^t) >= 0 'as t in B_soft^conf', whereas conflict was earlier defined as theta > 90 degrees, implying cos(theta) < 0. This is a substantive mathematical inconsistency.; Proposition 4.2 claims ESPO can remain in the safe/soft region without oscillation by 'adaptively choosing' weights x_t^r, x_t^c so that a desired inequality holds, but no constructive rule for choosing these weights is provided in the main algorithm. As stated, the proposition may be existential rather than operational.; The empirical validity of the claimed sample-efficiency improvement is somewhat limited because baselines appear to use fixed total sample budgets while ESPO is allowed to stop earlier/use fewer samples. This demonstrates efficiency in practice, but a stricter comparison at matched sample budgets and matched wall-clock-optimized implementations would better support the claim.; The experiments do not clearly disentangle the contribution of adaptive sample sizing from the contribution of the three-mode/PCRPO-style update mechanism. Since ESPO builds on PCRPO but also changes sampling, a dedicated ablation against 'PCRPO + same implementation + fixed sample size' is needed to isolate causality more convincingly.
