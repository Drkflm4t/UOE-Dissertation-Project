Summary: The paper proposes Efficient Safe Policy Optimization (ESPO), a primal-based safe reinforcement learning method that adaptively manipulates sample size based on the conflict between reward and safety gradients. ESPO combines a three-mode optimization scheme inherited from PCRPO/CRPO-style methods: reward-only updates in safe regions, cost-only updates under hard violations, and joint reward-cost updates in a soft-constraint region, with projected gradients when reward and safety gradients conflict. The main claimed contributions are: (i) adaptive sample-size control using gradient conflict as a signal, (ii) convergence and sample-efficiency theory in a tabular softmax-policy setting, and (iii) empirical gains on Safety-MuJoCo and Omnisafe benchmarks, including reduced sample counts and training time relative to several primal and primal-dual baselines.

Strengths: Addresses an important practical issue in safe RL: fixed sample budgets per iteration can be wasteful or insufficient depending on the optimization regime.; The high-level idea is intuitive: use gradient conflict between reward and safety objectives to decide when more samples are needed.; The method is simple to describe and implement on top of existing primal safe RL frameworks.; Empirical results on several benchmark tasks show improved reward and lower reported sample usage/training time relative to selected baselines.; The paper attempts to provide both theoretical and empirical support, which is valuable for safe RL work.

Weaknesses: The core novelty is limited: the optimization backbone is largely inherited from PCRPO, and the main addition is a heuristic sample-size adjustment rule.; There are serious inconsistencies between the algorithm description and the equations. In Section 4.1, Eq. (3) is described for conflicting gradients and Eq. (4) for aligned gradients, but Section 4.3/Algorithm 1 appears to swap these cases, suggesting a specification error in the practical method.; The theoretical claims about sample efficiency are weakly substantiated. Proposition 4.3 only states that ESPO needs fewer samples than a non-adaptive variant, but gives no explicit complexity bound; the proof relies on an ad hoc assumption (Assumption A.7) relating evaluation error linearly to sample size.; The theory is confined to a tabular softmax setting, whereas experiments are in deep continuous-control settings; the gap between theory and practice is large and not convincingly bridged.; Empirical evaluation is not fully convincing: only at least three seeds are used, no statistical significance tests are reported, and comparisons across different benchmarks/frameworks may not be apples-to-apples.; The reported sample-efficiency gains may be partly tautological because ESPO explicitly uses fewer samples per epoch than baselines with fixed budgets; the paper does not normalize performance by equal total environment interactions or provide matched-budget comparisons.; Safety evaluation is somewhat selective: plots show average episode cost, but there is limited reporting of violation rates, worst-case behavior, or cumulative constraint satisfaction during training.; Several theoretical statements appear overstated, e.g., 'globally optimal policy' convergence in a nonconvex policy-parameterized setting is claimed via tabular softmax/NPG-style analysis but not carefully qualified.; The paper has multiple notation and exposition issues, including inconsistent set definitions (e.g., Br/Bc naming versus mode descriptions), sign/conflict confusion, and typographical errors that make the method harder to trust.

Methodological Flaws: Major scientific-logic inconsistency: the update rules for the soft-constraint region are contradictory across Sections 4.1, 4.3, and Algorithm 1. Section 4.1 says use projected gradients (Eq. 3) when theta_{r,c} > 90 degrees and weighted sum (Eq. 4) when theta_{r,c} <= 90 degrees, but Section 4.3 and Algorithm 1 reverse these assignments. This is not a minor typo because it changes the actual algorithmic behavior.; The sample-efficiency proof is not rigorous enough for the claim made. Proposition 4.3 depends on Assumption A.7 (page 20), which posits a specific affine relation between policy-evaluation error delta_t and sample size. This assumption is neither derived from the RL setting nor justified empirically, so the claimed provable sample-efficiency improvement is not established from first principles.; The proof of Proposition 4.2 relies on 'adaptively choosing' weights x_t^r, x_t^c so that the algorithm remains in the safe/soft region, effectively assuming the desired property rather than deriving it. This weakens the claimed oscillation-reduction guarantee.; The convergence theorem is for a randomized output policy in a tabular setting with accurate policy evaluation, but the practical algorithm outputs the final iterate in deep RL. Thus the theorem does not directly justify the empirical implementation.; The empirical comparison is potentially confounded by unequal sampling budgets and implementation frameworks. ESPO changes steps per epoch while baselines keep fixed steps; without matched-interaction baselines or area-under-curve versus environment steps, it is difficult to attribute gains to better learning rather than altered budgeting.; The experiments use very few seeds ('at least three'), and no confidence intervals or hypothesis tests are reported. Given the variance of RL, this undermines empirical validity.; The paper compares against primal-dual methods on Omnisafe and primal methods on Safety-MuJoCo, but does not include stronger modern adaptive-budget or compute-aware baselines, so the evidence for state-of-the-art status is incomplete.; Some mathematical notation appears inconsistent or erroneous in the appendix (e.g., sign/conflict interpretation around cos(theta), swapped labels for B_r/B_c versus mode names, and references to equations that do not match the described cases), reducing confidence in proof correctness.
