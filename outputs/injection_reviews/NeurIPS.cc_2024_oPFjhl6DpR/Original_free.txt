This paper studies sample efficiency in safe reinforcement learning and proposes ESPO, a primal-style safe policy optimization method that adaptively changes the sample size based on the conflict between reward and safety gradients. The method builds on a three-mode optimization framework: reward-only updates when constraints are comfortably satisfied, cost-only updates when constraints are violated, and joint reward-cost updates in a soft-constraint region, with projected gradients used under conflict. The main additional idea is to increase sample size when reward and cost gradients conflict and decrease it otherwise. The paper provides a tabular softmax-policy analysis and empirical results on Safety-MuJoCo and Omnisafe benchmarks, claiming improved reward, safety, and reduced sample usage/training time relative to several primal and primal-dual baselines.

Overall, I find the paper interesting and potentially impactful because adaptive sampling is indeed underexplored in safe RL, and the intuition that gradient conflict should correlate with the need for more accurate updates is reasonable. The empirical results are promising at a high level, and the paper attempts to combine algorithmic design, theory, and experiments. However, in its current form, I have substantial concerns about the technical correctness and clarity of the method description, the strength of the theoretical claims, and the fairness/interpretability of the empirical evaluation. My assessment is therefore mixed: the core idea is worthwhile, but the paper needs significant revision before I would consider it ready for acceptance at a top-tier venue.

The main strength of the paper is the problem it targets. Sample inefficiency is a real bottleneck in safe RL, especially in on-policy methods where environment interaction is expensive. The paper also identifies a plausible signal for adaptive sampling: the angle between reward and cost gradients. This is more task-aware than generic variance-based batch adaptation and is conceptually aligned with the multi-objective nature of constrained RL. I also appreciate that the method is framed as a lightweight extension of existing primal methods rather than requiring a wholly new optimization stack. The experimental section reports consistent gains across several tasks, and the figures on pages 8 and 9 suggest that ESPO often improves both reward and cost relative to baselines while using fewer samples. If these gains hold under careful scrutiny, they would be meaningful.

That said, my biggest concern is that the algorithm description appears internally inconsistent in a way that makes it difficult to know what was actually implemented. In Section 4.1, equations (3) and (4) define the soft-constraint updates: equation (3) is the projected-gradient update for the conflict case with angle greater than 90 degrees, while equation (4) is the simple weighted combination for the non-conflict case. However, in Section 4.3, the prose seems to reverse these assignments: for angle less than or equal to 90 degrees it says the policy is updated according to equation (3), and for angle greater than 90 degrees it says equation (4). The same reversal appears in Algorithm 1 on page 26: line 19 checks whether the angle is at most 90 degrees, then line 21 applies equation (3), whereas line 24 applies equation (4) in the else branch. This directly contradicts Section 4.1. Since the distinction between conflict and non-conflict cases is central to the method, this is not a minor typo; it raises uncertainty about whether the implementation matches the intended algorithm and whether the reported results correspond to the claimed method.

Relatedly, the notation and mode naming are confusing throughout the theory section. In Section 4.1, “Safety Violations” corresponds to cost-only updates and “No Violations” corresponds to reward-only updates. But on page 5, after introducing sets Br, Bsoft, and Bc, the text says Br denotes “Safety Violation Response,” Bsoft denotes “Soft Constraint Violation Response,” and Bc denotes “No Violation Response,” which seems opposite to the natural mnemonic suggested by the subscripts and opposite to later usage in the appendix. In Lemma A.2 on page 13, the first line says “if t in Br” then the update uses Q_r, which is reward-only, while the fourth line says “if t in Bc” then the update uses Q_c, which is cost-only. So the naming in the main text is inconsistent with the appendix. This makes the proofs hard to follow and undermines confidence in the formal statements.

The theoretical contribution is ambitious, but I am not convinced the paper establishes what it claims. Theorem 4.1 proves a convergence rate in a tabular softmax setting with approximate policy evaluation and a randomized output policy. However, the theorem appears to establish essentially the same order as CRPO, and the paper itself acknowledges this on page 6. Thus the main novelty is not improved convergence rate but rather the sample manipulation and oscillation claims. Yet Proposition 4.3, which is supposed to show sample efficiency, is very weakly stated: “ESPO needs fewer number of samples than that without the sample manipulation.” This is qualitative rather than quantitative, and the proof in Appendix A.5 relies on Assumption A.7, which posits a specific linear relation between policy evaluation error and sample size around a base size. This assumption is introduced ad hoc and is not clearly justified from RL estimation theory. More importantly, the proposition does not provide an explicit sample complexity bound in terms of epsilon, delta, state/action sizes, or conflict frequency. Given the strong claims in the abstract about “improved sample complexity bounds,” the actual theorem/proposition support seems insufficient.

I also found the oscillation result in Proposition 4.2 somewhat problematic. The proposition claims that by adaptively choosing the parameters x_t^r and x_t^c, once ESPO enters the relaxed safe region it stays there and spends at least as many iterations optimizing reward as CRPO. But this is under an idealized setting in Appendix A.4 where policy evaluation error vanishes, and the proof seems to rely on choosing weights adaptively to ensure monotonicity. It is not clear how these weights are chosen in practice, whether such choices are always feasible, or whether the proposition says anything meaningful about the implemented algorithm. In the experiments, the paper reports on page 27 and Table 3 that ESPO spends many more updates in reward or reward-and-cost modes than CRPO, but this is more an empirical observation than a validation of the proposition’s assumptions.

Another issue is that the theory is disconnected from the practical deep RL setting used in experiments. The analysis is entirely tabular with softmax policies and approximate policy evaluation iterations T_pi, whereas the experiments use neural networks, PPO-style implementations, and continuous-control benchmarks. This gap is common in RL papers, but here the paper leans heavily on theory to justify the adaptive sampling mechanism. Since the sample manipulation itself is the main novelty, it would be helpful to either provide a more direct practical rationale, or at least discuss why the tabular analysis should be expected to transfer to actor-critic deep RL. As written, the theory mostly certifies a stylized version of the three-mode framework rather than the practical algorithm used in MuJoCo.

The empirical section is promising but not yet fully convincing. The reported gains are substantial: for example, Table 1 on page 7 reports 5.7M vs 8M samples on SafetyReacher-v4 and 6.2M vs 8M on SafetyWalker-v4; Table 2 on page 9 reports 7.3M vs 10M on SafetyHopperVelocity-v1 and 7.6M vs 10M on SafetyAntVelocity-v1. The bar charts on pages 8 and 28 also show reduced training time. However, several aspects of the evaluation need clarification.

First, the comparison across benchmarks is awkward. The paper compares against primal methods on Safety-MuJoCo and against primal-dual methods on Omnisafe, because of implementation convenience. But this means there is no single benchmark where all methods are compared under the same codebase and environment definitions. Since safe RL results can be highly sensitive to implementation details, this weakens the strength of the cross-paradigm claims in the abstract. Ideally, ESPO should be compared against both primal and primal-dual baselines within one unified benchmark and codebase, or at least the paper should be more careful not to overstate cross-family superiority.

Second, the sample-efficiency comparison may not be entirely fair because ESPO changes the number of environment steps per epoch while baselines use fixed steps per epoch. If all methods are trained for the same number of epochs, then ESPO simply sees fewer samples overall by design. The key question is whether performance should be compared as a function of environment interactions rather than epochs. The plots on pages 8 and 28 use training epochs on the x-axis, not total samples. This can visually favor a method that uses more or fewer samples per epoch depending on the regime. To support the sample-efficiency claim, the paper should include reward/cost versus total environment steps, not just versus epochs, and ideally area-under-curve or time-to-threshold metrics.

Third, the statistical rigor is limited. The paper states on page 29 that experiments use at least three random seeds. For top-tier RL standards, three seeds are usually not enough for strong claims, especially when improvements are moderate on some tasks. The figures show shaded regions, but the paper does not clearly state whether these are standard deviation, standard error, or confidence intervals. There are no significance tests, no aggregate metrics across tasks, and no reporting of variance in the sample counts themselves. Since adaptive sampling can introduce additional variability, stronger statistical evidence is needed.

Fourth, the ablation studies are too narrow to validate the central design choice. The ablations in Appendix C mainly vary cost limits and the multiplicative sample-size parameters (e.g., 1.2X vs 1.0X in Figure 4 on page 27). But the most important question is whether gradient-conflict-based adaptation is better than simpler alternatives. For example, how does ESPO compare to random sample-size variation, monotonic sample increase, variance-based adaptive sampling, or a heuristic based only on constraint violation magnitude? Without such baselines, it is hard to attribute gains specifically to the proposed conflict-aware criterion rather than to the general benefit of tuning batch size.

There are also several methodological details that need clarification. The paper says on page 29 that safety optimization starts after 40 epochs “to encourage more learning exploration.” This is a consequential design choice in safe RL, since it explicitly allows an initial unsafe phase. It is unclear whether the same warm-up is used for all baselines and whether this affects the safety comparisons. The sample adjustment parameters zeta-plus and zeta-minus are task-dependent (Table 5 on page 29), as are the slack parameters h-plus and h-minus (Table 6 on page 30), and some settings are extreme, such as h-plus = infinity or h-minus = minus infinity for certain tasks. This suggests substantial per-task tuning. The paper should discuss how sensitive ESPO is to these hyperparameters and whether baselines received comparable tuning effort. Otherwise, some of the empirical gains may reflect tuning budget rather than algorithmic superiority.

I also worry about the practical computation of the gradient conflict signal. In on-policy deep RL, estimating reward and cost gradients separately can itself be noisy and expensive. The paper motivates adaptive sampling as a way to reduce unnecessary computation, but if determining whether gradients conflict requires accurate estimates of both gradients every iteration, the overhead may offset some gains. The paper briefly notes that PCRPO computes both reward and safety gradients in each gradient handling step and faces computational challenges, but ESPO seems to inherit at least part of this burden. The runtime improvements in the figures suggest the overhead is manageable, but the paper should quantify the extra computation for conflict detection and explain how gradients are estimated in practice.

The writing quality is uneven. The high-level motivation is understandable, but there are many grammatical issues and notation inconsistencies. More importantly, several statements are imprecise or overstated. For example, the abstract claims “theoretically guarantees convergence, optimization stability, and improved sample complexity bounds,” but the actual theory for stability is indirect and the sample-complexity result is qualitative. The paper also repeatedly says ESPO “significantly outperforms” baselines, but without statistical testing this is too strong. Cleaning up these claims would improve credibility.

Despite these concerns, I do think the paper has a potentially valuable core idea. A conflict-aware adaptive sampling mechanism for safe RL is novel enough to merit attention, and the empirical trends are encouraging. But the current presentation leaves too many open questions about correctness, implementation, and evidence.

To strengthen the paper, I would strongly recommend the following revisions. First, fix the algorithmic inconsistencies between equations (3)/(4), Section 4.3, and Algorithm 1, and clearly state which update is used in each mode. Second, clean up the notation for Br, Bc, and Bsoft consistently across the main text and appendix. Third, substantially strengthen the empirical evaluation by plotting reward and cost against total environment interactions, not just epochs; increasing the number of seeds; and adding statistical summaries across tasks. Fourth, include ablations against alternative adaptive sampling rules to isolate the value of gradient conflict as the criterion. Fifth, clarify the tuning protocol for ESPO and baselines, especially the slack parameters, sample multipliers, and the 40-epoch safety warm-up. Sixth, temper the theoretical claims or provide a more explicit quantitative sample complexity theorem. Seventh, discuss the computational overhead of estimating gradient conflict and whether the method remains advantageous when this overhead is included.

In summary, my view is that the paper addresses an important problem and proposes an intuitively appealing idea, but the current version falls short on technical clarity and evidential strength. The empirical results are encouraging enough that I would be interested in a revised version, but I do not think the present manuscript is yet at the standard expected for a top-tier ML conference.
