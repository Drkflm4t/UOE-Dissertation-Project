This paper studies sample efficiency in safe reinforcement learning and proposes ESPO, a primal-style safe policy optimization method that adaptively manipulates sample size based on the degree of conflict between reward and safety gradients. The core idea is intuitive and appealing: when reward and safety objectives are aligned, fewer samples may suffice, whereas when they conflict, additional samples are allocated to better resolve the trade-off. The method is built on a three-mode optimization framework inherited from PCRPO-like logic: reward-only updates in clearly safe regions, cost-only updates under clear violations, and joint reward-cost updates in a soft-constraint region. The paper combines this optimization structure with adaptive sample sizing and provides both theoretical analysis and empirical evaluation on Safety-MuJoCo and Omnisafe tasks.

Overall, I found the paper interesting and potentially impactful. The problem is important: safe RL is often prohibitively sample-inefficient, and most prior work indeed fixes rollout budgets per iteration without considering whether the current optimization regime actually warrants that cost. The paper’s central contribution—using reward-cost gradient conflict as a signal for adaptive sampling—is novel in the safe RL context and conceptually well motivated. I also appreciate that the authors do not stop at an algorithmic heuristic, but attempt to connect the idea to convergence, oscillation reduction, and sample complexity. Empirically, the reported gains are substantial, with lower sample usage and training time while maintaining or improving reward and safety performance.

The strongest aspect of the paper is the framing of adaptive sampling through the geometry of reward and cost gradients. This is a natural signal in constrained RL, and the paper explains the intuition clearly. The figure on page 2 and the oscillation discussion are helpful: the claim is that existing methods can bounce across the safe boundary, whereas ESPO spends more time in a productive region where reward can still be optimized without repeatedly incurring hard safety corrections. This is a meaningful perspective, especially for primal methods where switching between reward and cost objectives can otherwise be brittle. The update rules in Section 4.1 are also sensible extensions of gradient manipulation ideas from PCRPO and multi-objective optimization.

The theoretical section is ambitious and is another strength of the submission. Theorem 4.1 gives a convergence guarantee in the tabular softmax setting with rate on the order of O~(sqrt(SA / ((1-gamma)^3 T))) for both reward suboptimality and constraint violation. Proposition 4.2 formalizes the reduced-oscillation intuition by arguing that once ESPO enters the relaxed safe region, it can remain there and continue at least partial reward optimization, unlike CRPO which may revert to pure cost updates more often. Proposition 4.3 then argues that the adaptive sample manipulation yields fewer samples than a non-adaptive counterpart for achieving target reward/cost gaps. Even though the theory is restricted to the tabular setting, it is still valuable because it gives a principled foundation for a design choice that is usually treated heuristically in deep RL papers.

The empirical section is also fairly convincing at a high level. On Safety-MuJoCo, the plots on page 8 and the additional results on page 28 suggest that ESPO improves reward while keeping costs under control, and does so with fewer samples than CRPO and PCRPO. The sample counts in Table 1 are notable: 5.7M vs 8M on SafetyReacher-v4, 6.2M vs 8M on SafetyWalker-v4, and 5.1M vs 8M on SafetyHumanoidStandup-v4. On Omnisafe, the comparisons against PCPO, CUP, and PPOLag in Figure 3 and Table 2 are similarly encouraging, with ESPO using 7.3M/7.6M samples versus 10M for the baselines on the two reported tasks. The training-time reductions shown in the bar charts are also practically relevant. I also appreciated the ablation section and the update-style analysis in Table 3 on page 28, which supports the claim that ESPO spends many more updates in reward or reward-cost modes than CRPO.

That said, there are several issues that should be addressed to strengthen the paper.

My main concern is that the theoretical claims around sample efficiency are not yet as rigorous as the presentation suggests. Proposition 4.3 depends on Assumption A.7 in the appendix, which posits a particular relation between policy-evaluation error and sample size. This assumption is only qualitatively motivated and appears to be introduced specifically to make the adaptive-sampling argument go through. As written, the proposition is more of a conditional argument than a fully derived sample complexity theorem. Since sample efficiency is one of the headline contributions, the paper should be more explicit in the main text that this result relies on an additional assumption beyond the convergence theorem. Ideally, the authors would either derive the dependence from a standard concentration result in their setting or soften the wording around “provable sample efficiency.”

A second concern is a mismatch between the theory and the practical algorithm. The theory is developed for tabular softmax policies with approximate NPG-style updates, whereas the experiments are on continuous-control deep RL benchmarks with neural networks and PPO/PCPO/CUP-style baselines. This gap is common in RL papers, but here it matters because the adaptive sampling mechanism is the main novelty, and its behavior may depend strongly on noisy gradient estimates in high-dimensional function approximation. The paper would benefit from a clearer explanation of how gradients are estimated in practice, how the angle theta_{r,c} is computed robustly, and whether the sample-size adaptation itself introduces instability in deep settings. The practical algorithm in Appendix B is somewhat underspecified in this regard.

Relatedly, there seems to be some inconsistency in the algorithm description around Equations (3) and (4). In Section 4.1, Equation (3) is described as the projected update used when theta_{r,c} > 90 degrees, and Equation (4) as the weighted sum used when theta_{r,c} <= 90 degrees. However, in Section 4.3 and Algorithm 1, the mapping between angle conditions and update equations appears reversed in places. For example, page 5 states that for soft constraint violations with gradient angle <= 90 degrees, the policy is updated according to Equation (3), and for angle > 90 degrees according to Equation (4), which contradicts the earlier description. This is likely a presentation bug rather than a conceptual flaw, but it is important because it affects reproducibility and interpretation.

There are also some ambiguities in the sample-size adaptation rule itself. Equations (6) and (7) define Xt+1 = X + X zeta_t^+ or X + X zeta_t^- depending on conflict, but it is not fully clear whether X is a fixed base batch size reset every iteration or whether Xt evolves recursively over time. The notation suggests recursion, but the formula uses X rather than Xt. Algorithm 1 also refers to iterative changes in zeta_t^+ and zeta_t^- without much justification for those schedules. Since the empirical gains depend heavily on these mechanics, the paper should explain them more carefully and perhaps provide pseudocode with exact rollout-budget updates.

The experimental evaluation is promising but still somewhat limited in breadth and statistical depth. The paper reports results on a modest number of tasks, and although it mentions using at least three random seeds on page 29, the plots do not clearly indicate variance bars or confidence intervals in a way that supports strong statistical conclusions. Given the variability of safe RL, especially on MuJoCo-style tasks, it would be helpful to report mean ± standard deviation over more seeds and perhaps aggregate metrics such as normalized regret, cumulative constraint violation, or area under the reward-cost curve. In addition, because ESPO is compared across two different benchmark suites with somewhat different safety definitions, a more unified evaluation protocol would make the empirical story cleaner.

Another issue is fairness of comparison and implementation detail. The paper compares against both primal and primal-dual methods, which is useful, but the training-time comparisons may be influenced by implementation differences, hardware, and framework overhead. The authors do acknowledge on page 9 that sample reduction does not necessarily translate directly to time reduction because of benchmark characteristics and parallel processing. Still, if training time is emphasized as a key result, the paper should more carefully normalize for implementation factors or focus more on environment interactions, which are the more standard measure in RL. Also, ESPO is implemented inside Omnisafe for some comparisons, but it is not entirely clear whether all baselines were re-tuned equally under the same compute budget and whether the chosen hyperparameters are optimal for each method in the tested tasks.

I also think the paper could do a better job clarifying novelty relative to PCRPO. Conceptually, ESPO appears to combine PCRPO’s three-mode gradient handling with an adaptive sample-size rule based on gradient conflict. That is a meaningful extension, but the paper sometimes presents the contribution as broader than it is. In particular, the optimization logic itself is not new, and some of the theoretical analysis seems to inherit heavily from CRPO/PCRPO proof templates. This is not a problem, but the paper should more sharply distinguish what is genuinely new: the conflict-aware sampling criterion, the oscillation interpretation, and the adaptation of the analysis to this setting.

There are also a number of writing and notation issues throughout the paper. Several typos and grammatical errors appear in the main text and appendix. Some notation is overloaded or inconsistent, especially around Br/Bc/Bsoft and the naming of “Safety Violation Response” versus “No Violation Response” on page 5. The appendix contains a few likely typographical mistakes in equations and references to equation numbers. These do not invalidate the work, but they make the theory harder to verify than necessary.

Despite these concerns, I think the paper has real merit. The central idea is simple, relevant, and plausibly useful beyond the specific algorithm studied here. The empirical results are strong enough to suggest that the method is not merely a theoretical curiosity, and the attempt to connect adaptive sampling with reward-safety gradient geometry is original. I especially like that the paper targets a practical bottleneck in safe RL—wasted interactions under fixed rollout budgets—rather than only proposing another constrained optimizer. If the authors can tighten the exposition, resolve the equation/algorithm inconsistencies, and better qualify the theoretical sample-efficiency claim, this would be a solid contribution.

In summary, this is a good paper on an important problem. Its main strengths are a novel conflict-aware adaptive sampling idea for safe RL, a thoughtful integration with three-mode primal optimization, meaningful theoretical analysis in the tabular setting, and strong empirical improvements in reward, safety, sample usage, and training time on standard benchmarks. Its main weaknesses are the gap between theory and deep-RL practice, some ambiguity and inconsistency in the algorithm description, and limited rigor in the sample-efficiency theorem relative to how strongly it is advertised. I view these as addressable issues rather than fatal flaws. My overall assessment is positive, and I would lean toward acceptance if the authors can clarify the presentation and strengthen the empirical/theoretical alignment.

A few concrete suggestions for improvement are: explicitly fix the inconsistency between Equations (3)/(4) and Algorithm 1; clarify whether sample size is updated recursively and how zeta schedules are chosen; report more seeds and statistical summaries; include an ablation isolating the effect of adaptive sampling from the three-mode update logic; compare against a simpler adaptive-batch baseline based on variance or uncertainty rather than gradient conflict; and discuss computational overhead from estimating both reward and cost gradients and their angle each iteration. These additions would make the paper significantly stronger and easier to trust and reproduce.
