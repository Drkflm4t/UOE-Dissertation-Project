This paper studies redundancy and over-parameterization in multi-head attention and proposes a two-stage “divide-and-conquer” approach: Group-Constrained Training (GCT), which encourages heads to organize into groups with high intra-group similarity and high inter-group diversity, and Voting-to-Stay (V2S), which prunes each group down to a representative “Pillar of Strength” head. The resulting models, GHT and GHT-PS, are evaluated on machine translation, language modeling, and abstractive summarization, with reported gains in both accuracy and efficiency. Overall, I found the paper interesting, well motivated, and empirically strong. The central idea is intuitive yet nontrivial: instead of only diversifying heads or only pruning them, the method first structures redundancy and then exploits it. That is a meaningful conceptual contribution relative to prior work on head pruning, head homogenization, and disagreement regularization.

A major strength of the paper is the clarity of its motivating hypothesis. The authors argue that the best use of attention capacity is not to preserve all heads nor to collapse them indiscriminately, but to retain a small set of representative and distinctive feature processors. This framing connects naturally to minimum-redundancy feature selection and helps unify several strands of prior work. I also appreciated that the paper explicitly positions itself against three families of methods discussed in the introduction and related work: homogenization-based approaches, diversification-based approaches, and significance-based pruning. The proposed method is not merely another pruning heuristic; it attempts to shape the geometry of head behavior before pruning. That makes the contribution more substantive than a purely empirical compression trick.

Technically, the method is reasonably novel. The Grouped Head Attention formulation on pages 3–4, together with the diagram in Figure 1 on page 3, gives a clear picture of how hidden group assignments supervise the heads. The use of unsupervised clustering over head feature maps to derive self-supervision is an appealing design choice. The paper extracts three possible feature maps—value projections, attention maps, and head outputs—and uses them to induce group structure. The loss in Eq. (4) combines an intra-group homogenization term and an inter-group diversification term, which is a sensible objective if the goal is to create pruneable redundancy within groups while preserving diversity across groups. The V2S procedure on page 5 is also easy to understand: after convergence, each group votes for the most representative head, and the others are masked out. This is a neat mechanism, and it is more interpretable than many learned importance-score pruning methods.

The empirical section is a strong point. The paper evaluates on three fairly different NLP settings: encoder-decoder machine translation, decoder-only language modeling, and abstractive summarization. This breadth is valuable because many efficiency papers only validate on one task. The machine translation results in Tables 1 and 2 on page 6 are particularly compelling. Against the vanilla transformer, GHT improves BLEU consistently at the same parameter count, and GHT-PS preserves most of the gains while reducing parameters from 44M to 30M. Against prior efficient-attention or pruning baselines, the reported margins are also substantial. The summarization results in Table 4 on page 8 and the language modeling results in Table 6 on page 8 further support the claim that the method is not narrowly tuned to MT. It is especially encouraging that GHT-PS often matches or slightly exceeds GHT despite pruning, which is consistent with the authors’ redundancy hypothesis.

I also want to highlight the analysis section as a meaningful contribution rather than filler. The ablations in Table 3 on page 6 are useful and mostly well chosen: removing diversification or homogenization hurts, skipping GCT before pruning hurts, and replacing the discovered representative heads with random choices hurts. The compactness analysis in Figure 2 on page 7 is interesting because it attempts to validate the paper’s central claim that there is an optimal range of group compactness. Likewise, Figure 3 on page 7 shows that neither the degenerate one-group case nor the maximal-group case is best, which supports the argument that the method is balancing homogenization and diversification rather than simply favoring one. Figure 4 on page 8 also gives some evidence that the proposed training objective actually changes head organization over time, instead of merely acting as an auxiliary regularizer with unclear effect.

That said, there are several issues that should be addressed to strengthen the paper further.

My main concern is whether the comparisons are fully controlled and sufficiently fair across all baselines. Some baselines in Tables 2, 4, and 6 come from somewhat different architectural families or parameter scales, and in a few cases it is not entirely clear whether they were reimplemented under the same training pipeline or taken from prior reports. For example, in language modeling, the comparison includes S4, BERT-L-CAS, GPT-2 Large, and a vanilla transformer with adaptive input, but these models differ substantially in scale and setup. The paper does compare directly to its own backbone, which is good, but the broader SOTA framing would be more convincing if the authors clarified exactly which baselines were rerun under matched settings and which were cited from prior work. Similarly, in MT, some baselines are controlled to the same parameter count, but others may differ in optimization details or implementation maturity. This does not invalidate the results, but the paper should be more explicit.

A second concern is the dependence on several design choices that are only partially explored. The method relies on clustering heads into a fixed number of groups C, selecting one of several feature maps for clustering, and tuning the coefficients alpha and beta. The appendix tables on page 13 show that different datasets prefer different settings of alpha, beta, and feature map choice. This suggests the method may require nontrivial task-specific tuning. Figure 3 on page 7 also indicates that performance depends on the number of hidden units/groups. While the paper does analyze this dependence, it would help to better quantify robustness: how sensitive is performance to suboptimal C, alpha, beta, and feature-map selection? Can one choose a single default configuration that works well across tasks? As written, the method appears effective but somewhat configuration-heavy.

Relatedly, the role of the clustering algorithm deserves more scrutiny. The paper uses K-means for fairness and simplicity, but the hidden-unit discovery system is central to the approach. Since K-means imposes spherical cluster assumptions and requires a preset number of clusters, it would be useful to know whether the gains are robust to alternative clustering methods or whether the discovered groups are stable across random seeds. The paper mentions that other clustering algorithms may be applicable, but does not test this. Because the grouping is the foundation for both GCT and V2S, some evidence of stability would strengthen confidence in the method.

Another issue is computational overhead during training. The paper emphasizes inference efficiency and parameter reduction, and the test-time gains are clearly reported in the tables on pages 6 and 8. However, GCT introduces auxiliary computations and HUDS clustering, and V2S requires a voting epoch after convergence. The paper does not clearly quantify the additional training cost in wall-clock time or memory. This matters because some efficiency methods shift cost from inference to training. If the training overhead is modest, the authors should say so explicitly and report it. If it is substantial, that should be discussed as part of the tradeoff.

I also think the theoretical claims could be stated a bit more carefully. The paper repeatedly argues that higher compactness of group patterns improves performance “in a certain range,” and Figure 2 on page 7 indeed suggests a non-monotonic relationship. But this is still empirical correlation rather than a principled explanation. Likewise, the connection to the Lottery Ticket Hypothesis is suggestive but not really established. The paper would be stronger if it framed these as intuitions supported by experiments rather than broader explanatory claims. This is not a fatal issue, but some moderation in wording would improve scientific precision.

There are also some presentation issues. The notation in Section 3 is fairly dense and occasionally hard to parse. For example, the indexing around hidden units and grouped feature maps on pages 3–4 is more complicated than necessary, and there are places where notation appears overloaded or slightly inconsistent. The paper would benefit from a cleaner notation table and perhaps a short worked example for one layer with a small number of heads and groups. Algorithm 1 on page 5 is understandable at a high level, but some details are underspecified, such as the exact convergence criterion rho and how often clustering assignments are refreshed during training. These are likely implementation details the authors know well, but readers would benefit from more explicitness.

I would also have liked stronger evidence that the learned groups correspond to semantically meaningful or functionally distinct head behaviors, beyond compactness metrics. Since the paper’s premise is that each group focuses on an “essential but distinctive feature subset,” some qualitative visualization of attention patterns or case studies of grouped heads would be valuable. For example, do different groups specialize in locality, syntax, rare words, long-range dependencies, or positional patterns? The current analysis shows that groups become compact and separable, but not what they represent. Such interpretability evidence is not strictly necessary for acceptance, but it would make the paper more insightful.

The limitations section is appropriately candid in noting that experiments are restricted to NLP and training-from-scratch settings. I agree these are real limitations. In particular, it remains unclear whether the method transfers to large pretrained transformers, where head redundancy may behave differently and where retraining from scratch is often impractical. Similarly, extension to vision transformers or multimodal transformers would be important for broader impact. These are natural next steps rather than flaws in the current work, but they should temper the scope of the claims.

Despite these concerns, my overall assessment is positive. The paper presents a coherent idea, a reasonably novel mechanism, and a broad empirical evaluation with strong results. The combination of grouping-based self-supervision and representative-head pruning is elegant and appears effective. The analysis is better than average for this type of paper, especially the compactness and group-number studies on page 7 and the efficiency comparisons on page 8. While I would like more clarity on training overhead, robustness to clustering/design choices, and fairness of some baseline comparisons, these issues seem addressable and do not undermine the core contribution.

In summary, I think this is a solid paper with meaningful practical and conceptual contributions to efficient transformer design. Its main strengths are the well-motivated formulation, the integration of diversification and pruning into a single framework, and the consistently strong results across multiple tasks. Its main weaknesses are incomplete discussion of training cost, some sensitivity to hyperparameters and grouping choices, and limited evidence about the semantic meaning or stability of the discovered groups. I would recommend acceptance if the venue values efficient transformer architectures and empirical NLP validation, and I encourage the authors to strengthen the final version with clearer implementation details, more controlled baseline reporting, and additional robustness analysis.
