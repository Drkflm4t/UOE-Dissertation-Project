This paper proposes Grouped Head Attention (GHA), a modification/training strategy for multi-head attention intended to reduce redundancy and over-parameterization. The core idea is to partition heads into groups via a self-supervised Group-Constrained Training (GCT) objective that encourages homogenization within groups and diversification across groups. After training, a Voting-to-Stay (V2S) procedure identifies one “Pillar of Strength” head per group and prunes the remaining heads, yielding GHA-PS / GHT-PS. The paper evaluates the approach on machine translation, abstractive summarization, and language modeling, reporting both accuracy gains and parameter/inference improvements over vanilla Transformers and several prior efficient-attention baselines.

Overall, I found the paper interesting and reasonably well-motivated. The problem it addresses is important: attention-head redundancy is well documented, and methods that can improve both quality and efficiency are valuable. The paper’s central intuition—encouraging a structured decomposition where groups specialize to distinct feature subsets while redundancy is concentrated within groups and then pruned—is appealing. The empirical section is broad, spanning three tasks, and the paper includes several analyses beyond headline numbers, including ablations, compactness-performance correlations, group-number sensitivity, and efficiency comparisons. These are meaningful strengths.

At the same time, I have substantial reservations about the methodological clarity, the strength of the causal claims, and some aspects of the experimental validation. My overall assessment is positive but cautious: the paper appears to contain a useful idea and promising empirical results, but several claims are stronger than what is fully established, and some design choices remain under-justified.

The main strength of the paper is the conceptual combination of grouping and pruning. Prior work often emphasizes either diversification, homogenization, or importance-based pruning. This paper’s “divide-and-conquer” framing is a sensible synthesis: first induce structured redundancy within groups and separation across groups, then prune within-group redundancy. That is a more coherent story than simply regularizing heads to be different or pruning them post hoc. The diagram on page 3 is helpful in conveying this pipeline: heads are grouped under hidden units discovered by an unsupervised HUDS module, and after convergence only the representative PS head in each group is retained. This is one of the paper’s clearest contributions.

A second strength is the breadth of empirical evaluation. The machine translation results in Tables 1 and 2 on page 6 are strong at face value. In particular, GHT improves over the vanilla Transformer at equal parameter count, and GHT-PS remains competitive or better after compression. The summarization results in Table 4 on page 8 are also notable, especially the large Rouge-2 gain over the vanilla Transformer baseline. The language modeling results in Table 6 on page 8 similarly suggest that the method is not narrowly tuned to encoder-decoder MT. Cross-task consistency is important here and increases confidence that the method is not exploiting a single benchmark peculiarity.

A third strength is that the paper does attempt to probe mechanism rather than only report end-task metrics. The compactness analysis in Figure 2 on page 7, the group-number sweep in Figure 3, and the training-dynamics plot in Figure 4 are all useful. Even if not fully conclusive, they show an effort to connect the proposed regularization to measurable structural effects in the learned heads. The ablation table on page 6 is also helpful and suggests that both the grouping constraint and the PS-selection procedure matter.

That said, my biggest concern is that the method is not described with enough precision to make the contribution fully reproducible or theoretically interpretable. The paper introduces hidden units discovered by an unsupervised algorithm such as k-means, but the exact representation being clustered is somewhat underspecified. Equation (3) suggests clustering feature maps per layer, but the feature maps themselves can be values, attention weights, or head outputs, and the appendix later reveals that the chosen feature map differs by dataset and even by model variant (Tables 7 and 8 on page 13). This makes the method feel more heuristic than the main text suggests. The fact that one of V-hat, A, or O is selected per dataset, with different alpha/beta settings also tuned per dataset, raises concerns about sensitivity and hidden search effort. The paper would be stronger if it clearly stated how these choices were selected, over what validation budget, and whether the gains persist under a fixed configuration across tasks.

Relatedly, the self-supervised objective in Equation (4) is difficult to parse and not fully convincing as presented. The paper offers two variants, categorical and continuous hidden units, but then states that the metric-learning version is used in experiments because it performs better and avoids extra projections. In practice, then, the categorical formulation seems unnecessary and distracts from the main method. More importantly, the notation is quite hard to follow: the indexing over heads, groups, layers, and feature maps is dense, and the role of z as either assigned hidden unit or group prototype is not always cleanly separated. I had to infer the intended behavior rather than understand it directly from the equations. For a top-tier conference paper, the methodology section should be substantially clearer.

A second major concern is the novelty relative to existing regularization-plus-pruning approaches. The paper positions itself against homogenization-only, diversification-only, and significance-based pruning methods, which is fair. However, at a higher level, the method can be viewed as clustering heads based on internal representations, regularizing them toward cluster structure, and then pruning cluster members except a representative. That is a sensible combination, but the paper could do more to articulate why this is fundamentally new rather than an engineering composition of known ingredients. The “Pillars of Strength” terminology is memorable, but the underlying V2S procedure is essentially representative-head selection after convergence. The paper would benefit from a sharper statement of what is algorithmically novel beyond combining clustering, similarity regularization, and pruning.

I am also not fully persuaded by some of the causal interpretations. For example, the paper claims that higher compactness of attention-head groups improves performance “in a certain range,” supported by Figure 2 on page 7. But this figure appears to plot final BLEU against compactness across independent runs, likely induced by varying alpha and beta. This is a correlation, not a controlled causal demonstration. It is plausible that both compactness and BLEU are jointly affected by hyperparameter settings or optimization dynamics. Similarly, Figure 4 shows that GHT has stronger intra-group homogeneity and inter-group diversity trends than the vanilla Transformer, but that is almost tautological given the training objective explicitly optimizes these quantities. What matters is whether these structural changes are the reason for better task performance, and the evidence for that is suggestive rather than definitive.

The experimental comparisons are generally good, but there are some fairness issues. In Table 2 on page 6, the baselines include methods from different papers and possibly different implementations/training recipes. The paper says all models are based on fairseq implementations, which helps, but it is not always clear whether each baseline was reimplemented under a unified setup or whether numbers were taken from prior work. This matters because some of the reported gains are large, and efficient Transformer variants can be sensitive to training details. The paper should explicitly state for each baseline whether it was reproduced by the authors or copied from the literature, and if reproduced, whether hyperparameters were tuned comparably.

The efficiency claims also deserve a more careful treatment. The paper reports parameter count, FLOPs, and inference speed. This is good in principle, but some comparisons are not apples-to-apples. For example, GHT has the same FLOPs and nearly the same speed as the vanilla Transformer, so its efficiency advantage is really about accuracy at fixed cost, not lower cost. GHT-PS does reduce parameters and FLOPs, but the speedups are modest in some MT tables and much larger in LM. The paper notes in a footnote that masking is used during pruning analysis and “real head removal” is used for inference speed, which is appropriate, but the exact deployment setup is not described in enough detail. Since structured pruning often yields smaller practical speedups than theoretical FLOP reductions, more transparency would help. It would also be useful to report training-time overhead, because the proposed method includes clustering/HUDS and an extra voting epoch. If training becomes substantially more expensive, that changes the practical value proposition.

Another weakness is that the method appears to rely on several discrete stages and heuristics: train with GCT, detect convergence via center shift threshold, freeze the model, collect votes over an epoch, build masks, prune, then finetune. This pipeline may work, but it is operationally more complex than many alternatives. The paper does not quantify how sensitive results are to the convergence threshold, the voting duration, or the finetuning schedule. Algorithm 1 on page 5 gives a high-level procedure, but important implementation details are omitted. For example, how often is clustering recomputed during training? Are hidden units updated every step, every epoch, or only at certain checkpoints? How expensive is HUDS relative to standard training? These details are important for assessing practicality.

I also have some concerns about the ablation design. Table 3 on page 6 is useful, but several ablations are not fully controlled. For instance, “w/o HUDS” replaces HUDS with random switching off heads after GCT, and “w/o PS stay” keeps random group members instead of PS heads. These show that informed pruning is better than random pruning, which is expected. More informative would be comparisons against simpler deterministic representatives, such as keeping the head closest to the group centroid, or the head with highest average attention entropy, or standard importance scores within each group. Likewise, “w/o GC” and “w/o GCT” are somewhat ambiguous because they alter both the training dynamics and the pruning readiness. A stronger ablation would isolate whether grouping alone helps without pruning, whether pruning alone helps without grouping, and whether the gains are additive.

The paper’s claims about statistical significance should also be interpreted carefully. Many tables state that improvements are statistically significant under a t-test, but the number of runs is not clearly reported for the main results. Without knowing the sample size and whether seeds were varied independently, these significance statements are hard to evaluate. For neural sequence models, variance across runs can be nontrivial, and significance testing over a very small number of seeds is not especially meaningful. The paper should report mean and standard deviation over multiple runs, at least for representative datasets.

There are also some presentation issues. The writing is generally understandable, but there are many grammatical infelicities and awkward phrasings. More importantly, terminology is occasionally inconsistent: “homology” appears where “homogeneity” is intended; “homogenization” and “homologizing” are both used; “group compactness” is discussed but not always formally defined in the main text. The notation in Section 3 is dense and could be simplified substantially. The appendix tables on page 13 reveal that different datasets use different feature maps and alpha/beta settings, but this important fact is buried too late. It should be surfaced in the main paper because it materially affects how one interprets robustness and generality.

Despite these concerns, I do think the empirical evidence suggests the method has merit. The MT results are consistently strong, and the fact that GHT-PS often matches or nearly matches GHT while using fewer parameters supports the central premise that structured redundancy can be induced and then removed. The group-number sweep in Figure 3 on page 7 is particularly informative: performance is best for an intermediate number of groups, which aligns with the paper’s intuition that neither full homogenization nor full diversification is ideal. This is a nice result and could inspire follow-up work.

To strengthen the paper, I would recommend several concrete improvements. First, simplify and sharpen the method description. Focus on the metric-learning version actually used in experiments, define exactly what is clustered and when, and provide pseudocode for the full training loop including HUDS updates. Second, make the hyperparameter-selection process transparent. Since alpha, beta, and feature-map choice vary by dataset, report the search space and validation protocol, and ideally include a fixed-configuration experiment to demonstrate robustness. Third, add stronger baselines and ablations: compare against centroid-based representative selection, within-group importance pruning, and perhaps a simple cluster-then-prune method without the explicit GCT regularizer. Fourth, report training overhead and multi-seed variance. Fifth, if space permits, include qualitative analysis of learned groups or representative heads—for example, whether different groups correspond to syntactic vs. positional vs. lexical patterns, or whether PS heads are stable across seeds.

I also think the paper should moderate some of its broader claims. Statements such as “higher compactness improves MHA’s performance” should be phrased more carefully as an empirical observation under the proposed regularization and within a limited range of settings. Likewise, the connection to the Lottery Ticket Hypothesis is more of an analogy than a demonstrated theoretical link; the paper does not actually test lottery-ticket-style rewinding or sparse subnetwork trainability. A more restrained framing would improve credibility.

In summary, this is a promising paper with a good high-level idea, broad experiments, and encouraging results. Its main contribution is a structured approach to concentrating redundancy within head groups and then pruning to representative heads. The empirical gains across MT, summarization, and LM are impressive enough to take seriously. However, the method is somewhat heuristic, under-specified in key places, and more sensitive to dataset-specific choices than the main narrative initially suggests. I view it as a solid empirical contribution with moderate novelty and incomplete methodological clarity. If accepted, it would benefit from revision toward clearer exposition, stronger reproducibility, and more careful claims.

My recommendation would be weak accept to borderline accept, depending on the conference bar and the strength of competing submissions. The idea is useful and the results are strong, but the paper falls short of being fully convincing on rigor and clarity.
