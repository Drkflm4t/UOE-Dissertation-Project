This paper proposes Pushdown Layers, a modified self-attention mechanism for autoregressive Transformer language models that explicitly tracks recursive structure through a stack-like “stack tape” encoding token depths in an incremental parse. The central idea is elegant: as the model predicts each next token, it also predicts an attachment decision that updates an implicit shift-reduce parse state; the resulting depth information is then injected into attention through additive depth embeddings on keys. The method is positioned as a drop-in replacement for standard self-attention, trained with silver constituency parses, and evaluated on synthetic Dyck languages, sentence-level syntactic generalization benchmarks, a newly constructed parsed Wikipedia corpus, and downstream GLUE-style classification tasks after GPT-2 finetuning.

Overall, I found the paper strong, technically interesting, and well motivated. The problem setting is important: despite the empirical success of Transformers, recursive structure remains a persistent weakness, especially under distribution shift and in low-data regimes. The paper identifies a concrete architectural gap—lack of explicit recursive state tracking—and addresses it with a mechanism that is substantially more structured than ordinary self-attention, yet less cumbersome than prior transition-based syntactic language models that expand the output space with structural tokens. This is a meaningful contribution. The proposed layer is conceptually clean, the paper is generally clear, and the empirical results are broad enough to support the main claims.

The strongest aspect of the paper is the design itself. The stack tape is a clever compromise between full symbolic parsing and unconstrained neural attention. Rather than forcing hard attention masks or requiring the model to emit long sequences of parse actions, the method uses a compact structural state that softly biases attention. This seems like exactly the right level of inductive bias for the stated goal. The illustrations are helpful here: the schematic on page 1 and the worked example on page 4 make the mechanism intuitive, and Figure 2 on page 4 clearly shows how attachment decisions correspond to constituent reductions and depth updates. I also appreciated that the authors provide explicit pseudocode for both the Pushdown attention block and the attachment head in the appendix (pages 14–15), which improves reproducibility and makes the proposal feel concrete rather than merely conceptual.

The paper also does a good job of situating itself relative to prior syntactic language models. The discussion on pages 2–3 clearly explains why prior parsing-as-language-modeling approaches and Transformer Grammars are not fully satisfactory: they increase sequence length, complicate decoding, and often impose hard structural constraints. Pushdown Layers appear to preserve much of the benefit of explicit syntax while avoiding these drawbacks. This distinction is important and, in my view, one of the paper’s main conceptual advances.

Empirically, the results are compelling. The Dyck experiments are a good sanity check and support the core recursion claim. Table 1 on page 5 shows large gains both for deeper nesting and longer-range dependencies, with especially striking improvements at larger dependency lengths and out-of-distribution depths. These synthetic results alone would not be sufficient, but they nicely validate the intended inductive bias.

The sentence-level language modeling experiments are stronger evidence. On BLLIP-LG, the model improves substantially over a standard Transformer LM on BLIMP and the SG test suites, while maintaining similar perplexity. Table 2 on page 7 is particularly persuasive because it compares against both unstructured and structured baselines. Pushdown-LM achieves the best BLIMP score among the listed models and is competitive with or better than prior structured approaches on SG. Figure 3 on page 6 is also informative: the model performs especially well on center embedding and long-distance dependencies, which are exactly the kinds of phenomena one would hope an explicit recursive-state mechanism would help with. That alignment between architectural motivation and empirical gains strengthens the paper considerably.

The WikiTrees experiments further improve the paper by addressing scale and sample efficiency. Figure 4 on page 7 is one of the most convincing plots in the paper: it shows that adding Pushdown Layers to the upper half of a GPT2-small-style model yields markedly better syntactic generalization at 10M, 50M, and 100M tokens, with a substantial sample-efficiency advantage over the baseline. This is an important result because it suggests the method is not merely a niche improvement on small curated corpora, but may actually matter in realistic pretraining regimes where inductive bias can trade off against data scale.

I also liked that the paper goes beyond intrinsic syntax benchmarks and tests downstream transfer. The gains on GLUE-style tasks in Table 3 on page 7 are modest, but still useful as evidence that the architectural change does not only optimize for synthetic syntax metrics. In particular, improvements on RTE, MRPC, and STS-B suggest that the learned structural bias may help sentence understanding more broadly. I would not oversell these gains, but they are directionally encouraging.

The analysis section is another strength. The parsing result in Table 4 on page 8 is surprisingly strong: recovering competitive unlabeled F1 from a language model trained with silver parses suggests that the model is genuinely learning a coherent latent incremental structure rather than merely exploiting the supervision as an auxiliary signal. The attention analysis in Figures 5 and 6 on pages 8–9 is also useful. It provides a mechanistic story for the subject-verb agreement improvements by showing reduced attention to distractor nouns. This kind of analysis is not definitive, but it is exactly the sort of evidence I would want to see for a paper making architectural claims about recursive computation.

That said, there are a few limitations and open questions that should be discussed more carefully.

First, the method depends on constituency parse supervision, and in the larger-scale setting this supervision is silver rather than gold. The paper acknowledges this in the limitations section on page 9, but the dependence is fairly central. It would help to better understand how sensitive the method is to parser quality, annotation scheme, and binarization choices. Since the stack tape is derived from incremental parses, errors in silver parses could propagate into both training targets and the induced attention bias. Some ablation on noisier parses, alternative parsers, or partial supervision would strengthen the practical case.

Second, the computational story is mixed. The paper argues on page 5 that FLOPs are similar to standard self-attention, but memory increases because keys become effectively 3D tensors indexed by destination and source positions. This is an important caveat. In practice, memory bandwidth and implementation efficiency matter a great deal, and the paper itself notes that standard attention is faster on GPUs. I would have liked more concrete runtime and memory benchmarks, especially for the WikiTrees and GPT-2 finetuning settings. Since the method is advertised as a drop-in replacement, practical overhead should be quantified more explicitly.

Third, inference requires approximate marginalization over parses with beam search. This is reasonable, and the paper correctly notes that it avoids some of the complexity of word-synchronous decoding used in transition-based models. Still, beam size 300 for BLIMP and SG evaluation is nontrivial. It would be useful to know how sensitive results are to beam size, and whether the gains persist under smaller beams or greedy parsing. More generally, because the model is trained with supervised attachments but evaluated through approximate marginalization, there is a train-test interface here that deserves a bit more scrutiny.

Fourth, while the paper claims that Pushdown Layers are a drop-in replacement, most experiments do not replace all layers in the same way. For example, the WikiTrees experiment uses Pushdown Layers only in the final 6 blocks, and GPT2-medium finetuning replaces the final 12 blocks. This is perfectly sensible, but it suggests that the best integration strategy may be nuanced. An ablation over which layers are replaced, or how many, would be informative. It might also reveal whether the structural bias is most useful in higher layers, which would be an interesting scientific finding in its own right.

Fifth, the comparison to prior structured models is mostly convincing, but not perfectly apples-to-apples in all places. Table 2 notes tokenization differences for perplexity, and TG lacks a BLIMP number in the table. None of this undermines the main message, but it does mean the empirical superiority claim should be interpreted with some care. If possible, a more standardized reimplementation or evaluation protocol would make the comparison even stronger.

I also have a few smaller technical questions. The attachment decision uses argmax during stack-tape updates at inference time, while training uses supervised attachment probabilities with precomputed tapes. It would be helpful to clarify whether exposure bias from structural decisions is a concern, and whether any experiments were done with sampled or beam-maintained stack states during training. Relatedly, the discussion on page 5 about “attending to hidden states with old memory” raises an interesting mismatch: earlier token representations are computed using depth values that may later become stale as future reductions occur. The paper acknowledges this but does not deeply analyze its effect. Since this is a distinctive property of the architecture, some discussion of whether it harms consistency—or perhaps acts as a useful approximation—would be valuable.

Despite these questions, I think the paper makes a substantial contribution. The core idea is novel and well executed, the empirical evidence is broad and aligned with the motivation, and the work opens an appealing middle ground between purely implicit Transformers and heavily structured symbolic/neural hybrids. In particular, I find the sample-efficiency result on WikiTrees and the strong syntactic generalization gains on BLLIP/BLIMP/SG to be quite compelling. The paper also feels likely to stimulate follow-up work on structured memory in Transformers, supervised or unsupervised stack induction, and syntax-aware scaling.

In terms of significance, I would rate this as more than an incremental architectural tweak. The proposal changes the internal state dynamics of autoregressive attention in a principled way, and it does so with a clear linguistic motivation and measurable benefits. The method is specialized—it is not a universal replacement for all Transformer use cases—but for language modeling and syntax-sensitive generalization it seems genuinely promising.

My recommendation is positive. I believe this paper is worthy of acceptance. To further strengthen the final version, I would encourage the authors to add more detail on runtime/memory overhead, beam-size sensitivity, and ablations over parser quality and layer placement. But these are refinements rather than fundamental concerns. The paper is thoughtful, technically solid, and presents a compelling case that explicit stack-like recursive state can materially improve Transformer language models.
