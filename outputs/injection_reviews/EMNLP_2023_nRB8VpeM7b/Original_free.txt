This paper proposes Pushdown Layers, a drop-in replacement for standard self-attention that augments Transformer language models with an explicit stack-like memory represented as a “stack tape” of token depths in an incremental constituency parse. The model predicts words and attachment decisions jointly, updates the stack tape synchronously during left-to-right generation, and uses depth-conditioned embeddings to softly modulate attention scores. Empirically, the paper reports gains on synthetic Dyck-language generalization, sentence-level syntactic generalization on BLLIP/BLIMP/SG benchmarks, improved sample efficiency on a newly constructed parsed Wikipedia corpus (WIKITREES), and modest downstream gains when GPT-2-medium is retrofitted with Pushdown Layers and fine-tuned for GLUE-style classification.

Overall, I found the paper interesting, technically thoughtful, and well motivated. The central idea is appealing: rather than forcing a Transformer to implicitly encode recursive state in hidden vectors, expose a lightweight structured memory that tracks incremental parse depth and use it to bias attention. This sits in a nice middle ground between unconstrained Transformers and more rigid syntax-aware models that linearize parse actions into the token stream or hard-mask attention. The paper is also clearly written at a high level, and the figures are helpful. In particular, the illustrations on pages 1 and 4 make the mechanism intuitive: the stack tape stores token depths, the attachment head selects a constituent by attending to its right boundary, and the resulting depth values are converted into embeddings that perturb attention keys.

The main strengths are threefold. First, the modeling contribution is novel enough to be meaningful. The proposed mechanism is not just another syntax-supervised auxiliary loss; it changes the internal computation of attention in a structured way while preserving the standard token-level output space. This is a real advantage over prior syntactic language models such as PLM and Transformer Grammars, which require extra structural tokens or transition actions and more specialized decoding. Second, the empirical story is coherent across scales: the Dyck experiments support the recursion claim in a controlled setting, the BLLIP/BLIMP/SG experiments show gains on established syntactic benchmarks, and the WIKITREES experiments suggest the inductive bias remains useful beyond small corpora. Third, the paper includes useful analysis. The parsing evaluation in Table 4 and the attention analyses in Figures 5 and 6 help connect the mechanism to behavior rather than only reporting end-task metrics.

That said, I also have several concerns, mostly around evaluation rigor, fairness of comparisons, and some unresolved modeling questions.

The biggest issue is that the paper’s strongest claims are about recursive structure and syntactic generalization, but the evidence is still somewhat indirect. The model is trained with silver or gold-ish constituency supervision, so gains on syntax-sensitive evaluations are not surprising in themselves. The key question is whether the specific pushdown-style inductive bias matters beyond simply injecting parse supervision. The paper partially addresses this by comparing against a base LM with an attachment loss in the Dyck setting, but this control does not appear consistently across the natural language experiments. A stronger ablation suite is needed. For example, what happens if one keeps the same attachment supervision but does not use the stack tape to modulate attention? What if one uses depth embeddings derived from gold parses but without online stack updates? What if one predicts attachments but feeds them only as an auxiliary objective? Without such controls, it is difficult to isolate whether the gains come from the pushdown computation itself, from multitask syntactic supervision, or simply from exposing parse-derived features.

Relatedly, the comparisons to prior structured models are somewhat hard to interpret. Table 2 compares BLIMP and SG scores against PLM, PLM-mask, and TG, but perplexities are explicitly noted as not directly comparable because of tokenization differences. More importantly, the training setups may differ in ways that matter: data preprocessing, tokenization, model size, and decoding approximations all affect these metrics. The paper says it uses the same hyperparameters as Sartran et al. (2022), which helps, but the comparison would be much stronger if the authors reimplemented or reran the baselines under a unified setup, especially because the claimed advantages include computational simplicity and not increasing sequence length. As written, the empirical edge over TG/PLM is promising but not fully definitive.

I also think the inference story deserves more scrutiny. The model defines a joint distribution over strings and parses, but marginal likelihood over strings is approximated with beam search over parses. This is reasonable, but it means reported sentence probabilities on BLIMP and SG depend on approximate inference quality. The paper fixes beam size at 300, yet there is no sensitivity analysis. It would be important to know whether the syntactic evaluation results are stable across beam sizes, whether the base LM baselines are advantaged or disadvantaged by exact versus approximate scoring, and how much search error affects conclusions. Since one of the selling points is simpler inference than transition-based syntactic LMs, some quantitative evidence on decoding cost and robustness would strengthen the paper.

A second concern is the computational tradeoff. The paper argues that FLOPs are similar to standard self-attention but memory increases because keys become effectively 3D tensors indexed by destination and source positions. This is not a minor detail. On page 5, the authors acknowledge the memory complexity increase from standard attention to a larger term involving nd * ns * d. In practice, this could be a serious limitation, especially for long contexts where syntax-aware modeling might be most useful. Yet the paper does not provide wall-clock training speed, peak memory, throughput, or scaling curves. Since the method is presented as a drop-in replacement, practical overhead matters a lot. The WIKITREES experiments use context windows of 512 and only replace the last 6 layers in some settings, which suggests the full method may be expensive. I would strongly encourage the authors to report actual runtime/memory overhead and perhaps compare variants that replace different subsets of layers.

There are also some conceptual questions about the stack representation. The stack tape stores token depths, not explicit constituent labels or spans. This is elegant, but it is also lossy. Different parse histories may induce similar depth patterns, and depth alone may not fully characterize the relevant recursive state. The paper partly compensates by using attachment decisions to rightmost tokens of constituents, but I would like more discussion of expressivity: what classes of structural distinctions can or cannot be represented by this tape? The example on page 5 about “stale” memory is especially interesting. Earlier token representations are computed using depth values that may later become outdated as future reductions occur. The paper notes this but does not really analyze whether it harms modeling or whether deeper layers compensate. This seems central to the architecture and deserves either theoretical justification or empirical ablation.

Another weakness is that the downstream task evidence is modest. Table 3 shows small gains on RTE, MRPC, and STS-B, and a slight drop on SST-5. These are not large enough to strongly support a broad claim that Pushdown Layers improve language understanding. Moreover, the setup uses staged fine-tuning on automatically parsed WIKITREES and then task-specific prompting with precomputed stack tapes. This is somewhat specialized and may not reflect realistic deployment. I would frame these results more cautiously as preliminary evidence of transfer rather than a major downstream validation.

The paper’s strongest empirical result may actually be the sample-efficiency curve on page 7. Figure 4 is compelling: with 10M–100M tokens, the Pushdown variant consistently outperforms the base GPT-2-small architecture on SG syntactic generalization, and the gap is large in low-data regimes. This supports the inductive-bias argument well. Still, I would like to see variance bars or confidence intervals on that figure, since sample-efficiency claims can be noisy. Similarly, Figure 3 compares SG subtests and suggests Pushdown-LM beats TG on 4/6 categories, especially center embedding and long-distance dependencies, but again significance is only partially discussed. The paper notes in Table 2 that some differences are not significant, but more systematic significance reporting would help.

I appreciated the parsing analysis in Table 4, but it also raises a subtle concern. The model is trained on data annotated by an automatic parser and then evaluated against binarized parses, including on auto-parsed BLLIP-LG. Achieving high unlabeled F1 is interesting, but because the training signal is derived from a parser, this result partly measures imitation of the annotation source. The PTB evaluation is more meaningful, though even there the comparison is slightly awkward because the paper filters out sentences with unknown words and compares against a benepar model whose headline score is on the full test set. This analysis is useful, but I would avoid overinterpreting it as evidence of state-of-the-art parsing ability.

On the positive side, the paper does a good job positioning itself relative to prior work. The discussion in Sections 2 and 6 clearly distinguishes the method from transition-linearization approaches like PLM/TG and from hard syntax-constrained attention. The claim that Pushdown Layers preserve the standard output space and allow syntax to influence representations through learned soft biases is a genuine conceptual advantage. I also think the implementation appendix is a plus; the pseudocode on pages 14–15 makes the method more reproducible than many architecture papers.

There are a few places where the exposition could be sharpened. Equation 5 and the attachment mechanism are understandable, but the relation between selecting a rightmost token and selecting a constituent could be formalized more carefully. The paper says that “none of the other tokens of a constituent can be attended to” for attachment decisions, as illustrated on page 4, but the exact masking or enforcement mechanism is not fully spelled out in the main text. Likewise, Equation 6 adds depth embeddings to keys, but it would help to clarify whether these embeddings are shared across heads or layers, how large the depth vocabulary is, and how depths beyond training range are handled. The Dyck generalization results suggest some extrapolation, so this detail matters.

I also think the paper should discuss the dependence on external parsers more directly. The limitations section mentions that constituency-annotated data may be unavailable and that experiments are limited to English. That is fair, but the broader implication is that the method currently inherits biases and errors from silver parses. Since WIKITREES is automatically parsed, one wonders how robust the method is to annotation noise. An experiment varying parser quality or comparing gold versus silver supervision on a smaller corpus would be informative.

If accepted, I would encourage the authors to add or emphasize the following in a revision. First, include stronger ablations isolating the contribution of stack-tape-conditioned attention from auxiliary syntactic supervision. Second, report practical efficiency metrics: training speed, memory, and scaling with context length and number of Pushdown layers. Third, provide beam-size sensitivity and perhaps oracle or approximate-search diagnostics for BLIMP/SG scoring. Fourth, clarify the attachment masking and constituent-selection mechanism in the main text. Fifth, temper the downstream claims and present GLUE results as preliminary.

In summary, I think this is a strong and creative paper with a meaningful architectural idea and encouraging empirical results. Its main contribution is not just “syntax helps,” but a specific way of injecting recursive state into Transformer attention without changing the token output space. The evidence on Dyck, SG, BLIMP, and sample efficiency is compelling enough to suggest the idea is real. My reservations are mostly about causal attribution, fairness and completeness of comparisons, and practical overhead. If those issues were better addressed, this would be an even stronger submission.

My overall recommendation is positive. I see this as a worthwhile contribution to the literature on structured inductive biases for Transformers, with clear novelty and promising results, though not yet a fully definitive empirical case.
