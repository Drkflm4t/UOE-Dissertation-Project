Summary: This paper proposes SyncLLM, a method for converting an autoregressive text LLM (Llama3-8B) into a full-duplex spoken dialogue agent that can speak and listen synchronously rather than in strict turns. The core idea is to inject coarse timing via periodic synchronization/speaker tokens and train the model on interleaved dual-speaker HuBERT token streams with deduplication to preserve semantic density. To address real-time latency, the model predicts short future chunks for both speakers and uses an estimated user chunk to generate its own next chunk before the real user chunk arrives. Training uses a three-stage recipe: large-scale synthetic spoken dialogue from text data, synthetic full-duplex data without overlaps, and a final finetuning stage on about 2k hours of real spoken dialogue. Experiments compare against dGSLM and show substantially better semantic quality (via ASR-transcribed dialogue perplexity and human meaningfulness MOS) while maintaining similar turn-taking naturalness, with some evidence of robustness to out-of-distribution data and simulated Internet-scale latency up to about 200–240 ms.

Strengths: Clear and timely problem formulation: moving beyond half-duplex/turn-based voice interfaces toward genuinely synchronous spoken interaction is important and well motivated.; Technically interesting design that adapts a standard autoregressive LLM rather than building a bespoke speech-only architecture, enabling reuse of strong text pretraining.; The synchronization-token plus deduplication scheme is simple and elegant; the token format is clearly illustrated in Figures 1 and 2.; Practical latency-aware inference formulation: predicting an estimated user chunk to compensate for delayed input is a sensible systems contribution.; Strong empirical gains in semantic quality over dGSLM, especially in human Meaningfulness MOS (Table 3) and ASR-based perplexity (Figures 5–7).; Uses relatively little real spoken dialogue data (about 1.9k–2k hours) by leveraging large synthetic data, which is practically valuable.; Includes both continuation and interaction settings, plus some out-of-distribution evaluation on CANDOR.; Human evaluation protocol is reasonably detailed, with 32 annotators, 180 items, and separate naturalness/meaningfulness criteria.

Weaknesses: The main baseline set is narrow. The paper primarily compares against dGSLM, but not against stronger modern speech-text or streaming spoken dialogue systems, cascaded ASR-LLM-TTS systems, or recent multimodal LLM baselines adapted for dialogue.; The semantic evaluation relies heavily on ASR transcription followed by text perplexity, which may conflate speech generation quality, ASR errors, and dialogue quality; this proxy is informative but indirect.; The interaction setting is simulated via LLM-LLM interaction rather than real human-agent interaction, so claims about full-duplex usability with users remain only partially validated.; Naturalness improvements over dGSLM are modest; in human N-MOS, SyncLLM is roughly at parity or slightly below re-synthesized references and not clearly better than dGSLM.; The synthetic data pipeline may introduce artifacts from Bark TTS and from the assumption in stage 2 that one speaker is silent during the other’s turn; the paper does not deeply analyze how these artifacts affect learned turn-taking behavior.; Interpolation from deduplicated tokens back to periodic HuBERT sequences is heuristic and acknowledged to be imperfect, but there is limited ablation on how much this affects speech quality or timing fidelity.; Evaluation of latency robustness suggests degradation at 240 ms and above (Appendix Figure 8/Table 6), so the robustness claim should be stated more cautiously.; Some methodological details remain underspecified for reproducibility, such as exact dataset composition for the 193k/20k synthetic hours, prompt sampling details, and inference/runtime characteristics.

Soundness Issues: The core method is logically coherent, but several headline claims depend on indirect proxies rather than direct task success with humans in live interaction.; ASR-based perplexity is not a standard end-to-end dialogue metric and may bias results toward models whose outputs are easier to transcribe rather than more conversationally appropriate.; Human evaluation uses only three raters per item and reports means/SEs over item medians; acceptable, but limited for subtle naturalness distinctions.; The comparison may not be fully apples-to-apples if dGSLM is disadvantaged by architecture class and by using Fisher-specialized speech representations, as the authors themselves note.; The paper does not provide enough ablations isolating the contribution of each major component: synchronization tokens, deduplication, future user-chunk estimation, and each training stage.; Claims about handling Internet-scale latency are supported only in simulation, not in deployed networked experiments with measured end-to-end latency budgets.; No statistical significance testing is reported for many comparisons beyond confidence intervals/standard errors, making it harder to judge whether smaller differences are meaningful.
