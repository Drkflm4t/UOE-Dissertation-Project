This paper tackles an important and timely problem: moving spoken dialogue systems beyond half-duplex, turn-based interaction toward genuinely synchronous, full-duplex conversation with overlaps, backchannels, and latency tolerance. The central idea is to adapt a pretrained autoregressive LLM, specifically Llama3-8B, so that it can operate against a real-world clock by generating interleaved chunks of speech-unit tokens for both interlocutors. The paper proposes periodic synchronization tokens, deduplicated HuBERT token modeling, a latency-tolerant inference scheme based on predicting the user’s current chunk before generating the model’s next chunk, and a three-stage training recipe that relies heavily on synthetic spoken dialogue derived from text data. Empirically, the paper reports strong gains over dGSLM in semantic coherence and competitive or better turn-taking naturalness, including some out-of-distribution robustness and simulated interaction under 160–240 ms latency.

Overall, I found the paper interesting, ambitious, and potentially impactful. The problem formulation is compelling, and the paper makes a serious attempt to bridge a real gap between current voice interfaces and human conversational dynamics. The strongest aspect of the work is that it does not merely claim “streaming” or “speech-to-speech” capability, but explicitly addresses synchrony, overlap, and delayed access to user input as first-class modeling constraints. The framing around the lack of temporal grounding in standard LLMs is insightful, and the proposed chunked interleaving mechanism is simple enough to be implementable while still being conceptually novel. The empirical results, especially the large gap in meaningfulness relative to dGSLM in Table 3 on page 8, suggest that leveraging text-pretrained LLMs plus synthetic dialogue data is indeed a powerful recipe.

The technical core is reasonably clear. The model represents dialogue as two interleaved streams of discrete speech units, with periodic speaker tags acting as synchronization markers. The illustration in Figure 1 on pages 2–3 is helpful: at chunk N, the model has access to its own speech through chunk N and the user’s speech only through chunk N-1, so it first predicts an estimate of the user’s current chunk and then conditions on that estimate to generate its own next chunk. This is a neat way to operationalize latency tolerance. The tokenization design in Figure 2 on page 5 is also one of the paper’s more original contributions. Training on deduplicated HuBERT sequences while preserving coarse timing via periodic synchronization tokens is a sensible compromise between semantic density and temporal structure. Figure 3 on page 5 supports the motivation by showing that deduplication substantially reduces token redundancy.

The training recipe is another strength. The three-stage progression from text/speech alignment in turn-based dialogue, to synthetic full-duplex dialogue without overlaps, to real-world spoken dialogue with actual turn-taking cues is well motivated. Given the scarcity of dual-channel spoken dialogue data, the use of 212k hours of synthetic speech and only about 2k hours of real spoken dialogue is practically important. The sentence-level text/speech interleaving strategy in stage 1 also seems thoughtful, and the ablation in Table 5 on page 12 gives some evidence that this choice matters.

The empirical story is broadly convincing, but there are also several limitations in the evaluation that weaken the strength of the claims. The semantic evaluation relies on ASR transcription followed by perplexity under a text dialogue model, while explicitly ignoring overlapping speech. This is understandable as a proxy, but it is also somewhat misaligned with the paper’s central claim of full-duplex dialogue modeling. If the main novelty is handling overlap, interruption, and backchanneling, then an evaluation pipeline that collapses generated speech into turn-based text and discards overlap may miss exactly the phenomena the model is supposed to improve. The reported gains in Figure 5 on page 6 and Figure 7 on page 8 are encouraging, but they mostly establish that the model produces more textually coherent dialogue than dGSLM after ASR, not necessarily that it is better at full-duplex interaction per se.

Similarly, the naturalness evaluation based on Pearson correlation of average durations of IPUs, pauses, and floor-transfer offsets is useful but limited. Correlation with ground-truth continuation statistics, as shown in Table 2 on page 7, captures some timing realism, yet it is a fairly coarse aggregate measure. It does not directly assess whether overlaps occur in pragmatically appropriate places, whether backchannels are semantically contingent, or whether interruptions are resolved coherently. In fact, because the metric averages event durations over a generated sample, it may be insensitive to local conversational failures. The human evaluation is therefore crucial, and the MOS results are probably the most important evidence in the paper. There, the meaningfulness gains are substantial: SyncLLM-F reaches 3.74 versus 1.55 for dGSLM overall, while naturalness remains roughly comparable around 3.9–4.0. That is a strong result. Still, the human study is relatively small: 180 items, 32 annotators, 3 ratings per item. This is not unreasonable, but for a paper making broad claims about natural full-duplex dialogue, I would have liked more detail on prompt selection, randomization, statistical significance testing between systems, and inter-rater agreement.

A more serious concern is the fairness and informativeness of the comparison to dGSLM. The paper repeatedly emphasizes that dGSLM is speech-only while SyncLLM leverages a large text-pretrained LLM and massive synthetic dialogue data. That is indeed part of the contribution, but it also means the comparison is not isolating the effect of synchrony modeling alone. The large meaningfulness gains may primarily reflect the use of Llama3-8B and text-derived supervision rather than the proposed synchronous chunking mechanism. In other words, the paper convincingly shows that “LLM + speech adaptation + synthetic dialogue” beats an older speech-only dialogue model, but it is less clear how much of the gain comes from the specific synchronization design. A stronger experimental section would include ablations against simpler LLM-based spoken dialogue baselines, for example a turn-based speech LLM with similar pretraining and tokenization but without synchronous interleaving, or a streaming variant without user-chunk estimation. Without such baselines, the novelty of the synchronization mechanism is somewhat entangled with the benefits of scale and pretraining.

Relatedly, the paper lacks several key ablations that would help validate the design choices. The chunk size is varied in some evaluations, but there is no systematic analysis of the synchronization token itself. How much does periodic [S0]/[S1] insertion matter relative to simply using fixed-rate chunk boundaries in positional encoding or explicit timestamp embeddings? The deduplication strategy is motivated and intuitively appealing, but there is no direct ablation comparing deduplicated versus non-deduplicated training under otherwise matched conditions. Since deduplication is central to the method, this omission is notable. Likewise, the interpolation heuristic used to reconstruct fixed-rate token streams for speech synthesis seems potentially lossy, yet the paper only states that the effect is “imperceptible” up to 240 ms chunk size. This should ideally be backed by objective or subjective evidence. The latency-tolerant inference mechanism is also important, but there is no ablation comparing interaction with and without predicted user chunks, or with oracle user chunks, to quantify how much anticipation helps.

Another weakness is that the paper’s “full-duplex interaction” evaluation is still simulated LLM-LLM interaction rather than real human-agent interaction. I understand why this is done, but the claims should be interpreted carefully. Simulating one SyncLLM talking to another is not the same as demonstrating robust interaction with real users, whose speech timing, disfluencies, repairs, and pragmatic behavior may differ substantially from model-generated speech. The paper does acknowledge this indirectly, but the framing in the abstract and conclusion is somewhat stronger than the evidence fully supports. The interaction results in Figure 7 on page 8 and Table 4 on page 9 are promising, especially the relatively small degradation from continuation mode to interaction mode, but they remain a simulation study.

I also have some questions about the exact modeling setup and reproducibility. The paper says the model predicts interleaved chunks for both speakers, but some implementation details remain underspecified. For example, how are chunk boundaries aligned during training when real dialogue contains asynchrony and variable onset times? Are chunks globally aligned from the start of the recording, and if so, how sensitive is the method to initial offset? How are long silences handled beyond deduplication? What is the exact prompting format at inference time for continuation and interaction? How are speaker identities or voices controlled in synthesis? The paper mentions Bark TTS for synthetic data and a simple HiFi-GAN vocoder for output, but the speech generation stack is not described in enough detail to fully reproduce the system. Since speech quality and timing can be heavily affected by the vocoder and token-to-waveform pipeline, more transparency here would help.

There are also some concerns about the evaluation datasets and domain mismatch. Fisher is used as the main real-world training source, and Candor as OOD evaluation. This is a reasonable setup, and the OOD results are encouraging. However, Fisher telephone conversations and Candor naturalistic conversations differ in multiple ways, including recording conditions, style, and topic distribution. It would be useful to know whether the gains are robust across more than one OOD benchmark, or whether they depend on the specific characteristics of Candor. Also, because the model uses general-purpose HuBERT units rather than Fisher-specialized representations, the OOD advantage over dGSLM may partly reflect tokenizer/domain robustness rather than dialogue modeling quality. That is still valuable, but it should be disentangled more carefully.

The paper is generally well written and easy to follow, though there are some places where the exposition could be tightened. The figures are helpful, especially Figures 1 and 2. The tables are informative, though Table 2 and Table 7 would benefit from clearer explanation of why correlation with ground-truth continuation versus prompt are both meaningful and how they should be interpreted differently. There are also a few minor grammatical issues and typos throughout, but nothing severe.

In terms of significance, I think the paper has real merit. The idea of synchronizing an autoregressive LLM to wall-clock time via periodic chunked generation is novel enough to be interesting, and the practical recipe of combining text-pretrained LLMs with synthetic spoken dialogue is likely to influence future work. The results suggest that the field may not need to choose between semantic competence and conversational timing: with the right representation and training pipeline, one can get much better content quality while preserving natural turn-taking. That is an important message.

At the same time, I do not think the current version fully nails the causal story behind its improvements. The paper demonstrates a strong system, but not yet a fully convincing scientific decomposition of which ingredients matter most. The strongest claims about full-duplex interaction would be more persuasive with stronger baselines, more targeted ablations, and at least some real interactive human evaluation.

My main suggestions for improvement are as follows. First, add ablations isolating the contribution of synchronization tokens, deduplication, and user-chunk estimation. Second, compare against a stronger LLM-based spoken dialogue baseline that shares the same pretrained backbone and synthetic-data recipe but remains turn-based or non-synchronous. Third, include more direct evaluations of overlap and backchannel quality, ideally with event-level precision/recall or human judgments specifically targeting interruption appropriateness, listener feedback, and responsiveness. Fourth, provide more implementation detail for reproducibility, especially around chunk alignment, inference, and speech synthesis. Fifth, if possible, include a small real-user interactive study or at least a Wizard-of-Oz style evaluation to validate that the simulated interaction findings transfer to human use.

In summary, this is a strong and creative paper on an important problem. Its main strengths are the compelling problem formulation, the elegant synchronization mechanism, the practical training recipe using synthetic dialogue, and the substantial gains in dialogue meaningfulness while maintaining naturalness. Its main weaknesses are limited ablation evidence, somewhat indirect evaluation of full-duplex phenomena, and reliance on a comparison that conflates the proposed method with the advantages of large text-pretrained LLMs. I view it as a promising contribution with clear novelty and practical relevance, but one that would benefit from a more rigorous experimental dissection of its core claims.
