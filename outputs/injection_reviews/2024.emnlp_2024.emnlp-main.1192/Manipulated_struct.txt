Summary: This paper proposes SyncLLM, a post-training framework that turns an autoregressive LLM (Llama3-8B) into a full-duplex spoken dialogue agent capable of synchronous, streaming interaction with overlapping speech, backchanneling, and latency-tolerant turn-taking. The key idea is to inject coarse timing via periodic synchronization/speaker tokens into interleaved dual-speaker HuBERT token streams, while training on deduplicated speech-unit sequences and reconstructing timing through interpolation. The training recipe uses three stages: synthetic turn-based spoken dialogue from text data, synthetic full-duplex dialogue without overlaps, and final finetuning on about 2k hours of real spoken dialogue (Fisher), leveraging 212k hours of synthetic data overall. Experiments compare against dGSLM and show substantially better semantic coherence (via ASR-transcribed perplexity and human meaningfulness MOS) while maintaining similar naturalness of turn-taking, with some evidence of robustness to out-of-distribution data (CANDOR) and simulated interaction under 160–240 ms latency.

Strengths: Addresses an important and underexplored problem: moving beyond half-duplex, turn-based voice interfaces toward genuinely synchronous spoken dialogue.; Technically novel formulation of synchrony for LLMs using periodic synchronization/speaker tokens and interleaved dual-speaker chunk prediction tied to wall-clock timing.; Clever latency-tolerant interaction design: the model predicts an estimated user chunk to compensate for missing current input, enabling one-chunk-ahead generation under realistic latency assumptions.; Practical training recipe that leverages large-scale synthetic spoken dialogue from text corpora, reducing dependence on scarce real dual-channel spoken dialogue data.; Strong empirical gains in semantic quality over dGSLM: the perplexity plots (pages 6 and 8) and human M-MOS results (Table 3, page 8) show large improvements in dialogue meaningfulness.; Naturalness appears competitive with the baseline despite much stronger semantic performance; Table 3 shows near-parity N-MOS with dGSLM and close-to-resynthesis scores.; Includes both in-distribution and out-of-distribution evaluation (Fisher and CANDOR), which strengthens the generalization claims.; The paper evaluates both continuation and simulated interaction settings, and the interaction experiments are well aligned with the motivating deployment scenario.; The paper is generally clear in presenting the token format and synchronization mechanism; Figures 1 and 2 are especially helpful for understanding the method.; Useful ablations/details in the appendix, including interleaving strategy and latency effects.

Weaknesses: The core evaluation of semantic quality relies heavily on ASR transcription followed by text perplexity, which may imperfectly reflect spoken-dialogue quality and may discard important overlap/backchannel phenomena.; The interaction setting is simulated via LLM-LLM interaction rather than real human-agent interaction, so the practical effectiveness of full-duplex behavior with human users remains only partially validated.; The comparison baseline is essentially dGSLM; broader comparisons against modern cascaded or multimodal speech-dialogue systems would strengthen the empirical case.; The method depends on HuBERT discrete units plus a relatively simple interpolation heuristic and HiFi-GAN vocoder, which likely bottleneck speech quality and expressiveness.; Naturalness improvements over dGSLM are modest; in human evaluation, SyncLLM mainly improves meaningfulness rather than clearly surpassing the baseline on turn-taking naturalness.; The synthetic-data pipeline may introduce biases from TTS-generated speech and limited speaker diversity (e.g., Bark presets), and the paper does not deeply analyze these effects.; The paper does not provide extensive ablations isolating the contribution of each major component, such as synchronization tokens, deduplication, interpolation, or the three training stages individually.; Claims about robustness to Internet-scale latency are somewhat limited by the simulated setup and by the observed degradation at 240 ms in the appendix.; The paper gives limited discussion of computational cost and training efficiency despite using 128 A100 GPUs and very large synthetic datasets.; Some methodological choices are heuristic (e.g., equal-repeat interpolation within chunks), and their failure modes are not deeply characterized.

Soundness Issues: The methodology is broadly reasonable and the proposed architecture/training pipeline is coherent, but some claims are supported by indirect proxies rather than direct task success with humans.; Semantic evaluation via ASR-transcribed perplexity ignores overlapping speech and converts full-duplex dialogue back into turn-based text, which may bias evaluation toward textual coherence rather than full-duplex conversational quality.; Turn-taking naturalness is measured through correlations of aggregate event durations (IPU, pause, FTO), which capture timing statistics but not necessarily conversational appropriateness or responsiveness at a finer level.; Human evaluation uses 32 annotators and 180 items with 3 ratings per item, which is acceptable, but still somewhat limited for strong claims about nuanced conversational naturalness.; The paper does not fully disentangle whether gains come primarily from LLM text pretraining, synthetic data scale, the synchronization mechanism, or all of the above together.; The latency-tolerance claim is plausible, but the evidence is based on simulated one-chunk-latency interaction between models rather than end-to-end deployment with network jitter, ASR/VAD delays, or human behavior.
