Summary:
This paper studies hybrid reinforcement learning in linear MDPs, where an agent can use both an offline dataset and online interaction. It proposes two computationally efficient algorithms tailored to two distinct objectives: RAPPEL for PAC-style policy learning via reward-agnostic online exploration followed by offline pessimistic planning, and HYRULE for regret minimization via warm-starting an online optimistic algorithm with offline data. The main claim is that both algorithms improve over the best known guarantees for hybrid RL in linear MDPs without requiring single-policy concentrability, and can outperform the corresponding offline-only or online-only minimax rates depending on the data regime. The paper provides theorem statements, proof sketches in the main text, full proofs in the appendix, and small-scale experiments on a Tetris-like environment.

Overall assessment:
This is a strong theoretical paper on hybrid RL. The problem is timely and important, the setting is well motivated, and the results appear novel and meaningful. In particular, obtaining provable improvements over offline-only and online-only baselines in linear MDPs, without single-policy concentrability, is a significant contribution. I also appreciate that the paper treats both major hybrid paradigms—online-to-offline and offline-to-online—rather than focusing on only one. The presentation is generally clear for a theory paper, and the comparison tables on pages 2 and 4 are helpful.

That said, I do have some concerns about the sharpness and interpretation of some assumptions, the practical significance of the burn-in/sample-size requirements, and the experimental validation. My overall leaning is positive, but I think the paper would benefit from clarification and tightening in several places.

Strengths:

1. Clear and important problem.
The paper addresses a central open question in hybrid RL: whether one can beat the barriers of purely offline and purely online RL without relying on single-policy concentrability. Extending such guarantees beyond the tabular setting to linear MDPs is a natural and meaningful step.

2. Two complementary algorithmic contributions.
The split between RAPPEL and HYRULE is conceptually clean and useful:
- RAPPEL targets PAC learning via reward-agnostic exploration plus pessimistic offline RL.
- HYRULE targets regret minimization via offline warm start plus online optimism.
This framing, introduced in Section 1.1 and developed in Section 3, is one of the paper’s strongest aspects.

3. Strong theoretical guarantees.
The main results in Theorem 1 and Theorem 2 are interesting. In particular:
- RAPPEL improves over prior hybrid PAC guarantees for linear MDPs, especially compared with Wagenmaker and Pacchiano (2023).
- HYRULE gives a regret bound that is no worse than the online-only minimax-optimal rate and can improve when offline data are informative.
The comparison table on page 4 is effective in highlighting these gains.

4. No single-policy concentrability assumption.
This is a major plus. The paper instead works with partition-based quantities such as partial concentrability/coverability, which seems to be the right direction for hybrid RL.

5. Technical novelty.
The paper identifies two technical ingredients:
- projection-based sharpening of dimension dependence to don and coff(Xoff),
- truncation plus total-variance arguments to maintain H^3-type dependence.
These seem nontrivial and potentially useful beyond this paper.

6. Computational efficiency discussion.
The authors make an effort to discuss computational tractability, including finite-action complexity for both algorithms. This is appreciated.

Weaknesses / concerns:

1. The assumptions around the partition and projected coverage need more discussion.
A central theme is that the guarantees depend on a partition Xoff, Xon and associated quantities coff(Xoff), con(Xon), doff, don. While this is elegant analytically, it raises several questions:
- How should a practitioner choose the partition?
- Is the partition purely existential in the theorem, or algorithmically constructible?
- For RAPPEL, the user must choose a tolerance related to don; Section 3.1 acknowledges this, but the practical guidance remains limited.
- For HYRULE, the algorithm is said to be “unaware of the partition,” which is nice, but the theorem still optimizes over partitions in the bound. More intuition is needed for how meaningful this is operationally.

2. Assumption 2 may be mild in theory but still feels strong in practice.
The “full rank projected covariates” assumption for RAPPEL requires finite partial coverability on the online partition. The paper argues this is achievable in linear MDPs via feature transformation, and gives a Tetris implementation detail on page 7. Still, this deserves a more careful discussion:
- Is the transformation assumed known?
- Does it preserve the linear MDP structure in the exact way needed?
- Is the assumption essentially equivalent to requiring explorable features after projection?
The current explanation is plausible but somewhat quick.

3. Burn-in/sample-size requirements are very large.
Theorems 1 and 2 require substantial lower bounds on N, Non, Noff, and HYRULE in particular assumes Non, Noff = \tilde{\Omega}(d^{13} H^{14}). Even if these are proof artifacts, they materially affect the practical meaning of the results. The paper does acknowledge nontrivial burn-in costs in Section 5, but I think this issue should be emphasized more strongly earlier. As written, the asymptotic improvements are compelling, but the finite-sample story is less convincing.

4. Experimental validation is limited.
The experiments in Section 4 are small and mainly illustrative. They do support the qualitative message, but they are not yet strong enough to validate the practical relevance of the theory.
Specific issues:
- Only one environment family is used.
- The implementations are simplified substitutes rather than exact implementations of the proposed algorithms (footnote on page 12).
- There is little ablation on partition choice, tolerance choice, or sensitivity to offline data quality beyond the adversarial/uniform contrast.
- No empirical scaling with d, H, Noff, Non is shown, despite these being central to the theory.

5. Some theorem statements are hard to parse and compare.
The bounds are interesting but not always easy to interpret. For example, Theorem 1 gives two forms in (8), and the role of alpha_off and alpha_on in the denominator may not be immediately transparent. Similarly, the notation around weighted covariance matrices and truncated variances is dense. This is acceptable for a theory paper, but some simplification or an additional corollary in the main text would help.

6. Novelty relative to prior ingredients is good but somewhat incremental at the algorithmic level.
The paper is honest that it builds on existing components: OPTCOV, LinPEVI-ADV+, and LSVI-UCB++. The novelty is in combining them with the right decomposition and sharper analysis. That is a valid and valuable contribution, but the paper should be judged primarily on theory rather than algorithmic originality.

Detailed comments:

1. On RAPPEL:
The online-to-offline approach is well motivated, especially for safety-critical settings where one wants a fixed final policy rather than a randomized online learner. I found this discussion in Section 1.1 compelling. Theorem 1 is probably the paper’s cleanest conceptual result: hybrid exploration can improve offline RL guarantees without requiring good behavior-policy coverage. The comparison to the offline-only bound from Xiong et al. (2023) is persuasive.

That said, the dependence on the OPTCOV tolerance and the minimal exploration cost N*(tau) is a practical caveat. The paper notes this, but I would like a clearer statement of when the hybrid gain dominates the exploration overhead.

2. On HYRULE:
The offline-to-online warm-start idea is natural, and the regret result is strong. I especially like that the algorithm itself does not need the partition, yet the analysis adapts to the best partition. This is elegant. The proof strategy in Appendix E seems technically substantial, especially the modified bonus summation lemma and the offline/online regret split.

However, the very large polynomial lower bound on Non, Noff weakens the practical impact. Also, the PAC conversion in Corollary 2 is standard but not especially satisfying if the motivating advantage of RAPPEL is precisely that it outputs a fixed policy.

3. On exposition:
Overall the paper is readable for a technical audience. The introduction is strong, and Table 1 and Table 2 are useful. The proof sketches in Sections 3.1 and 3.2 help. Still, there are places where notation becomes heavy quickly. A short “roadmap of symbols” or a compact notation table would help.

4. On experiments:
The figures on pages 12 and 13 are useful:
- Figure 1 shows improved coverage under hybrid exploration relative to online-only, even with adversarial offline data.
- Figure 2 suggests the hybrid dataset is more useful for offline planning than either offline-only or online-only data in that setup.
- Figure 3 shows lower regret for the warm-started online method than online-only LSVI-UCB++.
These are directionally supportive, but still modest. I would not want the paper to oversell empirical evidence.

Questions for the authors:

1. Can the authors clarify the operational meaning of the partition optimization in Theorems 1 and 2? Is there any constructive procedure for finding a near-optimal partition from data?

2. For RAPPEL, how sensitive is performance to the tolerance parameter and to mis-specification of don? Can the authors provide either a heuristic or a data-driven selection rule beyond inspecting singular values?

3. In Assumption 2, the paper says the required feature transformation is achievable in practice. Is this transformation assumed to be computed from offline data only? Does it preserve all theorem assumptions exactly?

4. How much of the large lower-bound requirement in Theorem 2 is believed to be an artifact of proof technique versus fundamentally needed for the current algorithm?

5. Could the authors provide a more explicit corollary comparing HYRULE directly to the best known online-only regret bound under representative regimes such as Noff >> Non?

Suggestions for improvement:

1. Add a short notation table and a clearer summary of the main quantities coff(Xoff), con(Xon), doff, don, alpha_off, alpha_on.

2. Expand the discussion of partition choice and tolerance selection, ideally with a practical recipe and perhaps an empirical ablation.

3. Strengthen the experiments:
- include at least one additional environment,
- show sensitivity to offline data quality and quantity,
- compare against more hybrid baselines if feasible,
- include scaling plots with Noff and Non.

4. Clarify the role and severity of the burn-in/sample-size assumptions in the main text, not only in the discussion section.

5. If space permits, provide a simplified corollary or informal theorem statement that isolates the main gain in the most interpretable regime.

Recommendation:
Accept / weak accept.

Confidence:
Medium.

Reason for recommendation:
The paper makes a meaningful theoretical advance on an important open problem in hybrid RL with function approximation. The main results appear novel, technically nontrivial, and stronger than prior work in linear MDPs. My reservations are mostly about assumptions, finite-sample practicality, and limited experiments, rather than about the core correctness or significance of the theory. If the proofs check out, this is a worthwhile contribution.
