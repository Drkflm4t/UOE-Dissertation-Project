This paper studies hybrid reinforcement learning in linear MDPs, where an agent can use both an offline dataset and online interaction. The paper proposes two algorithms corresponding to the two main hybrid paradigms: an online-to-offline method, RAPPEL, which performs reward-agnostic exploration and then runs pessimistic offline RL; and an offline-to-online method, HYRULE, which warm-starts an online optimistic algorithm using offline data. The main claims are that both algorithms are computationally efficient, avoid single-policy concentrability assumptions, and achieve guarantees that can improve over the best known offline-only or online-only rates in linear MDPs. The paper positions itself as the first to obtain such improvements with function approximation and for both PAC and regret settings.

Overall, I found the paper interesting, timely, and technically ambitious. The central question is important: whether hybrid RL can provably outperform the separate offline-only and online-only baselines without relying on strong coverage assumptions. The paper gives a fairly compelling affirmative answer in the linear MDP setting, and the results appear to advance the state of the art. In particular, the distinction between the two hybrid paradigms is clearly articulated in the introduction, and it is valuable that the paper addresses both rather than only one. The theoretical message is also easy to summarize: hybrid RL can exploit whichever source of data is better on a given subspace of the feature space, and this can lead to strictly better guarantees than treating the problem as purely offline or purely online.

The strongest aspect of the paper is the novelty and significance of the theoretical contribution. For RAPPEL, the bound in Theorem 1 improves substantially over the prior hybrid linear-MDP PAC result of Wagenmaker and Pacchiano, and the comparison table on page 4 makes the improvement visually clear. The paper also argues that the bound is never worse than the offline-only benchmark from Xiong et al. and can be better when online exploration fills in poorly covered directions. For HYRULE, Theorem 2 gives a regret bound that is no worse than the online-only minimax-optimal rate and can improve when the offline data is informative. This is a meaningful conceptual contribution: the paper does not merely show that offline data can help constants, but that hybridization can improve the dependence on effective coverage quantities through a partition of the feature space. That is a strong and elegant idea.

A second major strength is the technical synthesis. The paper combines several recent ingredients—OPTCOV-style reward-agnostic exploration, pessimistic offline RL for linear MDPs, and LSVI-UCB++-style online learning—and adapts them in nontrivial ways. The use of a partition into offline-covered and online-covered subspaces is intuitive and appears to be the right abstraction for hybrid RL without global concentrability. The dimensional refinement from ambient dimension d to quantities like don and coff(Xoff) is also appealing. The proof strategy for maintaining H^3-type dependence via truncation and total-variance arguments seems technically thoughtful and is one of the more interesting aspects of the work.

The paper is also reasonably well written at a high level. The introduction motivates the problem well, Table 1 and Table 2 are useful, and the discussion section is refreshingly candid about limitations. The numerical experiments, while limited, do support the qualitative claims that hybridization can help even with poor offline data. In particular, the plots on pages 12–13 are helpful: Figure 1 shows improved coverage under hybrid exploration relative to online-only, Figure 2 suggests that the hybrid dataset can be more useful than either offline-only or online-only data in the adversarial-behavior setting, and Figure 3 shows lower regret for the warm-started online method than for online-only LSVI-UCB++.

That said, I also have several concerns, mostly around clarity, assumptions, and the sharpness of the empirical validation.

My main concern is that the paper’s headline claim that hybrid RL “breaks sample size barriers” is somewhat stronger than what is fully established in the body. The results do improve over offline-only or online-only baselines, but each algorithm improves one side more directly than the other: RAPPEL improves over offline-only error and can outperform prior hybrid PAC bounds, while HYRULE improves over online-only regret. The discussion section itself acknowledges that the paper does not yet provide a single algorithm that simultaneously improves upon both the best offline-only and online-only rates. This does not invalidate the results, but it does suggest the title and some framing could be more precise. As written, a reader may initially expect a unified hybrid algorithm dominating both paradigms simultaneously.

A second concern is the role and practicality of the partition Xoff/Xon. The theory is built around this partition, and the guarantees are expressed in terms of coff(Xoff) and don. For HYRULE, the algorithm is claimed to be partition-unaware and the bound adapts to the best partition, which is elegant. For RAPPEL, however, the user must effectively choose a tolerance related to don, and the paper states on page 9 that one may estimate this via an SVD plot or use a generic tolerance at the cost of losing the dimensional improvement. This is a meaningful caveat. In practice, the usefulness of RAPPEL depends on identifying the right effective online subspace, but the paper does not provide a principled data-driven procedure with guarantees for doing so. Since the partition is central to the claimed gains, this issue deserves more emphasis and perhaps a more formal treatment.

Relatedly, the definitions of coff(Xoff) and con(Xon) are somewhat abstract and may be difficult for readers to interpret operationally. The paper does explain them as projected coverage quantities, but more intuition or examples would help. For instance, a simple geometric example showing how a poor behavior policy can still yield small coff(Xoff) on a large subspace while leaving a low-dimensional hard subspace for online exploration would make the contribution more concrete.

A third concern is the assumptions behind RAPPEL. Assumption 2 requires full-rank projected covariates on the online partition, equivalently finite con(Xon). The paper argues on page 7 that this is achievable in practice by projecting onto the nonzero singular space of the offline covariance, and the Tetris experiments use such a projection. However, this explanation is not entirely satisfying theoretically. If the projection is derived from the offline covariance, it is not obvious that it preserves the directions needed for optimal online exploration in the underlying MDP, especially when the offline data are poor. More importantly, the assumption that there exists a policy spanning the online partition is natural but still nontrivial; it would help to discuss more explicitly how restrictive this is relative to standard linear-MDP assumptions. The statement in Lemma 1 that con(Xon) ≤ don is elegant, but it depends on the infimum over policies and the geometry of the projected feature space; some readers may find the assumption-to-conclusion path too compressed.

A fourth concern is that the burn-in/sample-size requirements are quite large and somewhat hidden behind asymptotic notation. For example, Theorem 2 requires Non, Noff = \tilde{\Omega}(d^{13} H^{14}), which is extremely large. The paper is transparent that these are inherited from prior analyses and truncation arguments, but this substantially limits the practical meaning of the regret theorem. Likewise, RAPPEL inherits the nontrivial OPTCOV burn-in cost N*. These issues do not negate the asymptotic contribution, but they do weaken the practical significance of the guarantees. I would encourage the authors to foreground this more clearly in the main theorem statements or discussion, rather than mainly in the limitations section.

On the technical side, I found the proof ideas plausible from the appendices, but there are places where the exposition could be tightened. Theorem 1’s second bound in equation (8) is somewhat hard to parse, and the transition between the two forms of the bound is not especially intuitive. The proof sketch on page 9 helps, but the notation around weighted covariances, truncated variances, and the role of alpha_off/alpha_on could be explained more carefully. Similarly, for HYRULE, the proof sketch on pages 11–12 gives the right high-level decomposition, but the origin of some large polynomial terms and how they disappear under the final sample-size condition is easy to lose track of. Since the paper’s contribution is primarily theoretical, readability of the main proof ideas matters a lot.

I also noticed a few presentation issues. The notation is dense, and there are several places where symbols are introduced quickly and reused in slightly different forms. For example, the paper uses both covariance matrices Lambda_h and Sigma_h, plus starred and barred variants, and the distinction between offline-only, combined, and variance-weighted versions is easy to miss. The algorithm descriptions in the main text are concise, but perhaps too concise for readers unfamiliar with the cited subroutines. In Algorithm 1, “Run OPTCOV” and “run LinPEVI-ADV+” hides much of the actual mechanism; this is acceptable for a theory paper, but then the main text should perhaps spend a bit more effort explaining what exactly is inherited and what is new. The same applies to Algorithm 2, where the warm-starting idea is simple conceptually but the actual update rules are fairly involved.

The empirical section is useful but limited. The experiments are all on a scaled-down Tetris environment with handcrafted linear features obtained by projecting one-hot encodings onto the top 60 eigenvectors of the offline covariance. This setup is reasonable for illustration, but it is also closely aligned with the paper’s assumptions and may favor the proposed methods. The experiments mainly show qualitative trends rather than stress-testing the theory. I would have liked to see at least one additional environment, some ablation on the amount or quality of offline data, and perhaps sensitivity to the chosen tolerance in RAPPEL. Since the paper emphasizes robustness to poor behavior policies, a more systematic sweep over behavior-policy quality would strengthen the empirical story. Also, the implementation note on page 12 says that for simplicity the experiments substitute LSVI-UCB for FORCE inside OPTCOV and LinPEVI-ADV for LinPEVI-ADV+, which means the experiments are not exactly evaluating the stated algorithms. This is understandable, but it should be discussed more explicitly as a limitation.

Another subtle issue is the comparison to lower bounds. Table 2 is helpful, but some of the comparisons mix instance-dependent upper bounds, worst-case upper bounds, and lower bounds under different assumptions. For example, the offline row involves bounds under single-policy concentrability, while the hybrid results are framed without requiring it. The paper is careful in wording, but the table could still leave a casual reader with the impression of a cleaner apples-to-apples domination than is strictly true. I would suggest clarifying in the caption or surrounding text exactly which comparisons are worst-case, which are instance-dependent, and under what assumptions.

Despite these concerns, I think the paper is a strong contribution. The core ideas are novel, the results appear meaningful, and the work addresses an important open question in hybrid RL with function approximation. The limitations are real, especially the large polynomial burn-in and the practical dependence on partition/tolerance choices, but these seem more like opportunities for follow-up work than fatal flaws.

For improvement, I would recommend the following. First, moderate the framing slightly to distinguish clearly between “improves over offline-only or online-only baselines via two complementary algorithms” and “a single algorithm that dominates both simultaneously.” Second, add more intuition and perhaps a toy example for the partition-based quantities coff(Xoff) and con(Xon). Third, discuss more explicitly how one might choose the RAPPEL tolerance in a principled way, and whether any adaptive scheme is possible. Fourth, strengthen the empirical section with at least one more environment or a more systematic ablation over offline-data quality and quantity. Fifth, improve the exposition of the main proof ideas, especially the role of the truncation argument and how the H^3 dependence is obtained.

In summary, I view this as a strong theory paper with genuine novelty and significance. It advances the understanding of hybrid RL in linear MDPs, provides the best known guarantees in this setting, and opens several promising directions. My overall assessment is positive.
