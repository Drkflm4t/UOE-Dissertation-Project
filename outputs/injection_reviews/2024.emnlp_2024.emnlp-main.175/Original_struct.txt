Summary: This paper proposes an automated method to characterize NLI datasets into easy, ambiguous, and hard subsets using training dynamics. The approach extends dataset cartography by combining 8 features derived from two training settings—premise+hypothesis and hypothesis-only—and clustering examples with a Gaussian Mixture Model. Applied to SNLI, MultiNLI, and FEVER, the method identifies hard subsets with substantially lower model accuracy and reduced heuristic indicators of spurious correlations. The authors also show that applying the same characterization to training data can filter examples aggressively while retaining comparable downstream performance, sometimes outperforming prior training-dynamics-based selection methods.

Strengths: Addresses an important and well-motivated problem: standard NLI benchmarks contain annotation artifacts and spurious correlations that inflate performance.; Method is conceptually simple, general, and largely model-agnostic: it relies only on training dynamics and clustering, not architecture-specific internals.; Nice extension of prior dataset cartography work by combining multiple dynamics features (confidence, variability, correctness, AUM) across both full-input and hypothesis-only settings.; Empirical results are intuitive and compelling: accuracy drops sharply on the induced hard splits (e.g., around 56% on SNLI-hard and 53% on MultiNLI-hard for RoBERTa), while hypothesis-only performance approaches chance on hard subsets.; Includes analysis of heuristic spurious-correlation measures and statistical testing showing reduced class-specific artifacts in the hard split.; Covers multiple datasets, including FEVER, suggesting some breadth beyond classic NLI benchmarks.; Cross-model comparison with RoBERTa and DeBERTa supports the claim that the induced difficulty splits transfer across encoders.; The training-set characterization experiments are practically interesting and suggest the method may improve data efficiency relative to Data Maps and AUM baselines.

Weaknesses: A central methodological concern is that the test-set characterization trains directly on the test set to obtain training dynamics. Although used only for characterization, this is unconventional and risks entangling evaluation with test-set-specific fitting.; The paper fixes the number of clusters to three and then maps them to easy/ambiguous/hard, but provides limited justification or ablation for this choice versus alternative numbers of clusters or clustering methods.; The evidence for reduced spurious correlations relies mainly on a small set of surface heuristics (word overlap, antonyms, negation, etc.), which may not capture deeper artifacts or reasoning shortcuts.; There is limited ablation isolating the contribution of each feature group: P+H dynamics vs hypothesis-only dynamics, and the added features beyond standard dataset cartography.; The claim of model-agnosticism is only partially validated with two encoder families; broader testing across substantially different model classes would strengthen it.; Some training-set selection results are mixed: models trained on all data still achieve the best overall score in at least some settings, so the practical gains are not uniformly strong.; The paper occasionally conflates 'hard examples' with 'less spuriously correlated examples'; these are related but not identical notions, especially for FEVER where hardness may reflect genuine complexity rather than artifact reduction.; There is little discussion of computational cost: the method requires multiple training runs over the target dataset, including hypothesis-only and full-input models, which may be expensive for large benchmarks.

Soundness Issues: Training on the test set, even if only to derive dynamics-based features, is a nonstandard evaluation protocol and should be more carefully justified. It may produce a benchmark tailored to the inductive biases of the characterization models rather than an intrinsic notion of difficulty.; The clustering objective is unsupervised, but the semantic interpretation of clusters as difficulty levels is imposed post hoc via average confidence. This is reasonable, yet it would benefit from stronger validation that the clusters are stable and meaningful across random seeds.; The statistical analysis of heuristic measures is useful, but the paper does not fully establish causality between reduced heuristics and improved benchmark validity.; The comparison to Data Maps and AUM for training-set characterization may not be entirely apples-to-apples because thresholding and subset construction choices for baselines appear somewhat heuristic.; Because the hard split is relatively small (roughly 10–13% for SNLI/MultiNLI), conclusions about benchmark-wide evaluation utility should be tempered; the method may be identifying a niche subset rather than a replacement evaluation standard.
