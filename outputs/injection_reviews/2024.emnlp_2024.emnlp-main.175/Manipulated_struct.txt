Summary: The paper proposes an automated method to characterize NLI datasets into easy, ambiguous, and hard subsets using training dynamics. It extends dataset cartography by combining eight features derived from two training settings—premise+hypothesis and hypothesis-only—and clustering examples with a Gaussian Mixture Model. Applied to SNLI, MultiNLI, and FEVER, the method identifies harder subsets with substantially lower model accuracy and reduced heuristic indicators of spurious correlations. The authors also show that applying the same characterization to training data can filter examples aggressively while retaining comparable downstream performance, sometimes outperforming prior characterization methods such as Data Maps and AUM.

Strengths: Addresses an important and well-motivated problem: inflated NLI performance due to annotation artifacts and spurious correlations.; Method is conceptually simple, automated, and does not require manual construction of adversarial examples.; Nice extension of dataset cartography: combines multiple training-dynamics features and hypothesis-only signals rather than relying on a single thresholded statistic.; Empirical results are reasonably broad, covering SNLI, MultiNLI, and FEVER, with both test-set and training-set characterization experiments.; The hard splits appear meaningfully more challenging: accuracy drops sharply, especially for hypothesis-only models, suggesting reduced artifact exploitation.; Includes analysis of heuristic spurious-correlation measures and statistical testing, which helps support the claim that hard subsets are less artifact-driven.; Provides some evidence of model-agnostic behavior via cross-model comparison between RoBERTa and DeBERTa.; Training-set filtering results are practically interesting, suggesting data-efficiency gains over Data Maps and AUM baselines.; Qualitative examples and figures help illustrate what the easy vs. hard splits look like.

Weaknesses: A central methodological concern is that the test-set characterization is obtained by training directly on the test set to collect training dynamics, which is unusual from an evaluation standpoint and may limit the practical interpretation of the resulting benchmark.; The notion of 'difficulty' is partly model-dependent despite claims of model agnosticism; evidence is limited to two encoder families and similar fine-tuning setups.; The paper relies heavily on heuristic proxies for spurious correlations (word overlap, antonyms, negation, etc.), which capture only a narrow subset of artifacts.; There is no comparison against stronger contemporary hard-NLI benchmarks or alternative difficulty estimation methods beyond Data Maps and AUM.; The choice of exactly three GMM clusters seems inherited from prior work rather than strongly justified empirically.; Some claims about providing a 'more realistic' evaluation are plausible but not fully validated with external human judgments or downstream transfer evidence.; FEVER is repurposed as NLI, but the setup differs structurally from SNLI/MultiNLI, making cross-dataset conclusions somewhat uneven.; The paper would benefit from clearer reporting of variance across random seeds and robustness of the clustering assignments.

Soundness Issues: Training on the test set, even if only to derive training dynamics, risks entangling benchmark construction with model memorization behavior; the paper should more carefully justify why this yields a valid evaluation split rather than a model-specific partition.; The statistical analysis shows reduced heuristic differences across classes in hard splits, but this does not by itself establish absence of spurious correlations or improved construct validity.; The claim of model independence is only partially supported: cross-model consistency is shown for RoBERTa and DeBERTa, but both are similar pretrained transformer classifiers, so broader generalization remains uncertain.; Using average intra-cluster confidence to order clusters as easy/ambiguous/hard may circularly align the partition with model performance, so 'difficulty' may largely reflect learnability under the chosen model rather than intrinsic example hardness.; For training-set characterization, gains may depend on the specific stress-test evaluation and subset proportions; stronger significance testing and seed averaging would strengthen the conclusions.
