Summary

This paper proposes an automated method to characterize NLI datasets into difficulty levels using training dynamics. The core idea is to extend dataset cartography by collecting 8 features per example: confidence, variability, correctness, and AUM, each computed under two training settings: premise+hypothesis and hypothesis-only. These features are clustered with a 3-component Gaussian Mixture Model to partition examples into easy, ambiguous, and hard subsets. The authors apply the method to SNLI, MultiNLI, and FEVER, arguing that the hard subsets reduce spurious correlations and provide a more realistic stress test. They also apply the same characterization to training data and show that training on selected subsets can match or nearly match full-data performance on the Naik et al. stress test while using less data.

Overall assessment

The paper addresses an important and timely problem: standard NLI benchmarks are known to contain annotation artifacts and shortcuts, and better evaluation sets are needed. The proposed approach is intuitive, reasonably simple, and potentially useful in practice. I especially appreciate the attempt to avoid manually constructed adversarial examples and instead mine naturally occurring difficult examples from existing benchmarks. The inclusion of hypothesis-only dynamics is a sensible and novel ingredient for NLI specifically.

That said, I have several concerns about the experimental design and the strength of the claims. The biggest issue is that the method characterizes the test set by training directly on the test set. Although the authors state this is only for collecting dynamics and not for evaluation as a classifier, this still makes the notion of “difficulty” partly dependent on memorization behavior on the evaluation set itself. This weakens the claim that the resulting split is a clean, reusable benchmark. In addition, the paper’s central claims about reducing spurious correlations and yielding a more realistic evaluation are supported mostly by a small set of heuristics and by performance drops, but not by stronger external validation. The training-set filtering results are interesting, but they also need more careful framing and stronger baselines.

My recommendation is borderline to weak reject in a competitive venue, mainly because the idea is promising but the current empirical validation and methodology do not yet fully support the paper’s strongest claims.

Strengths

1. Important problem
The paper tackles a real weakness of NLI evaluation: inflated performance due to artifacts and shortcuts in SNLI/MultiNLI. This is a meaningful contribution area.

2. Simple and practical method
The method is easy to understand and implement. The workflow shown in the diagram on page 3 is clear: train P+H and H-only models, extract dynamics features, concatenate them, and cluster with GMM.

3. NLI-specific extension of dataset cartography
Using both standard training-dynamics features and hypothesis-only dynamics is a nice adaptation to NLI. This is more tailored than simply reusing prior cartography methods unchanged.

4. Natural examples rather than synthetic adversarial construction
The paper’s emphasis on retaining naturally occurring examples is appealing. This could make the resulting hard subsets more realistic than some manually engineered stress tests.

5. Some evidence of cross-model stability
The RoBERTa/DeBERTa comparison in Table 4 suggests that the discovered splits are not entirely model-specific, which is encouraging.

6. Training-set characterization results are potentially useful
The results in Tables 5 and 6 suggest that subset selection based on the proposed characterization can be competitive with or better than Data Maps/AUM baselines on the Naik et al. stress test.

Weaknesses and concerns

1. Training on the test set is a major methodological concern
The paper’s main procedure explicitly trains on the test set to derive training dynamics for test examples. Even if this is not used as a deployed classifier, it still uses labels from the test set and adapts model parameters to those exact examples. This is unusual for benchmark construction and raises several issues:
- The “difficulty” estimate may reflect ease of memorization under repeated supervised exposure, not intrinsic evaluation difficulty.
- The resulting split is not independent of the model family, optimization setup, number of epochs, and hyperparameters.
- It is hard to claim this yields a benchmark for fair model comparison if benchmark construction itself requires supervised fitting on the benchmark labels.

This concern should be addressed much more directly. At minimum, the paper should compare against a setup where difficulty is inferred without training on the target test set, e.g., via cross-dataset transfer, cross-validation style held-out dynamics, or dynamics from a model trained only on the training set.

2. The notion of “hard” is partly circular
Examples are defined as hard using features derived from model learning behavior, and then the paper shows that models perform worse on them. This is expected by construction. The more important question is whether these examples are hard in a linguistically meaningful and benchmark-relevant sense, beyond being low-confidence or unstable for the chosen models. The paper provides some qualitative examples and heuristic analyses, but this is not enough to establish the broader claim.

3. Limited validation of “reduced spurious correlations”
The paper uses five heuristics (word overlap, antonyms, length mismatch, misspellings, negation) and shows that differences across labels diminish in the hard split. This is useful, but limited:
- These heuristics capture only a narrow slice of known NLI artifacts.
- Reduced separability under these heuristics does not necessarily imply reduced shortcut exploitation overall.
- The claim that the hard split is “more realistic” is not strongly validated.

A stronger evaluation would include external artifact detectors, challenge sets, human judgments of difficulty/naturalness, or transfer to out-of-distribution benchmarks such as ANLI or other adversarial NLI sets.

4. Comparison to prior work is somewhat incomplete
The paper compares training-set filtering to Data Maps and AUM, but for test-set construction the comparisons are weaker. Since the paper’s main contribution is benchmark characterization, it would help to compare against simpler alternatives such as:
- ranking by P+H confidence only,
- ranking by H-only confidence only,
- ranking by confidence gap between P+H and H-only,
- standard cartography thresholds without GMM,
- entropy/margin-based ranking without clustering.

Without these ablations, it is hard to know whether the full 8-feature GMM machinery is necessary.

5. Choice of 3 clusters is under-justified
The paper fixes three clusters to align with easy/ambiguous/hard terminology from prior work. But if GMM is introduced to avoid arbitrary thresholds, fixing the number of clusters to 3 is itself an arbitrary structural assumption. There is no model selection analysis (e.g., BIC/AIC, stability, silhouette-like diagnostics) to justify that three clusters are appropriate across datasets.

6. FEVER setup is not entirely convincing
The paper includes FEVER “repurposed for NLI,” but the formulation differs from SNLI/MultiNLI in important ways, as the authors note on page 7: premise/hypothesis lengths and roles differ substantially. The hypothesis-only baseline on FEVER is near chance when trained on train/dev, but much higher when trained on dev/dev (Table 1), which suggests the dynamics may behave differently there. The FEVER results feel more like an auxiliary demonstration than strong evidence for generality.

7. Statistical testing is narrow
The Mann-Whitney tests in the analysis of heuristics are fine as far as they go, but they do not address the main claims. Also, the paper reports many significance markers in Figure 3 but does not clearly discuss effect sizes. Given the large sample sizes, significance alone is not very informative.

8. Some claims are overstated
Examples:
- “providing a more accurate measure of model performance in the real world” is too strong given the evidence.
- “model-independent” is stronger than what is shown; only RoBERTa and DeBERTa are tested, both similar pretrained transformer encoders.
- “general” and “easily extensible” may be true in principle, but the method still requires supervised labels and repeated training on the target set.

Detailed comments

On the method

The feature design is sensible. Confidence, variability, correctness, and AUM are standard and complementary. Including both P+H and H-only versions is the most interesting part of the paper. However, I would like to see:
- feature importance analysis,
- correlation matrix among the 8 features,
- ablation of each feature group,
- whether H-only features alone already recover most of the benefit.

The GMM choice is plausible, but the paper should explain:
- covariance type used,
- initialization details,
- sensitivity to random seed,
- whether clusters are stable across runs.

On the results in Table 3

The monotonic drop from easy to hard is clear for SNLI and MultiNLI, and especially striking for H-only models. This supports the claim that the hard split reduces hypothesis-only artifacts. However, because the split is derived from training dynamics on the same test set, this result is less surprising than it first appears.

The class imbalance shift shown in Figure 4 is important. Hard splits contain more neutral examples. This raises a concern: is the hard split simply harder because neutral is harder? The paper acknowledges this but does not fully disentangle it. A stronger analysis would report per-class accuracy within each difficulty split and perhaps construct label-balanced versions of the splits.

On the heuristic analysis in Figure 3

The plots on page 6 are useful and visually support the claim that easy splits have stronger artifact signatures. Still, the heuristics are fairly shallow. For example, word overlap and negation are classic signals, but modern NLI artifacts include many lexical and annotation-style cues not captured here. It would help to quantify whether a simple artifact classifier can predict labels much less effectively on the hard split than on the easy split.

On cross-model generalization

Table 4 is encouraging but mixed. The splits transfer to DeBERTa reasonably well, but some numbers are odd, especially ambiguous splits where accuracies can be unexpectedly high depending on source/target characterization. This deserves more interpretation. Also, two encoder families are not enough to support a broad model-agnostic claim. At least one non-RoBERTa-like architecture or a smaller model would strengthen this point.

On training-set characterization

This section is interesting and perhaps the most practically useful part of the paper. The results in Tables 5 and 6 suggest that selected subsets can preserve or improve stress-test performance with less data. However:
- The evaluation is only on the Naik et al. stress test, which itself is synthetic and narrow.
- It would be useful to also report standard validation/test accuracy to show the tradeoff between robustness and in-domain performance.
- The subset naming is a bit confusing, especially because “Amb” and “Amb+Hard” sometimes outperform “All”; more discussion is needed on why.
- The baseline implementations need more detail to ensure fairness.

Questions for the authors

1. Why is it acceptable, in your view, to train on the test set labels to construct the benchmark? How do you distinguish intrinsic difficulty from memorization dynamics on the benchmark itself?

2. Have you tried constructing difficulty splits using only models trained on the training set, then applying those scores to the test set without fitting on test labels?

3. How sensitive are the clusters to:
- number of epochs,
- random seed,
- GMM covariance type,
- number of clusters?

4. How much of the gain comes specifically from hypothesis-only features? What happens with only P+H dynamics, only H-only dynamics, or a simple confidence-gap score?

5. Can you provide label-balanced evaluations within each split to rule out the possibility that the hard split is mainly a neutral-heavy subset?

6. Have you evaluated whether the hard split better predicts performance on external challenge sets such as ANLI or other OOD NLI benchmarks?

7. For FEVER, what exact NLI formulation is used, and how do the label semantics align with entailment/neutral/contradiction?

Suggestions for improvement

1. Remove or substantially strengthen the test-set training methodology
The paper would be much stronger if difficulty estimation did not require supervised training on the target test set. Even a cross-fitting or leave-partition-out approach would be preferable.

2. Add stronger baselines and ablations
Compare against:
- confidence-only,
- margin-only,
- H-only confidence,
- P+H minus H confidence,
- standard Data Maps on test examples,
- no-GMM thresholding.

3. Validate with external benchmarks
Show that models’ performance on your hard split correlates better with performance on ANLI or other challenge sets than standard SNLI/MultiNLI accuracy does.

4. Control for label distribution
Provide label-balanced hard/easy subsets or per-label analyses.

5. Improve the analysis of realism
Human evaluation of naturalness/difficulty would help support the claim that the hard split is more realistic than synthetic stress tests.

6. Clarify reproducibility details
Include GMM hyperparameters, seed variance, and computational cost. Training two models on each target test set for multiple epochs is not trivial.

Minor comments

- There are several wording issues and occasional overclaims that should be toned down.
- The paper sometimes uses “test set characterization” and “stress test construction” interchangeably; these are related but not identical.
- Table 1 is useful, but the “test/test” and “dev/dev” rows should be framed more carefully since they may be misread as evaluation results rather than dynamics-collection runs.
- In Table 7, the “Correct?” column uses question marks for likely mislabeled examples; this is interesting but should be discussed more systematically if included.
- The appendix algorithm is clear and helpful.

Reason for recommendation

The paper has a good motivation and a promising idea, especially the use of hypothesis-only training dynamics to identify artifact-heavy examples. However, the central methodology of training on the test set to define benchmark difficulty is a substantial concern, and the empirical validation does not yet fully justify the strongest claims about realism, generality, and improved evaluation quality. With stronger methodological safeguards, more ablations, and external validation, this could become a solid contribution.
