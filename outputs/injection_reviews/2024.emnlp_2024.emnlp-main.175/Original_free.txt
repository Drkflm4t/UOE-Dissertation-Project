This paper studies a timely and important problem: standard NLI benchmarks such as SNLI and MultiNLI are known to contain annotation artifacts and spurious correlations that make reported test accuracy an unreliable proxy for genuine language understanding. The paper proposes an automated way to partition an existing test set into three difficulty levels using training-dynamics features, with the goal of isolating a harder subset that is less contaminated by shortcuts. The method extends dataset cartography by combining eight features per example: confidence, variability, correctness, and AUM, each computed under both premise+hypothesis and hypothesis-only training. These features are clustered with a 3-component Gaussian mixture model, and clusters are ordered by average confidence to define easy, ambiguous, and hard subsets. The paper evaluates this idea on SNLI, MultiNLI, and FEVER, and also explores using the same characterization procedure on training data for data filtering.

Overall, I find the paper interesting and potentially useful. The central idea is intuitive and reasonably well motivated: if examples are easy to fit both with full input and with hypothesis-only input, they are likely to reflect artifacts; if they remain difficult even under full input, they may better probe genuine inference. The paper also has a practical flavor that I appreciate: it does not require manual adversarial construction, and it aims to reuse naturally occurring examples rather than synthetic stress-test items. The inclusion of FEVER is also a nice attempt to show broader applicability beyond canonical NLI.

That said, while the empirical trends are promising, I have substantial concerns about the methodological framing and the strength of the claims. In its current form, I would view the paper as a useful exploratory study rather than a fully convincing benchmark-construction method. The main issue is that the proposed “hard” split is defined using supervised training on the test set itself. Although the authors are careful to say that the model trained on the test set is used only to gather training dynamics and not as the final classifier, this still means the benchmark is constructed by fitting labels on the evaluation set. That is a strong intervention on the test set, and it raises questions about circularity, benchmark contamination, and whether the resulting hardness reflects intrinsic linguistic difficulty versus idiosyncrasies of optimization dynamics under a particular model family and training recipe. This does not invalidate the analysis, but it weakens the claim that the method yields a generally better test set for evaluation.

The paper’s strongest empirical result is the monotonic drop in accuracy across easy, ambiguous, and hard splits in Table 3, together with the reduction of hypothesis-only performance on the hard split. For SNLI, RoBERTa drops from 0.97 on easy to 0.56 on hard, and the hypothesis-only model drops from 0.82 to 0.38. MultiNLI shows a similar pattern, from 0.94 to 0.53 and from 0.67 to 0.39. These are meaningful differences and support the claim that the hard subset is more challenging and less exploitable by hypothesis-only shortcuts. The visualizations on page 6 are also helpful: Figure 2 shows clear separation in confidence and margin distributions across difficulty levels, and Figure 3 suggests that heuristic artifact measures become less class-distinguishing in the hard split. These are good signs.

However, the evidence that the hard split is specifically “less spurious” rather than simply “harder for RoBERTa-like models trained in this way” is still incomplete. The heuristic analysis is limited to a small set of shallow measures: word overlap, antonyms, length mismatch, misspellings, and negation. These are reasonable starting points, but they cover only a narrow slice of known NLI artifacts. The conclusion that the hard split “minimizes spurious correlations” feels too strong given this limited operationalization. At best, the paper shows reduction in several selected heuristics and in hypothesis-only predictability. That is valuable, but narrower than the headline claim.

A related concern is that the clustering objective is not directly tied to artifact detection or linguistic validity. The GMM clusters examples in an 8-dimensional feature space derived from training dynamics, and clusters are then ordered by average confidence under premise+hypothesis training. This may indeed recover useful strata, but there is little analysis of cluster stability, sensitivity, or identifiability. Why exactly three clusters? The choice is inherited from dataset cartography, but here it is treated as a natural decomposition rather than a design decision. It would strengthen the paper considerably to report whether the conclusions hold for different numbers of clusters, different random seeds, or alternative clustering methods. Since GMMs can be sensitive to initialization and covariance assumptions, some robustness analysis seems necessary.

The use of training on the test set also creates a conceptual ambiguity around what “difficulty” means. Because the model is optimized directly on the test labels for five epochs, examples that are hard may include genuinely subtle inference cases, mislabeled items, minority phenomena, or simply examples that are atypical under the pretrained encoder’s inductive bias. The qualitative examples on page 12 partly support this ambiguity: some “easy” SNLI contradiction examples appear mislabeled or at least dubious, which is consistent with the authors’ interpretation, but some “hard” examples also look annotation-sensitive rather than clearly more linguistically sophisticated. The paper acknowledges in the limitations that the method is unsuitable for automatically identifying mislabeled examples, but this caveat should be more central because mislabeled or ambiguous items may be a major component of the hard split.

The FEVER results are interesting but underdeveloped. Table 3 shows a dramatic drop on the hard split for FEVER, but the interpretation is less clear because FEVER is structurally different from SNLI/MultiNLI: the “hypothesis” side is actually evidence text and can be much longer, as the authors note. The heuristic measures are therefore not directly comparable, and the negative length mismatch is a reminder that the setup is somewhat forced. If FEVER is included as evidence of generality, I would like to see a more careful discussion of how the method transfers across task formulations and whether the same features remain meaningful.

I also have reservations about the model-agnosticity claim. The cross-model comparison in Table 4 is encouraging in the sense that hard subsets remain harder across RoBERTa and DeBERTa, but this is still a fairly narrow test: both are closely related pretrained encoder architectures fine-tuned in similar ways. The paper states that the method is model-independent because it uses only logits and confidences, but in practice those quantities are highly model- and calibration-dependent, as the authors themselves note on page 4. So the evidence supports some degree of transfer across similar encoders, not full model-agnosticity. The claim should be softened.

The training-set characterization section is promising but also somewhat confusing in its interpretation. Tables 5 and 6 show that training on selected subsets can match or occasionally exceed training on all data on Naik et al.’s stress test while using less data. This is potentially useful. However, the best-performing subset is not consistently the “hard” subset; in fact, training only on hard examples performs very poorly, while combinations such as ambiguous+hard or easy+ambiguous do better. This suggests the method is not simply identifying “good” versus “bad” data, but rather partitioning data into strata with different utility depending on the target evaluation. That is fine, but the paper sometimes overstates this as “aggressive filtering of uninformative examples” or “increasing data quality.” The evidence is more nuanced.

There are also several experimental details that need clarification for reproducibility and for assessing validity. First, the paper says models are trained on the test set for five epochs to gather dynamics, but it is not fully clear whether hyperparameters were tuned elsewhere, whether multiple seeds were used, and how much variance there is in the resulting splits. Second, the GMM setup is underspecified: covariance type, initialization strategy, number of restarts, and whether class imbalance affects clustering are not discussed. Third, the statistical testing in Figure 3 is only briefly described. Since many pairwise tests are performed across datasets, labels, heuristics, and difficulty levels, the exact testing protocol and effect sizes should be reported more carefully. Fourth, the paper would benefit from reporting the overlap between splits induced by different seeds or different encoders, not just downstream accuracy on each other’s splits.

A broader concern is evaluation leakage. If the community were to adopt this method to define a “hard test set,” then the hard set would be derived using labels from the original test set and a specific family of models. This may be acceptable for post hoc analysis of benchmark artifacts, but it is less convincing as a benchmark-construction paradigm for fair future evaluation. A stronger setup would derive hardness from training dynamics on the training set or on a held-out development set, then transfer the learned characterization to the test set without fitting on test labels. Alternatively, one could characterize examples using cross-validation or leave-one-fold-out procedures to avoid directly training on the same examples used for evaluation. As written, the method is best viewed as a diagnostic partitioning of an existing benchmark, not a clean replacement test set.

Despite these concerns, I do think the paper has several notable strengths. The motivation is strong and well grounded in prior work on NLI artifacts. The method is simple enough to be adopted by others. The empirical patterns are coherent across SNLI and MultiNLI. The figures and qualitative examples are useful, especially the examples on page 12 showing that easy SNLI contradictions can include unrelated sentence pairs that look mislabeled or artifact-prone, while hard examples often require more nuanced interpretation. The comparison to Data Maps and AUM in the training-data filtering setting is also a worthwhile addition.

To improve the paper, I would strongly encourage the authors to do the following. First, substantially temper the benchmark-construction claims and explicitly frame the method as a diagnostic stratification of existing test sets based on model-dependent training dynamics. Second, add robustness analyses: multiple random seeds, alternative numbers of clusters, and perhaps a simpler clustering baseline such as k-means or thresholding on a subset of features. Third, provide a cleaner ablation of the eight features to show which components matter most, especially whether hypothesis-only dynamics are essential. Fourth, evaluate transfer to more diverse architectures or at least to a non-RoBERTa-like model family. Fifth, address the test-set-training issue more directly, either with a cross-validated variant or with a discussion of when such a procedure is acceptable. Sixth, expand the artifact analysis beyond the five heuristics used here, or at minimum narrow the claims to those heuristics. Seventh, quantify annotation quality in the hard and easy splits via human inspection on a sample; this would help disentangle “hard because subtle” from “hard because noisy.”

In summary, this is a thoughtful and potentially impactful paper on NLI evaluation, with a practical method and several compelling empirical observations. The main idea of using combined premise+hypothesis and hypothesis-only training dynamics to stratify examples is novel enough and empirically useful. However, the current presentation overclaims what the method establishes, especially regarding automatic construction of a better test set and model-agnostic reduction of spurious correlations. The reliance on supervised fitting on the test set is the biggest conceptual weakness, and the robustness analysis is not yet sufficient for strong conclusions. I would encourage revision toward a more careful framing and stronger validation. Under that framing, the work could become a valuable contribution to benchmark diagnostics and data-centric evaluation in NLI.
