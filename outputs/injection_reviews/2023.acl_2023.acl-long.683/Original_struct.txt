Summary: The paper introduces a new task setting for open-world product attribute mining under light supervision, where the goal is both to expand known seed attribute types and to discover entirely new attribute types from e-commerce product text. To support this setting, the authors construct a new dataset, WOAM, spanning four product categories with curated seed attributes and human-annotated test data containing many unseen types and values. The proposed method, Amacer, is a pipeline with: (i) syntax/POS-pattern-based candidate span generation, (ii) BERT-based representation learning using supervised contrastive learning on seed attributes, (iii) adaptive expansion of seed clusters plus DBSCAN for remaining spans, and (iv) two additional regularizers for new-attribute discovery: a self-supervised bullet-point co-occurrence contrastive loss and an unsupervised latent-attribute/topic-modeling-style reconstruction objective. Experiments show improvements over closed-world tagging baselines and OA-Mine-style open-world baselines on their proposed clustering-based evaluation, with especially notable gains on new attribute types and bullet-point descriptions.

Strengths: The paper tackles a practically relevant and underexplored problem setting: open-world attribute mining without requiring explicit names/descriptions of unseen target attributes at inference time.; The dataset contribution appears meaningful. WOAM includes richer product context than prior title-only datasets, and the test set contains a substantial proportion of unseen attribute types and values (Table 11, pages 13-14).; The method is modular and intuitively motivated. The pipeline in Figure 2 (page 4) clearly separates candidate generation, representation learning, and grouping.; The use of lightly supervised seed attributes combined with implicit signals from context is a sensible design for the stated problem setting.; Empirical results are consistently favorable relative to the chosen baselines, including gains from the proposed regularization terms and from the syntax-based candidate generation (Tables 3, 12-14).; The paper includes useful ablations/decompositions, especially performance on seed vs. new types and title vs. bullet-point text (Tables 4, 12, 13), which helps support the claimed benefits for open-world discovery.; The qualitative examples are informative: page 9 shows both successful extraction and characteristic failure modes, and Table 7 illustrates interpretable latent attributes.

Weaknesses: The evaluation protocol is nonstandard and somewhat weakly justified. The paper defines a 'pseudo-F1' by averaging Jaccard/ARI/NMI as a proxy for precision and combining it with recall; this metric lacks a clear theoretical grounding and may be hard to interpret or compare across works.; The comparison to baselines is not fully balanced. Several baselines are adapted to the authors' candidate spans or stripped-down variants, while stronger modern alternatives for open-set clustering, metric learning, or span extraction are absent.; The candidate generation stage is crucial, but its recall ceiling is limited and noisy. Table 5 shows low span-level precision/recall/F1, indicating that much of the end-to-end performance may be bottlenecked by heuristic span generation.; The method relies on multiple heuristics and hyperparameters (POS patterns, adaptive thresholding, DBSCAN settings, number of latent attributes), raising concerns about robustness and reproducibility across domains.; The paper claims discovery of new attribute types, but the output is unlabeled clusters rather than semantically named types; thus the practical meaning of 'discovering a new type' is somewhat weaker than true ontology induction.; The dataset construction involves human curation and heuristic sanitization of retailer profiles, but inter-annotator agreement and annotation consistency statistics are not reported for the test set or seed curation.; The latent-attribute component is interesting but under-validated: there is no strong analysis of sensitivity to K, collapse behavior, or whether the learned latent attributes correspond to gold attribute structure beyond anecdotal examples.

Methodological Flaws: There is a likely issue in the supervised contrastive loss formulation on page 4. The denominator is defined over Ns(i)=Is\Ps(i), which appears to include the anchor i itself unless explicitly excluded. Standard supervised contrastive objectives exclude the anchor from the denominator; including it changes the optimization and may be unintended.; The adaptive expansion threshold in Eq. (2) on page 5 appears scientifically questionable. It uses the average pairwise cosine similarity within a seed set as a threshold after multiplying by δ, but cosine similarity can vary substantially with seed-set size, heterogeneity, and annotation granularity. There is no derivation or robustness analysis showing this threshold is calibrated or comparable across attributes.; The self-supervised heuristic in Section 6.1 assumes spans from the same bullet point should be more similar than spans from different bullet points of the same product. This assumption is often false in product descriptions, where a bullet may mention multiple unrelated attributes and different bullets may discuss the same attribute. The paper acknowledges noise but does not quantify when this heuristic helps versus hurts.; The unsupervised latent-attribute model reconstructs products as bags of candidate spans, but candidate spans themselves are generated heuristically and contain substantial noise. This creates a circular dependency: noisy candidates define the latent-variable training signal, yet the paper does not analyze how sensitive the latent model is to candidate noise.; The empirical validity of the main metric is questionable. Treating the average of Jaccard, ARI, and NMI as 'pseudo precision' and then computing F1 with recall is ad hoc; these clustering metrics are not precision measures and have different scales/behaviors. Conclusions based primarily on this derived F1 should therefore be interpreted cautiously.; The paper reports statistical significance for the best score in Table 3, but the testing protocol is not described in sufficient detail (e.g., what are the independent samples for the t-test, across seeds or categories). With only four product categories, significance claims may be fragile.; The claim of discovering values from 39% of new types is weaker than it sounds because recall over new types depends on the clustering/evaluation setup and candidate coverage; the paper does not provide a direct type-discovery metric or a rigorous definition of when a new type is considered discovered.; There is limited evidence that improvements stem from better open-world semantic discovery rather than from better candidate generation and clustering heuristics. Although Table 14 partially controls for candidate spans, the overall pipeline still conflates extraction quality, representation quality, and clustering quality.
