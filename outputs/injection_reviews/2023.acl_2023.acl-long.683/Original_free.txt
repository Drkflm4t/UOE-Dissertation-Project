This paper introduces a new task setting for product attribute mining in e-commerce: lightly supervised open-world attribute mining, where the goal is both to expand the value vocabulary of known seed attribute types and to discover values of previously unseen attribute types without requiring explicit type descriptions at inference time. To support this setting, the authors construct a new dataset, WOAM, spanning four Amazon product categories and including titles plus bullet-point descriptions. They propose Amacer, a pipeline that combines syntax-based candidate span generation, supervised contrastive learning from a curated seed attribute set, adaptive expansion for known attributes, DBSCAN-based clustering for additional coverage, and two forms of weak regularization for unseen attributes: a self-supervised bullet-point co-occurrence objective and an unsupervised latent-attribute reconstruction objective inspired by neural topic models. Experiments show consistent gains over closed-world tagging baselines and OA-Mine-style open-world baselines, with especially notable improvements on new attribute types.

Overall, I found the paper interesting, timely, and practically motivated. The problem formulation is meaningful: in realistic e-commerce settings, attribute inventories are incomplete and evolving, while exhaustive annotation is expensive and brittle. The paper makes a credible case that existing closed-world extraction and even zero-shot extraction settings do not fully capture the operational need to discover new attributes automatically. The proposed task therefore fills a useful gap between fully supervised extraction and unconstrained open information extraction. I also appreciate that the authors explicitly target richer product context beyond titles; the statistics in Table 1 and Table 9, together with the discussion on pages 2–3 and Appendix A, make a convincing argument that bullet points contain substantially more unseen attribute types and values and are therefore a more realistic but harder benchmark.

The strongest contribution is probably the task/dataset framing rather than the modeling novelty alone. WOAM appears thoughtfully constructed. The hybrid seed-set creation process—automatic sanitization of retailer-provided attributes followed by lightweight human curation—is sensible and practically relevant. The dataset statistics on page 13 are useful: 36 seed attribute types overall, 66 test attribute types with 42.4% unseen, and 86.9% unseen values. These numbers support the claim that the benchmark is genuinely open-world. I also appreciated the qualitative comparison to OA-Mine’s noisier seed attributes in Table 10. If the released data and code are indeed complete and usable, this could be a valuable benchmark for future work.

Methodologically, the paper is clear and reasonably well organized. Figure 2 on page 4 gives a good overview of the pipeline. The syntax-oriented candidate generation step is simple but well motivated: unlike segmentation-based approaches, it is better aligned with sparse attribute mentions in bullet points and avoids over-fragmentation. The supervised contrastive objective for seed expansion is standard but appropriate. The adaptive expansion stage for known attributes is also intuitive and arguably better matched to the task than applying a single clustering algorithm to everything. The most novel part is Section 6: using bullet-point-local co-occurrence as a self-supervised signal and introducing latent attributes via a neural topic-model-like reconstruction objective. This is a reasonable way to inject weak semantic structure for unseen attributes, and the decomposition in Table 12 suggests that these regularizers help disproportionately on new types, which is exactly the intended effect.

The empirical results are promising. In Table 3, Amacer improves over the strongest baselines by a substantial margin in both exact and partial pseudo-F1. The decomposed results in Table 12 are particularly important: the gain on new attribute types is much larger than on seed types, which supports the paper’s central claim. The title-versus-bullet-point breakdown in Table 13 is also informative and highlights the difficulty of the richer setting. The gold-span analysis in Table 14 is a nice diagnostic showing that representation learning and grouping still matter even when span extraction is removed from the equation. The qualitative examples on pages 8–9 and Table 15 are also helpful; they show both the strengths of the approach and the remaining noise.

That said, I have several concerns that limit my enthusiasm.

My main concern is evaluation. The paper evaluates an extraction-and-clustering task using Jaccard, ARI, NMI, and recall, then defines a “pseudo precision” as the average of Jaccard/ARI/NMI and derives a pseudo-F1 from that average and recall. This is unconventional and somewhat hard to interpret. These clustering metrics capture different properties and are not precision measures in the usual sense; averaging them and combining with recall into a single F1-like score lacks a strong theoretical justification. Because the paper’s headline improvements are reported in this pseudo-F1, the practical meaning of a “12 F1” gain is unclear. The paper does include span-level extraction metrics in Table 5, but those ignore type assignment, while the clustering metrics ignore some extraction nuances. I would have liked a more principled end-to-end metric, or at least a stronger justification for the chosen aggregate. For example, pairwise precision/recall/F1 over span pairs in the same predicted/gold cluster, B-cubed metrics, or a direct cluster-aware extraction metric might be more standard and interpretable. At minimum, the paper should report the main conclusions under multiple aggregation schemes to show robustness.

Relatedly, the exact-match and partial-match span evaluation protocol is only partially satisfying for this task. The partial-match criterion of “more than half of the span falls into a gold value” is asymmetric and may behave oddly for long or nested spans. Since candidate generation is a major component of the method, evaluation sensitivity to boundary choices matters. More discussion or ablation on this would strengthen the paper.

A second concern is the fairness and informativeness of the baselines. The paper compares against closed-world tagging models, OA-Mine, and several internal ablations. However, many of the gains seem to come from the candidate generation stage rather than the representation learning alone. The authors do acknowledge this and include OA-Mine* and Amacer* variants, which is good, but the baseline space still feels somewhat narrow. In particular, once candidate spans are generated, the task becomes close to clustering or metric learning over phrases. It would be useful to compare against stronger modern embedding baselines, such as Sentence-BERT or SimCSE-style phrase embeddings with simple clustering, or even a nearest-neighbor propagation baseline from seed values. Likewise, for the latent-attribute component, a simpler unsupervised regularizer or pseudo-labeling baseline would help isolate whether the proposed topic-model-inspired machinery is necessary. The current comparisons mostly show that the full pipeline is better than OA-Mine-style segmentation plus older losses, but not necessarily that each modeling choice is close to optimal.

A third concern is that the candidate generation step may encode substantial task-specific prior knowledge while also constraining the problem in ways that are not fully examined. The POS-pattern approach is elegant and efficient, but it depends on product-profile-derived patterns and English POS tagging quality. More importantly, it may systematically miss attributes expressed in less canonical syntactic forms, especially longer relational or compositional phrases. The paper acknowledges noise and some limitations, but I would like more quantitative analysis of candidate recall by attribute type, especially for unseen types. Table 5 shows low span precision and modest recall overall, but it is hard to tell whether the bottleneck for new attributes is candidate omission or clustering failure. Since the paper’s open-world claim depends critically on discovering unseen types, a candidate-recall analysis would be very valuable.

A fourth issue is that the latent-attribute formulation is interesting but under-validated. The paper states that this component contributes around 70% of the regularization improvement, but I did not see a clean ablation separating self-supervised contrastive regularization from the unsupervised latent-attribute objective in the main tables. A full ablation with Amacer minus self-supervision only, minus latent attributes only, and minus both would make the contribution much clearer. Similarly, the choice of K=50 latent attributes is only briefly mentioned in Appendix C; sensitivity to K, as well as evidence of stability across runs, would be useful. The qualitative examples in Table 7 are encouraging, but the paper also notes topic collapse. If collapse is common, it raises questions about how much effective signal the latent attributes are really providing.

I also have some concerns about generality and scope. The dataset covers only four product categories, all from Amazon, and all in English. While this is already useful, the paper occasionally makes broader claims about practical open-world attribute mining. It would be better to temper those claims or provide stronger evidence of transfer across categories. For example, does the same POS-pattern inventory generalize to a held-out category? How much human curation is needed when moving to a new domain? The reported curation time of under 40 minutes per category is appealing, but more detail on annotator expertise and reproducibility would help. Similarly, the seed-set construction uses retailer profiles and frequency heuristics; this may work well on Amazon-like platforms but less well elsewhere.

Another weakness is that the paper does not fully disentangle extraction from clustering. The pipeline is modular, but the reported end-to-end gains combine improvements from candidate generation, embedding learning, adaptive expansion, and DBSCAN. Some ablations are present, but a more systematic error decomposition would improve scientific clarity. For instance: what fraction of missed new-type values are absent from candidates, embedded near wrong seed types, or filtered as DBSCAN noise? The qualitative example on page 9 suggests that many new-type values are extracted but absorbed into existing clusters, which is an important failure mode. A quantitative breakdown of such errors would help guide future work.

There are also some smaller technical and presentation issues. The notation is mostly understandable, but a few definitions could be cleaner. In the supervised contrastive loss, the denominator appears to sum over negatives only, whereas standard supervised contrastive formulations often include positives and exclude the anchor; this may be intentional, but the exact choice should be clarified. The adaptive threshold in Eq. (2) is intuitive, but because cosine similarity can vary substantially across attributes with different intra-class diversity, it would be useful to discuss whether this threshold is robust for broad versus narrow attributes. The paper reports statistical significance in Table 3, but details of the test setup are missing from the main text. Since clustering outcomes can vary with random seeds and DBSCAN sensitivity, reporting variance across runs would strengthen confidence. Finally, the paper would benefit from some language polishing; there are a number of grammatical issues and awkward phrasings throughout.

Despite these concerns, I think the paper has real merit. The problem is important, the dataset appears valuable, and the method is sensible and reasonably effective. The work is especially compelling as a benchmark-and-framework paper for a more realistic weakly supervised open-world extraction setting. My main hesitation is that the evaluation methodology is not yet strong enough for a top-tier venue, and the empirical validation of the novel regularization components could be more rigorous.

To strengthen the paper, I would recommend the following. First, improve the evaluation section substantially: either adopt a more standard cluster-aware metric suite or at least supplement the current pseudo-F1 with alternative end-to-end metrics and show that conclusions are unchanged. Second, add cleaner ablations isolating self-supervised regularization and latent-attribute regularization, plus sensitivity analyses for K, DBSCAN parameters, and the adaptive expansion threshold. Third, provide candidate-span recall analysis, especially on unseen attribute types, to clarify where the main bottlenecks lie. Fourth, compare against a few stronger embedding/clustering baselines beyond OA-Mine variants. Fifth, if possible, include some evidence of cross-category robustness or transfer.

In summary, this is a solid and practically motivated paper with a valuable new task setting and dataset, plus a reasonable method that performs well empirically. The strongest aspects are the benchmark construction, the realistic open-world framing, and the consistent gains on unseen attribute types. The main weaknesses are the unconventional evaluation metric, somewhat limited baseline coverage, and insufficient isolation of the novel regularization components. I would view this as a borderline-to-positive submission: promising and potentially impactful, but needing stronger empirical rigor and clearer evaluation to fully justify acceptance at a top-tier conference.
