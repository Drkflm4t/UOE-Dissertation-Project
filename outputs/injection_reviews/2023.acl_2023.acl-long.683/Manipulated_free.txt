This paper tackles an important and practically motivated problem: open-world product attribute mining under limited supervision. Rather than assuming a fixed ontology of attributes or requiring explicit descriptions of unseen target attributes at inference time, the paper asks the model to both expand known seed attribute types and discover entirely new attribute types from product text. This is a meaningful step beyond standard closed-world extraction and even beyond zero-shot attribute extraction. The setting is well motivated for e-commerce, where attribute vocabularies evolve quickly and exhaustive annotation is expensive and brittle.

The paper’s main contributions are threefold and, in my view, all substantial. First, it formulates a realistic lightly supervised open-world attribute mining task. Second, it introduces a new dataset, WOAM, with richer product context than prior work by including both titles and bullet-point descriptions, and with a seed set constructed through a hybrid sanitization-plus-curation process. Third, it proposes Amacer, a pipeline that combines syntax-based candidate generation, supervised contrastive learning from seed attributes, adaptive expansion for known attributes, DBSCAN-based discovery for remaining candidates, and two additional regularizers aimed at improving discovery of unseen attributes: a self-supervised bullet-point co-occurrence objective and an unsupervised latent-attribute reconstruction objective inspired by neural topic models.

Overall, I found the paper technically sound, clearly motivated, and empirically convincing. The problem formulation is novel relative to the cited literature, and the method is thoughtfully designed around the constraints of the setting rather than being a generic clustering baseline with minor modifications. The paper also does a good job of explaining why standard alternatives are insufficient: closed-world tagging cannot discover new types; segmentation-based open-world methods are less suitable for long bullet-point descriptions; and direct supervision is unavailable for unseen attributes, motivating the use of implicit contextual signals.

A major strength is the dataset design. The statistics in Table 1 and Table 11 make clear that the benchmark is genuinely challenging: 42.4% of test attribute types are unseen from the seed set overall, and 86.9% of test values are unseen; bullet points contain substantially more new types and values than titles. This is a much more realistic and difficult setting than title-only extraction. I also appreciated the discussion in Appendix A/B about why raw retailer profiles are too sparse and noisy to serve directly as supervision, and how the authors construct a higher-quality seed set with modest human effort. The reported curation time of under 40 minutes per category is especially useful for understanding the practical cost model.

The method itself is well structured. The syntax-oriented candidate generation stage is simple but well justified. In this setting, simplicity is a virtue: the POS-pattern approach appears to strike a good balance between recall, quality, efficiency, and portability. The examples in Table 2 and the discussion around bullet-point density make the design choice intuitive. The representation learning stage is also sensible. Using supervised contrastive learning over seed values is a natural way to shape an attribute-aware embedding space, and the choice to simplify prior OA-Mine losses into an in-batch contrastive objective seems effective. The grouping stage is another strong point: separating expansion of existing seed attributes from discovery of new clusters is more aligned with the task than applying a single off-the-shelf clustering algorithm to everything.

The most interesting technical contribution is Section 6. The self-supervised regularization based on bullet-point locality is a clever heuristic: it is noisy, but plausible, and the paper is appropriately modest in presenting it as weak regularization rather than a primary supervision source. The latent-attribute module is also a nice idea. Framing products as mixtures over latent attributes and reconstructing observed candidate spans gives the model a way to exploit corpus-level structure even when explicit labels are absent. This component is particularly well matched to the open-world discovery goal.

The empirical results are strong. In Table 3, Amacer achieves the best overall performance among all compared systems, with especially notable gains over both closed-world tagging baselines and the most relevant open-world baseline OA-Mine. The improvements are not marginal: the paper reports roughly 10–12 F1 gains depending on exact vs partial matching and baseline choice. The decomposition in Table 12 is particularly compelling: the gains on new attribute types are large, which is exactly where the proposed regularization should matter most. The comparison between Amacer-R and Amacer supports the claim that the additional implicit-signal objectives help unseen-type discovery. Table 14, where gold spans are used as candidates, is also informative because it isolates the representation/grouping quality and still shows a clear advantage for the proposed method.

I also appreciated the qualitative analysis. The examples on page 9 and Table 15 help make the task concrete. The model’s ability to expand seed attributes such as Flavor Profile into many plausible long-tail expressions is impressive and practically valuable. The latent-attribute examples in Table 7 are also useful for interpreting what the unsupervised component is learning. Even the failure case in Table 6 is informative: the model often extracts relevant spans for unseen concepts like region of origin but assigns them to existing clusters, which suggests partial semantic understanding even when full open-world discovery remains difficult.

That said, there are several limitations and questions that should be addressed, though none of them undermine the overall contribution.

My main concern is evaluation methodology. The paper uses clustering metrics such as Jaccard, ARI, NMI, and recall, then defines a “pseudo precision” as the average of Jaccard/ARI/NMI and derives a pseudo-F1 from that average and recall. I understand the motivation, but this aggregate metric is somewhat ad hoc and may be hard to interpret or compare across papers. Since the paper’s main claims rely heavily on this F1, it would be helpful to justify more carefully why averaging these three clustering metrics is meaningful, and whether conclusions are robust under alternative summaries. The paper does report the underlying metrics, which is good, but the headline metric remains unconventional.

Relatedly, the evaluation protocol depends strongly on candidate span generation. Table 5 shows that exact span extraction quality is still fairly low, with precision below 34 and exact F1 below 37 for all models. The paper is transparent about this, which I appreciate, but it means that a substantial portion of the end-to-end challenge remains unresolved at the span proposal stage. Since Amacer’s strongest gains partly come from better candidate generation than segmentation-based baselines, it would be useful to see a more direct analysis of candidate recall/precision before clustering, perhaps broken down by titles vs bullet points and by seed vs new types. Table 14 partially addresses this by using gold spans, but a dedicated candidate-generation evaluation would strengthen the paper further.

A second concern is the fairness and scope of the baseline comparisons. OA-Mine is the most relevant prior work, but some comparisons mix multiple changes at once: candidate generation, contrastive objective, grouping strategy, and regularization. The paper does include OA-Mine*, Amacer*, and Amacer-R variants, which helps, but I still think the ablation story could be made more systematic. For example, it would be useful to isolate the effect of adaptive expansion independently from the syntax-based candidates and independently from the new representation learning objective. Likewise, the latent-attribute regularizer is said to contribute around 70% of the regularization gain, but I did not see a clean table separating self-supervised-only vs latent-only vs both in the main text.

A third issue is generality. The dataset covers four categories: tea, vitamins, sofas, and phone cases. This is a reasonable starting point and already spans multiple domains, but the method relies on POS patterns extracted from profiles and on English POS tagging quality. The limitations section acknowledges this. I would have liked a stronger discussion of how category-specific the learned patterns are, whether patterns transfer across categories, and how the approach might behave in noisier or more multilingual settings. Similarly, the seed-set construction includes human curation and granularity adjustment; while the reported effort is modest, some readers may wonder how reproducible these decisions are across organizations or marketplaces.

There are also some modeling details that deserve clarification. In Section 4, the supervised contrastive loss denominator appears to sum over negatives only, whereas standard supervised contrastive formulations often include positives and negatives except the anchor itself; if this is intentional, the paper should explain the choice more explicitly. In Section 5, the adaptive threshold uses average pairwise cosine similarity within a support set multiplied by a relaxation factor; intuitively this makes sense, but I would like more discussion of how stable this threshold is for attributes with very small or semantically diverse seed sets. Since the median number of seed values per type can be low in some categories, threshold sensitivity could matter. Likewise, DBSCAN hyperparameters are fixed globally; some comment on robustness across categories would be helpful.

Another limitation is that the discovered new clusters are not named. This is understandable given the task formulation and evaluation setup, but from an application perspective, unnamed clusters may limit immediate usability. The paper evaluates clustering quality rather than type induction with labels, which is reasonable, yet a short discussion of how one might assign interpretable names to discovered clusters would improve the practical picture.

The paper is generally well written, but there are a few places where exposition could be tightened. Some terminology could be made more precise, especially around “attribute-aware embedding space” and “latent attributes,” to distinguish clearly between induced latent factors and actual semantic attribute types. There are also minor grammatical issues throughout, though nothing that impedes understanding. The figures are helpful; in particular, Figure 1 on page 1 clearly illustrates the task setting, and Figure 2 on page 4 gives a good overview of the pipeline.

Despite the above concerns, my overall assessment is positive. The paper addresses a genuinely important problem that is underexplored, introduces a useful benchmark, and proposes a coherent method that is well aligned with the task’s constraints. The empirical gains are substantial, especially on unseen attribute types, and the analysis is thoughtful. I believe this work would be of interest to both the information extraction and applied NLP/data mining communities, particularly those working on e-commerce product understanding and open-world extraction.

For improvement, I would encourage the authors to add a more principled discussion of the evaluation metric, include a cleaner ablation table for the two regularization terms and adaptive expansion, and provide explicit candidate-generation quality metrics. If space permits, some robustness analysis over DBSCAN and threshold hyperparameters would also be valuable. A brief discussion of cluster naming or downstream integration into product catalogs would further strengthen the practical impact.

In summary, this is a strong paper with clear novelty in task formulation, a valuable dataset contribution, and a well-motivated method that delivers convincing gains. I would recommend acceptance.
