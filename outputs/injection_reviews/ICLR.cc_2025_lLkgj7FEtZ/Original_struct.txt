Summary: This paper studies privacy-preserving activation steering for LLM alignment. It proposes PSA, a simple method that computes mean steering vectors from paired positive/negative demonstrations, clips per-example activation differences, and adds Gaussian noise to obtain differentially private steering vectors. These private vectors are then injected at selected layers during inference, yielding formal DP guarantees for arbitrarily many downstream queries by post-processing. The paper evaluates PSA on seven alignment-related behaviors across several open-source instruction-tuned LLMs (Llama-2, Mistral, Gemma, Qwen families), comparing against zero-shot, mean steering, and PCA steering. Results show PSA generally preserves much of the alignment utility and open-ended generation quality while maintaining similar MMLU performance. The paper also introduces a canary-based membership inference attack tailored to activation steering and shows empirically reduced leakage under PSA relative to non-private steering.

Strengths: Timely and novel problem formulation: bringing differential privacy to inference-time activation steering is an important and underexplored direction.; Method is simple, easy to implement, and computationally lightweight compared with DP fine-tuning; the offline privatization of steering vectors is practically appealing.; Clear privacy argument: clipping plus Gaussian mechanism per selected layer, followed by composition and post-processing, gives a straightforward formal DP guarantee for unlimited future queries.; Broad empirical coverage: seven alignment behaviors, multiple model families, and model sizes from 0.5B to 7B.; Utility results are reasonably strong: PSA is usually close to non-private mean steering and often clearly better than zero-shot on alignment benchmarks.; The paper evaluates not only multiple-choice alignment accuracy but also open-ended generation quality and general capability retention (MMLU), which gives a more complete utility picture.; The proposed canary-based MIA for activation steering is a useful contribution in its own right and provides empirical auditing beyond the formal DP theorem.; Ablations on noise scale and clipping threshold help clarify the privacy-utility tradeoff.

Weaknesses: The technical novelty of the core mechanism is limited: PSA is essentially mean steering with clipping and Gaussian noise, plus a basic composition analysis.; The privacy accounting is fairly coarse. The paper uses basic composition over layers and does not exploit tighter accounting; this is acceptable for small |S| but weakens the theoretical contribution.; The noise calibration and notation are somewhat under-specified/inconsistent. Algorithm 1 lists a fixed sigma expression that appears independent of clipping threshold and dimensionality, while the text motivates clipping via sensitivity control; this deserves a more careful derivation and clearer assumptions.; Experimental privacy evaluation is narrow: the MIA is only tested on the hallucination dataset and two 7B models, so it is unclear how broadly the empirical privacy conclusions generalize.; The MIA is highly specialized and threshold-based, relying on synthetic canaries and repeated sampling; it is useful for auditing but may not reflect realistic adversaries or broader leakage modes.; Evaluation of open-ended quality relies on GPT-4 judging with behavior-specific prompts, which is common but noisy; no human evaluation or inter-rater robustness is provided.; Some reported qualitative examples suggest steering can still produce clearly false content under the hallucination setting, making interpretation of the behavior labels/results somewhat confusing.; The paper does not compare against stronger or more recent steering methods beyond mean/PCA steering, limiting conclusions about PSA’s applicability to the broader activation-editing landscape.; Hyperparameter selection for clipping thresholds is said to use public data or tuning, but the practical sensitivity of results to this choice and the availability of public calibration data are not deeply examined.

Soundness Issues: The main DP claim is plausible, but the derivation of sensitivity/noise scale should be stated more rigorously. In particular, the neighboring relation, whether one paired demonstration changes, and the exact sensitivity after clipping/averaging should be explicitly proved.; Algorithm 1 appears to add isotropic Gaussian noise with scalar variance notation N(0, sigma^2) to a vector output; the dimensional form should be clarified.; The implementation section says noisy vectors are generated with standard deviation 0.02 and then theoretical epsilon is inferred, whereas Algorithm 1 presents sigma as determined by epsilon and delta. This inversion is fine experimentally, but the presentation should more clearly distinguish target-epsilon vs fixed-noise regimes.; The empirical epsilon computed from FPR/FNR of the custom MIA is only an audit proxy and should not be over-interpreted as a direct estimate of the true privacy loss of the full mechanism.; MMLU evaluation follows a reduced A/B reformulation from prior work rather than standard MMLU accuracy, so claims about preserving general reasoning should be interpreted cautiously.
