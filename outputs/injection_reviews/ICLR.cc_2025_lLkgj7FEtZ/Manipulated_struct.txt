Summary: The paper studies privacy-preserving activation steering for LLM alignment and proposes PSA, a simple method that computes mean steering vectors from paired positive/negative demonstrations, clips per-example activation differences, and adds Gaussian noise to obtain differentially private steering vectors. By applying these privatized vectors at inference time, the method provides formal DP guarantees for infinitely many downstream queries via post-processing. Empirically, the paper evaluates PSA on seven alignment-related behaviors across four open-source instruction-tuned LLM families and multiple sizes, comparing against zero-shot, mean steering, and PCA steering. The results show that PSA usually preserves much of the utility of non-private steering on behavioral MCQ accuracy, open-ended generation judged by GPT-4, and MMLU, while also reducing privacy leakage under a newly proposed membership inference attack tailored to activation editing.

Strengths: Timely and novel problem formulation: the paper appears to be the first to study differential privacy for activation-editing-based LLM alignment, addressing an important and previously underexplored privacy risk.; Method is simple, clear, and practically appealing: clipping activation-difference vectors and adding Gaussian noise is easy to implement and compatible with existing steering pipelines.; The privacy argument is conceptually clean: privatize the steering vectors once offline, then obtain privacy for arbitrarily many future generations by post-processing, which is a strong and useful deployment property.; Broad empirical coverage: experiments span seven alignment behaviors, four model families, and multiple model sizes, giving a reasonably comprehensive picture of utility/privacy tradeoffs.; Utility results are generally strong: PSA often remains close to non-private mean steering and consistently improves over zero-shot on many tasks, suggesting privacy can be added at modest cost.; The paper evaluates not only alignment metrics but also open-ended generation quality and general capabilities (MMLU), which strengthens the practical relevance of the study.; The proposed MIA tailored to activation steering is a meaningful contribution on its own, especially because it operates from generated text only and does not require logits/probabilities.; Empirical privacy results support the theoretical motivation: PSA substantially reduces attack success and yields empirical privacy estimates below the theoretical bounds, which is reassuring.; The paper is well organized and easy to follow, with clear algorithms, figures, and appendices including ablations and qualitative examples.

Weaknesses: The DP accounting is fairly basic and may be somewhat loose; the paper mainly uses per-layer composition and fixed noise choices rather than a more systematic privacy-utility optimization.; The empirical privacy evaluation is limited to a canary-based MIA on one dataset and two models, so it does not fully characterize privacy leakage across behaviors, prompts, or stronger adversaries.; Some evaluation choices rely heavily on GPT-4-as-a-judge and synthetic behavioral benchmarks, which may not fully capture real-world alignment quality or downstream usefulness.; The comparison set is somewhat narrow: PSA is mainly compared to mean steering and PCA steering, while more recent or stronger steering/editing baselines are not extensively evaluated.; Hyperparameter selection for clipping thresholds and selected layers is only briefly discussed, and the dependence on public data or tuning choices could affect reproducibility and fairness.; The paper focuses on monolingual and relatively stylized paired demonstrations, leaving open how well the method transfers to richer, noisier, or more realistic private datasets.

Methodological Flaws: There is a notable issue in the stated Gaussian mechanism calibration. Equation (3) and Algorithm 1 specify adding noise N(0, sigma^2) with sigma = 2*sqrt(2 ln(1.25/delta)) / (n epsilon), but the mechanism should scale with the clipping threshold after sensitivity analysis unless the normalization fully absorbs it. The presentation suggests this dependence is removed by dividing by C_l, but the derivation is not shown carefully, making the proof sketch incomplete and easy to misinterpret.; The privacy proof relies on basic composition over selected layers and post-processing, which is valid at a high level, but the paper does not rigorously formalize adjacency for paired demonstrations or discuss whether neighboring datasets differ in one triplet or one positive/negative completion. This matters for sensitivity and should be stated more precisely.; The empirical epsilon computation from FPR/FNR is only an audit-style estimate under a specific attack family, not a general privacy guarantee. The paper mostly phrases this appropriately, but readers could over-interpret empirical epsilon as a true privacy parameter; this distinction should be emphasized more strongly.; The MIA uses synthetic canaries inserted into demonstrations and thresholded generation counts. This is a reasonable audit, but it may overstate or understate realistic leakage depending on canary design and decoding temperature; thus the empirical privacy conclusions should be interpreted as indicative rather than definitive.; Open-ended evaluation has some inconsistencies in interpretation because lower scores are better for some behaviors (e.g., hallucination, sycophancy) while higher is better for others (e.g., refusal, corrigibility). The paper reports raw scores but does not always normalize or aggregate them, making cross-behavior comparisons less straightforward.; There appears to be at least one likely typo or anomaly in Appendix Table 21, where the zero-shot corrigibility score is listed as 74.74 despite the stated 0-10 scale. This does not undermine the main claims but indicates some care is needed in checking reported tables.
