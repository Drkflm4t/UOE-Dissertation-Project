Summary: This paper studies privacy-preserving activation steering for LLM alignment. It proposes PSA, a simple method that computes mean steering vectors from paired positive/negative demonstrations, clips and normalizes per-example activation differences, and adds Gaussian noise to obtain differentially private steering vectors. These private vectors are then reused at inference time to steer selected layers, yielding formal DP guarantees for infinitely many downstream queries by post-processing. Empirically, the paper evaluates PSA on seven alignment-related behaviors across several open-source instruction-tuned LLMs and compares against zero-shot, mean steering, and PCA-based steering. Results show PSA usually matches non-private steering reasonably closely while outperforming zero-shot on many tasks, with limited degradation on MMLU. The paper also introduces a canary-based membership inference attack tailored to activation steering and shows PSA reduces empirical leakage relative to non-private steering.

Strengths: Addresses a novel and important problem: privacy risks in activation editing/steering have been largely unexplored compared with fine-tuning and in-context learning.; Method is simple, easy to implement, and conceptually clean: clip per-example activation differences and add calibrated Gaussian noise to the steering vector.; Provides a clear formal privacy argument that private steering vectors can be computed once offline and then reused for arbitrarily many queries without additional privacy cost.; Empirical evaluation is fairly broad in model families and behaviors, covering seven alignment datasets and multiple open-source LLMs.; Includes utility checks beyond the target alignment tasks, notably open-ended generation judged by GPT-4 and MMLU for general capabilities.; Introduces a tailored canary-based membership inference attack for activation steering, which is a useful contribution for privacy auditing in this setting.; Ablation studies on noise scale and clipping threshold help clarify the privacy-utility tradeoff.

Weaknesses: The core algorithm is technically straightforward and largely an application of the Gaussian mechanism to mean steering, so the methodological novelty is moderate.; The privacy accounting is somewhat simplified: the paper mainly uses basic composition over selected layers and does not deeply analyze tighter accounting or alternative neighboring relations.; Experimental privacy evaluation is limited to one main dataset setting (hallucination) and two models for the MIA, so it is unclear how broadly the empirical privacy findings generalize.; The choice of fixed noise standard deviation in experiments is somewhat ad hoc relative to the formal DP presentation; the mapping between practical hyperparameters and reported epsilon could be clearer.; Open-ended evaluation relies on GPT-4 as judge, which is common but noisy and potentially biased; human evaluation is absent.; Comparisons are restricted to mean steering and PCA steering, without benchmarking against more recent or stronger steering/editing methods.; Some claims of minimal utility loss are only partially supported, since degradation can be noticeable on certain tasks/models and the strongest results appear mainly for larger models.; The paper focuses on a narrow set of alignment axes and monolingual settings, limiting external validity.

Soundness Issues: The DP mechanism itself appears broadly sound under the stated setup: clipping bounds sensitivity and Gaussian noise is added before release, after which inference-time use is post-processing. However, the exposition is somewhat loose about constants and assumptions, and the exact sensitivity derivation could be presented more rigorously.; The paper assumes privacy with respect to the demonstration dataset used to form steering vectors, but does not deeply discuss whether auxiliary public data, layer selection, clipping thresholds, or hyperparameter tuning could themselves leak information if chosen adaptively from private data.; The empirical MIA is interesting but threshold-based and specialized; reduced attack success supports privacy claims but does not strongly validate robustness against stronger adversaries.; Utility evaluation is generally reasonable, but some conclusions are based on relatively limited metrics: multiple-choice behavioral accuracy, GPT-4 judgments, and MMLU probability scores. Stronger downstream evaluations would improve confidence.; The claim that larger models have a better privacy-utility tradeoff is plausible and supported by one scaling plot, but evidence is limited to a single model family/dataset combination.
