This paper studies privacy-preserving activation steering for LLM alignment. The core idea is simple and appealing: compute standard mean steering vectors from paired positive/negative demonstrations, clip and normalize per-example activation differences, and add Gaussian noise so that the released steering vectors satisfy differential privacy. The resulting method, PSA, is then used at inference time exactly like ordinary activation editing, with the important benefit that once the private steering vectors are released, arbitrarily many downstream generations incur no additional privacy cost by post-processing. The paper also proposes what the authors describe as the first membership inference attack tailored to activation steering, based only on generated text rather than token probabilities, and evaluates both utility and empirical privacy across several alignment behaviors and multiple open-source LLM families.

Overall, I find the paper timely, well motivated, and potentially impactful. Activation steering is increasingly used as a lightweight alternative to fine-tuning, and the privacy implications of constructing steering vectors from sensitive demonstrations are indeed underexplored. The paper’s main conceptual contribution is straightforward but meaningful: privatize the steering vectors themselves rather than the model training process. This is a natural fit for differential privacy, and the offline nature of the mechanism is practically attractive. The empirical scope is also fairly broad: seven behavioral datasets, several model families, multiple utility metrics, and an empirical privacy audit. I think the paper makes a credible case that privacy for activation editing is an important problem and that a simple DP mechanism can work surprisingly well.

The strongest aspect of the paper is the clarity of the privacy framing. The setup in Sections 3 and 4 is easy to follow, and Figure 1 on page 2 and Figure 2 on page 5 communicate the two-phase workflow effectively: first generate private steering vectors from paired demonstrations, then use them for unlimited private generation. The post-processing argument is standard but important here, and the paper correctly emphasizes that the privacy budget is paid once when releasing the steering vectors, not per query. This is a genuine practical advantage over many inference-time privacy mechanisms. I also appreciated that the paper explicitly discusses why activation editing may still leak information even though it is not conventional fine-tuning.

The empirical evaluation on alignment benchmarks is encouraging. Figure 3 on page 7 shows that PSA generally tracks non-private mean steering closely and usually remains clearly above zero-shot performance for Llama-2, Mistral, and Qwen across the seven behaviors. The scaling result in Figure 4 on page 9 is also interesting: the privacy-utility gap appears to shrink with model size, at least on the Refusal dataset for the Qwen family. If robust, this is an important practical message, since many real deployments will use larger instruction-tuned models. The MMLU results in Table 5 on page 9 suggest only modest degradation in general capabilities, which helps support the claim that PSA does not catastrophically distort the model outside the target behavior.

That said, I have several concerns, some of them substantial.

My main concern is that the privacy accounting and mechanism specification are underdeveloped relative to the claims. The paper states in Algorithm 1 on page 5 that the private steering vector is computed as the average of clipped/scaled difference vectors plus Gaussian noise with standard deviation sigma = 2 sqrt(2 ln(1.25/delta)) / (n epsilon). This formula appears to assume sensitivity 2/n, presumably because neighboring datasets can differ in one paired demonstration and each clipped vector has norm at most 1. That is plausible, but the paper should derive this explicitly and carefully. As written, the notation is a bit sloppy: the Gaussian mechanism in Fact 2.1 on page 3 is written as adding N(0, sigma^2), which for a vector-valued output is ambiguous. Is the noise isotropic Gaussian N(0, sigma^2 I_d)? Since the steering vectors live in high-dimensional activation space, this matters. More importantly, the privacy guarantee is stated as (|S| epsilon, |S| delta)-DP by basic composition across layers, but the mechanism releases multiple high-dimensional vectors, one per selected layer. If the same example contributes to all selected layers, then composition is indeed needed, but the paper should be more explicit about whether the per-layer epsilon values in Table 2 are chosen independently or derived from a fixed total budget. Right now the presentation mixes “per-layer epsilon” and “total epsilon” in a way that is easy to misread.

Relatedly, the experimental privacy setup fixes the noise standard deviation to 0.02 and then reports the resulting theoretical epsilon values in Table 2 on page 7. This is a somewhat unusual presentation for a DP paper. Typically one fixes a target privacy budget and calibrates noise accordingly. Here, because sigma is fixed across datasets and models, epsilon varies with n and perhaps implicitly with clipping. That is not wrong, but it weakens the privacy-utility story because the comparisons are not made at matched privacy budgets. In particular, some datasets have epsilon around 2 while others are as high as 6.6 total, which is a much weaker guarantee. The paper repeatedly says PSA achieves DP “with minimal loss in performance,” but the utility numbers are aggregated across quite different privacy regimes. A stronger evaluation would sweep epsilon directly and compare utility as a function of privacy budget, ideally against alternative DP baselines.

A second major concern is that the empirical privacy attack, while novel and interesting, is narrow and somewhat stylized. The canary-based MIA in Section 6 inserts synthetic anchor-target pairs into demonstrations and then checks whether the steered model preferentially emits the target when prompted with the anchor. This is a reasonable first audit, but it is closer to a targeted canary extraction test than a general membership inference attack on natural demonstrations. The attack depends on specially constructed synthetic entities, repeated sampling at temperature 0.8, and threshold tuning that differs by model (tau = 40 for Llama-2 and 70 for Qwen, page 10). It demonstrates that non-private steering can encode and reveal synthetic associations, but it does not yet establish how vulnerable activation steering is to realistic MIAs on ordinary private data. The paper should be more careful in its claims here. Calling this “the first MIA for activation editing” is probably fair, but the broader implication that PSA protects against membership inference in realistic settings is less well supported.

The empirical epsilon computation in Section 6 also deserves more scrutiny. The paper uses FPR and FNR from the attack to derive an empirical epsilon, following prior auditing work. This is fine as an audit heuristic, but it is not a direct estimate of the true privacy loss of the mechanism; it is only with respect to a particular adversary and test. The paper does mention that theoretical bounds are conservative, but some of the wording risks overinterpretation. For example, saying that empirical epsilon is lower than theoretical epsilon “suggesting stronger protection with PSA in real-world settings” is too broad given the narrow attack family. It would be better to say that under this specific canary-based audit, the observed leakage is lower than the worst-case DP bound.

A third concern is the strength of the utility evaluation itself. The behavioral datasets are inherited from prior work and are useful for controlled steering experiments, but they are fairly artificial binary-choice tasks. The paper does include open-ended generation evaluation using GPT-4 as a judge, which is good, but Table 3 on page 8 raises questions. For some behaviors, the scores are extremely low across all methods, especially AI Coordination and Sycophancy, making it hard to interpret practical significance. Also, the paper does not report variance, confidence intervals, or significance tests anywhere in the main results. Since the differences between Mean Steering and PSA are often small, it is difficult to know whether they are meaningful or within run-to-run noise. This is especially important because the method itself is randomized. Were the reported PSA numbers averaged over multiple noise draws, or from a single sampled steering vector? The paper does not clearly say. If only one draw was used, the results may be unstable.

The choice of baselines is another weakness. The paper compares PSA mainly to zero-shot, mean steering, and PCA steering. That is a reasonable starting point, but for a privacy paper I would have liked at least one stronger privacy-aware baseline, even if adapted from adjacent settings. For example, a simple non-DP clipping-only baseline, or a mechanism that privatizes a lower-dimensional projection of the steering vectors, or a DP variant of PCA steering, would help isolate whether the proposed mean-based privatization is especially effective. The appendix does include clipping-only ablations on pages 20–21, but these are not integrated into the main comparison. More broadly, the paper’s claim of being the first DP activation editing method is plausible, but the empirical case would be stronger if it compared against more than two non-private steering methods.

There are also some methodological ambiguities. The paper says on page 6 that “the noisy vectors are generated by adding Gaussian noise with 0.02 standard deviation,” but because the vectors are normalized by clipping threshold and live in different layer dimensions across models, it is not obvious that a fixed sigma is comparable across settings. The clipping threshold itself is treated as a hyperparameter estimated from public data, but the main text does not specify how it was chosen in the reported experiments. The appendix ablation on pages 20–21 suggests utility is sensitive to clipping threshold, so this choice matters. Similarly, the selected layers are said to be the five middle layers (11–15) for 7B models in Table 2, but it is unclear whether the same relative layer positions are used for smaller models and all model families, and whether this was tuned per behavior. Since steering performance is known to depend strongly on layer choice, more detail is needed for reproducibility and for assessing fairness of comparisons.

I also found some of the qualitative examples a bit awkward. For instance, the hallucination example in Table 11 on page 19 appears to show both Mean Steering and PSA producing clearly false content, which is consistent with the benchmark’s framing but makes it hard to understand whether lower GPT-4 hallucination scores correspond to better factuality in a practical sense. More generally, because the behaviors include unusual axes like AI Coordination and Survival Instinct, the paper would benefit from a clearer discussion of which results should be interpreted as safety-relevant versus merely diagnostic. This matters because “alignment” is used broadly throughout, but some of these axes are more speculative than others.

Despite these concerns, I think the paper has real merit. The problem is novel, the proposed solution is elegant, and the empirical results are promising enough to justify publication if the claims are tightened and the presentation improved. In particular, the paper would be significantly stronger with the following revisions.

First, strengthen the DP exposition. Provide a formal theorem with a complete sensitivity derivation for the vector-valued mechanism, explicitly state the neighboring relation on paired demonstrations, and clarify the exact covariance of the added Gaussian noise. Also explain how the per-layer and total privacy budgets are allocated in experiments, and consider reporting results as utility versus target epsilon rather than fixing sigma.

Second, improve the empirical rigor of the utility evaluation. Report averages over multiple PSA noise draws with standard deviations or confidence intervals. If possible, include significance testing for the gap between Mean Steering and PSA. Clarify how clipping thresholds and layer subsets were selected, and whether any public validation data was used.

Third, temper and broaden the privacy audit claims. The canary-based attack is a useful contribution, but it should be framed as a targeted extraction-style MIA rather than a comprehensive privacy evaluation. If feasible, add at least one more attack variant on naturalistic demonstrations or paraphrased canaries, and analyze sensitivity to temperature, threshold, and number of samples. It would also help to compare against a clipping-only non-DP baseline in the privacy audit.

Fourth, expand the discussion of limitations. The appendix briefly notes that only mean steering and monolingual settings are considered. I would add that the current evaluation does not address adaptive adversaries with white-box access to steering vectors, nor does it study whether private steering vectors can leak aggregate sensitive attributes even when individual membership is protected.

In summary, I view this as a good and original paper with a simple but meaningful technical contribution, solid motivation, and encouraging experiments, but also with some important gaps in privacy accounting clarity, empirical rigor, and attack realism. If accepted, it would likely stimulate useful follow-up work on privacy for inference-time alignment methods. My overall assessment is positive but not unreserved: the paper is above the bar on novelty and relevance, and probably on empirical usefulness, but it would benefit from a more careful and precise treatment of both the DP guarantees and the empirical privacy claims.
