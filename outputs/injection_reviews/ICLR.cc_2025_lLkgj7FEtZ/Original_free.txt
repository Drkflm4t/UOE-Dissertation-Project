Summary:
This paper studies privacy-preserving activation steering for LLM alignment. It proposes PSA, a simple method that computes mean steering vectors from paired positive/negative demonstrations, clips and normalizes per-example difference vectors, and adds Gaussian noise to obtain differentially private steering vectors. These vectors are then reused at inference time with no further privacy cost by post-processing. The paper also introduces a membership inference attack tailored to activation steering using canary demonstrations and output-only access. Experiments across seven alignment behaviors and several open-source instruction-tuned LLMs suggest that PSA preserves much of the utility of non-private mean steering while providing formal DP guarantees and reducing empirical privacy leakage.

Overall assessment:
I found the paper timely, well-motivated, and potentially impactful. The core idea is simple but meaningful: if organizations use private demonstrations to derive steering directions, those directions themselves may leak information, and DP is a natural way to protect them. The paper appears to be the first to formulate and study this problem for activation editing. The empirical scope is fairly broad in terms of behaviors and model families, and the privacy auditing component is a useful addition.

That said, I have several concerns about the technical precision of the DP accounting, the strength and realism of the empirical privacy evaluation, and some aspects of the experimental methodology. The paper is promising, but I think it would benefit from tighter theory statements, stronger baselines, and more careful privacy/utility analysis before being accepted at a top venue.

Strengths:

1. Novel problem formulation
The paper identifies an important and underexplored privacy risk: activation steering vectors derived from private demonstrations may leak membership information. This is a natural and practically relevant setting, especially for enterprise/private-domain deployment.

2. Simple and elegant method
PSA is straightforward: clip per-example activation differences and add Gaussian noise. This simplicity is a virtue. It is easy to implement and compatible with existing steering pipelines.

3. Formal privacy framing
The paper gives a clean DP formulation and correctly leverages post-processing to argue that once the private steering vectors are released, arbitrarily many downstream generations incur no additional privacy cost.

4. Broad empirical coverage
The experiments span seven alignment-relevant behaviors, multiple model families (Llama, Qwen, Mistral, Gemma), and some scaling analysis. This breadth is appreciated.

5. First attack tailored to this setting
The canary-based MIA is a useful first step toward auditing privacy in activation steering, especially under output-only access.

6. Utility appears reasonably preserved
Across the plots in Figure 3 and the tables on pages 7–10, PSA often tracks non-private mean steering fairly closely and generally improves over zero-shot on the targeted behaviors.

Weaknesses / concerns:

1. DP theorem statements are too loose and in places technically questionable
The privacy guarantee is presented as (|S|ε, |S|δ)-DP by basic composition over layers. This is fine as a simple upper bound, but the paper’s actual mechanism and notation need more care.

- In Algorithm 1 on page 5, the Gaussian noise is written as N(0, σ^2), but the steering vector is d-dimensional. Presumably this means isotropic Gaussian N(0, σ^2 I_d). This should be stated explicitly.
- The sensitivity argument is only implicit. Since neighboring datasets differ in one paired demonstration, replacing one clipped vector with another changes the average by at most 2/n in L2 norm if each clipped-normalized vector has norm at most 1. This seems to explain the factor 2 in σ, but the paper should state this derivation clearly.
- The clipping/normalization description is somewhat confusing. The algorithm uses \bar d_i^l = d_i^l / max{C_l, ||d_i^l||_2}, which indeed ensures norm at most 1, but the text alternates between “project to an L2 ball of radius C_l and then scale by C_l” and “divide by threshold before noise addition.” These are equivalent only if carefully defined; the exposition should be tightened.
- The paper repeatedly says the model can answer “infinitely many queries” with no extra privacy cost. Formally, this is true only if the private steering vectors are computed once and then all future outputs are pure post-processing of those fixed DP outputs. However, if any hyperparameter tuning, layer selection, threshold selection, or multiplier selection uses private data adaptively, that incurs privacy cost. The current presentation underplays this issue.

2. Hyperparameter selection and privacy accounting are under-specified
A major practical concern is whether the reported privacy guarantees include model selection and tuning.

- The paper says the clipping constant can be “optimised for or estimated using a small public dataset” (page 5), but it is unclear what was actually done in experiments.
- The selected layers appear fixed to five middle layers for the main experiments (Table 2 on page 7), but the rationale seems borrowed from prior work rather than validated privately. If layer selection was informed by private validation, privacy accounting should include it.
- The noise scale is fixed to 0.02 standard deviation (page 6), and then ε is derived from that. This is acceptable if chosen independently of private data, but the paper should explicitly state whether all hyperparameters were fixed a priori or tuned on public data only.
- The steering strength λ is fixed to 1 for positive steering, but again it is not clear whether this was selected using private data.

Without a precise statement of what is public, what is private, and what tuning touched private data, the end-to-end DP claim is incomplete.

3. The empirical privacy evaluation is interesting but limited
The MIA is novel, but its realism and interpretation are limited.

- The attack inserts synthetic canaries directly into the demonstration set (pages 9–10, 22). This is useful as a stress test, but it is not the same as inferring membership of naturally occurring private examples.
- The attack is highly specialized to lexical canary recall. It may detect one kind of memorization-like leakage, but activation steering could leak semantic or attribute-level information in ways this attack would miss.
- The threshold τ is chosen separately for Llama and Qwen (page 10). It is not clear how τ was selected and whether this uses held-out attack calibration data. This matters for fairness and reproducibility.
- The empirical ε computation from FPR/FNR is borrowed from prior auditing work, but the paper should be more careful not to overinterpret it. Empirical ε from one attack family is only a lower bound on privacy leakage detectable by that attack, not a comprehensive privacy characterization.
- The comparison to non-private methods is somewhat trivial because non-private mean steering has no finite DP guarantee. Reporting ε_theoretical = ∞ is correct but not especially informative.

4. Evaluation methodology has several weaknesses
- The main alignment evaluation uses binary multiple-choice datasets from Anthropic/Rimsky et al. This is standard for steering work, but these tasks are narrow and may not reflect realistic alignment behavior.
- GPT-4 judging is used for open-ended evaluation (page 8, Table 3), but only for Llama-2-7B in the main table. It would be stronger to report this across more models.
- The GPT-4 scores are difficult to interpret because for some behaviors lower is better (e.g., hallucination, sycophancy, AI coordination depending on framing), while for others higher is better (e.g., refusal, corrigibility). The paper should make this explicit in each table/caption.
- There are no confidence intervals or significance tests in the main results. Given that some differences between Mean Steering and PSA are small, uncertainty estimates are important.
- The MMLU evaluation is unusual: the paper reformats questions into A/B format and reports “average probability of selecting the correct answer” (page 8). This is not standard MMLU accuracy and may be hard to compare or interpret.
- The scaling analysis in Figure 4 is only shown for one dataset (Refusal) and one model family (Qwen). The claim that larger LLMs exhibit a stronger privacy-utility tradeoff is plausible but under-supported.

5. Baseline selection could be stronger
The paper compares mainly against zero-shot, PCA steering, and mean steering.

- Since the contribution is DP steering, stronger non-private steering baselines would help, especially more recent activation editing methods beyond mean/PCA if applicable.
- More importantly, there should be a comparison to simpler privacy baselines, e.g., subsampling demonstrations, using fewer examples, or adding noise directly to activations at inference time, if meaningful.
- It would also be useful to compare against a DP in-context learning baseline or DP fine-tuning baseline in at least one setting, even if expensive, to contextualize utility/privacy tradeoffs.

6. Some claims are overstated
- “Minimal loss in performance” is mostly true qualitatively, but not uniformly. In Figure 3 there are visible drops on some datasets/models.
- “Improved guarantees compared to several existing non-private techniques” is a bit awkward: of course any DP method improves formal privacy guarantees over non-private methods. The more meaningful question is whether the utility cost is acceptable.
- The paper occasionally suggests that PSA “protects against MIA.” The experiments show reduced success for one tailored attack, not broad protection against all MIAs.

7. Presentation issues and possible inconsistencies
- There are a few notation and wording issues. For example, Algorithm 1 has a typo in the difference vector line (“hl((p, c+))” instead of indexed notation).
- Table 21 on page 23 appears to contain an obvious error: the zero-shot Corrigibility score is listed as 74.74, which is impossible on a 0–10 scale. This raises concerns about proofreading.
- Some qualitative examples are not very convincing. For instance, the hallucination example in Table 11 still contains the false premise rather than correcting it robustly.
- The paper would benefit from a clearer distinction between “alignment toward desirable behavior” and “behavioral steering according to benchmark labels,” since some axes are normatively loaded.

Questions for the authors:

1. What exact neighboring relation is assumed for DP: add/remove one paired demonstration, or replace one paired demonstration? Please state this explicitly and derive the sensitivity formally.

2. Were clipping thresholds, selected layers, noise scales, and steering multipliers chosen entirely without access to private data? If not, how is that privacy cost accounted for?

3. Is the Gaussian noise in Algorithm 1 isotropic in R^d, i.e., N(0, σ^2 I_d)? Please clarify.

4. Why is the noise scale fixed to 0.02 across datasets/models? Was this chosen for utility, privacy, or both?

5. Can the authors provide confidence intervals over the main results in Figure 3 and Table 3?

6. How sensitive is the MIA to the choice of threshold τ, number of generations N, and temperature? Was τ calibrated on separate attack data?

7. Can the authors evaluate privacy leakage on more natural membership signals, not only synthetic canaries?

8. Did the authors try advanced composition or zCDP/RDP accounting? Even if |S| is small, a more standard accountant might improve clarity.

Suggestions for improvement:

1. Strengthen the theory section
Provide a formal theorem with explicit sensitivity proof, dimensionality of the Gaussian mechanism, and a precise neighboring-dataset definition. Clarify all assumptions needed for the “infinite queries at no extra cost” statement.

2. Clarify end-to-end privacy accounting
Add a subsection specifying which components are public, which are private, and how hyperparameter tuning is handled. If tuning used public data only, say so clearly.

3. Improve empirical rigor
Add standard deviations/confidence intervals, multiple random seeds for noise draws, and significance testing where appropriate.

4. Expand privacy auditing
Complement the canary MIA with at least one more attack style, ideally targeting natural examples or semantic attributes rather than lexical canaries alone.

5. Broaden utility evaluation
Report open-ended GPT-4 evaluations for more than one model, and consider a stronger factuality or safety benchmark beyond binary MCQ tasks.

6. Add stronger baselines
Include at least one simple privacy baseline and, if feasible, one alternative alignment method with privacy protection for context.

7. Fix presentation issues
Correct typos and table inconsistencies, especially the apparent error in Table 21.

Recommendation:
Weak Accept to Borderline Weak Reject

Rationale:
I like the problem, the method is clean, and the paper likely opens a useful new direction. The empirical results are encouraging, and the privacy framing is important. However, for a top conference paper, I would want tighter DP accounting, clearer handling of hyperparameter selection, stronger and more realistic privacy evaluation, and somewhat more rigorous experimental reporting. If the authors can address these concerns convincingly, I would be supportive. As it stands, I see this as promising but not fully polished.

Confidence:
3/5

I am reasonably confident in the high-level assessment, especially regarding novelty and the main methodological concerns, but less confident about whether the current empirical evidence is sufficient relative to venue standards in this area.
