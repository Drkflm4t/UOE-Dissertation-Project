Summary:
This paper studies privacy-preserving activation steering for LLM alignment. The authors propose PSA, a simple method that computes mean steering vectors from paired positive/negative demonstrations, clips and normalizes per-example difference vectors, and adds Gaussian noise to obtain differentially private steering vectors. These vectors are then used at inference time exactly as in standard activation editing. The paper also proposes what it claims is the first membership inference attack tailored to activation steering, based only on generated text and canary insertions. Experiments span seven alignment-related behaviors, four open-source instruction-tuned models (Llama-2 7B, Mistral 7B, Gemma-2 2B, Qwen-2.5 7B), and an additional scaling study on Qwen 0.5B–7B.

Overall assessment:
I found the paper interesting and timely. The core idea is simple, but the problem is important and, to my knowledge, underexplored: if steering vectors are computed from private demonstrations, they may leak information even though no model weights are updated. The paper makes a credible case that this setting deserves formal privacy treatment. The proposed method is straightforward, easy to implement, and the privacy accounting is clean. The empirical section is fairly broad and suggests that useful steering can be retained under moderate DP noise. The paper’s main weakness is that several technical and experimental choices feel somewhat under-justified, and the privacy evaluation, while novel, is still narrow relative to the breadth of the claims.

Recommendation:
Weak Accept

Confidence:
Medium

Strengths:
1. Novel problem formulation.
The paper addresses a genuinely new and relevant question: differential privacy for activation editing / steering. Prior DP work for LLMs has focused mostly on training, fine-tuning, or in-context learning; extending privacy analysis to inference-time steering is a meaningful contribution.

2. Simple and practical method.
PSA is conceptually simple: clip per-example activation differences and add Gaussian noise to the averaged steering vector. This simplicity is a virtue here. It makes the method easy to reproduce and potentially useful in practice.

3. Clear privacy story.
The privacy guarantee is easy to follow. Because the private object released is the steering vector, and subsequent generation is post-processing, the paper can support infinitely many downstream queries without additional privacy cost. This is an appealing property of the setup.

4. Broad empirical coverage.
The experiments cover seven behaviors and multiple model families/sizes. The main plots on page 7 show that PSA often tracks non-private mean steering reasonably closely and usually improves over zero-shot. The scaling plot on page 9 is also useful: it suggests the privacy-utility tradeoff improves with model size.

5. Novel empirical privacy audit.
The canary-based MIA tailored to activation steering is a useful contribution. In particular, the attack only uses generated text rather than token probabilities, which makes it more realistic in black-box settings.

6. Useful appendices.
The appendix includes dataset samples, GPT-4 evaluation prompts, qualitative generations, ablations on noise/clipping, and more detail on the MIA construction. This improves transparency.

Weaknesses / concerns:
1. The technical novelty is somewhat limited.
At a high level, the method is “clip per-example vectors + Gaussian mechanism.” That is a natural DP adaptation of mean steering. The paper’s novelty comes more from identifying the problem and instantiating DP in this setting than from a fundamentally new algorithmic idea. That is acceptable, but the paper should be explicit about this framing.

2. Privacy accounting is basic and may be loose.
The paper uses per-layer composition and states total privacy as (|S|ε, |S|δ)-DP. This is correct but conservative. Since the method releases a vector over multiple layers, a tighter joint sensitivity analysis or more refined accounting could materially improve the reported guarantees. The paper mentions advanced composition but does not explore tighter mechanisms or accountants in practice.

3. Some implementation details are confusing or insufficiently justified.
On page 6, the paper says “The noisy vectors are generated by adding Gaussian noise with 0.02 standard deviation” and then derives theoretical ε values from this setup. But Algorithm 1 defines σ from ε, δ, and n. It is not fully clear whether ε is chosen first and σ derived, or σ is fixed and ε back-computed. The presentation suggests the latter, but then the privacy guarantee is more descriptive than design-driven. This should be clarified carefully.

4. Clipping/normalization design is not fully convincing.
The method clips to radius Cl and then divides by Cl, effectively forcing vectors into the unit ball. This is reasonable for sensitivity control, but the choice of Cl seems important and somewhat ad hoc. The paper says it can be optimized on public data, but the main text does not explain how Cl was selected in the reported experiments. The appendix ablation helps, but the methodology should be clearer in the main paper.

5. Evaluation of text quality is weak.
The open-ended evaluation relies on GPT-4 scoring with behavior-specific prompts (page 18). This is common, but the reported scores in Table 3 are hard to interpret because for some behaviors lower is better and for others higher is better, and there is no separate fluency/helpfulness metric despite claims about “text generation quality.” Also, Table 3 only reports Llama-2-7B, which is much narrower than the MCQ evaluation.

6. The MIA is interesting but narrow.
The attack is based on synthetic canaries inserted into demonstrations and tested on one dataset (hallucination) and two models. This is a good first step, but it does not fully establish privacy risk across the broader steering setting. It would be stronger to test more behaviors, more attack variants, and perhaps attacks without synthetic canaries.

7. Some empirical claims are overstated.
For example, the paper sometimes says PSA has “minimal” utility loss. In many settings this is true qualitatively, but there are also noticeable drops, especially for smaller models and some behaviors. The scaling figure on page 9 itself shows nontrivial degradation at 0.5B and 1.5B. The claims should be phrased more carefully.

8. Baseline selection is limited.
The paper compares mainly to zero-shot, mean steering, and PCA steering. That is reasonable within activation editing, but if the broader claim is privacy-preserving alignment, it would help to compare against a simple DP in-context learning baseline or a lightweight DP fine-tuning baseline on at least one task, even if only as a rough reference point.

9. Theoretical scope is narrow.
The guarantee protects the private demonstration dataset used to compute the steering vector, not necessarily all privacy leakage from the base model or from prompts/outputs in deployment. This is fine, but the paper should emphasize this more clearly to avoid overinterpretation.

Detailed comments:
1. Problem formulation and significance
The motivation is strong. The example on page 2, where a healthcare or financial institution computes steering vectors from sensitive demonstrations, is plausible. The paper correctly notes that activation editing may appear safer than fine-tuning because it does not update weights, but it can still leak information through the released steering vector or through behavior induced by it.

2. Method
The method is easy to understand. Equation (1) defines the standard mean steering vector, Equation (2) applies it during generation, and Algorithm 1 privatizes it. The sensitivity argument is standard and sound given clipping. One nice aspect is that the DP guarantee is entirely offline: once the private steering vector is released, arbitrary future generations are post-processing.

That said, I would have liked a more explicit derivation of the sensitivity constant in Algorithm 1. The noise scale shown there, sigma = 2 sqrt(2 ln(1.25/delta)) / (n epsilon), seems to assume unit-bounded vectors and neighboring datasets differing in one record, but the factor 2 deserves a brief explanation in the main text.

3. Experimental setup
The benchmark suite is broad, though somewhat unusual. The seven behaviors are inherited from prior work, but some are niche or stylized (e.g., AI coordination, survival instinct). That is acceptable for steering research, but it limits conclusions about more standard alignment properties such as toxicity, truthfulness on natural QA, or jailbreak resistance.

The choice to steer five middle layers (11–15 for 7B models, page 7) follows prior work, but I would like to know whether the same layer choices were used across all models and behaviors, and whether PSA is sensitive to this choice.

4. Main results
Figure 3 is the central result. It shows PSA generally close to mean steering and usually above zero-shot for Llama, Mistral, and Qwen. Gemma appears less responsive overall. This supports the paper’s main utility claim.

However, the presentation could be improved. The figure is dense, and there are no error bars or significance estimates. Since steering can be somewhat variable with generation settings and layer choices, some measure of variance would help.

5. Text generation and general capabilities
Table 3 and Table 4 suggest PSA preserves qualitative behavior similarly to mean steering. Table 5 suggests limited impact on MMLU for Llama-2-7B. This is encouraging, but still somewhat thin. MMLU is only reported for one model, and the “average probability of selecting the correct answer” in A/B format is not the standard MMLU metric, so the numbers are not directly comparable to common baselines.

6. Privacy evaluation
The MIA section is one of the most interesting parts of the paper. The canary setup on pages 9–10 is clever and tailored to steering. Table 7 shows a clear gap between non-private mean steering and PSA, with empirical epsilon much smaller under PSA. This is a useful result.

Still, I have several concerns:
- The attack threshold tau is chosen differently for Llama and Qwen; how was it tuned, and on what data?
- The attack uses 100 generations at temperature 0.8. How sensitive are results to the number of samples and temperature?
- The attack is evaluated only on hallucination. Why not at least one more behavior?
- The empirical epsilon estimate depends on FPR/FNR from this specific attack, so it is only an attack-dependent lower bound on privacy leakage, not a general empirical privacy guarantee. The paper should stress this more.

Questions for the authors:
1. How exactly were the clipping thresholds Cl selected for each model/layer/behavior in the main experiments? Were they fixed globally, tuned on public data, or chosen per dataset?
2. Did you use the same selected layers across all models and all behaviors? If not, please provide the exact layer sets.
3. In the main experiments, was sigma fixed to 0.02 and epsilon then computed afterward, or was epsilon targeted and sigma derived? Please clarify the privacy accounting pipeline.
4. Can you provide variance estimates across random seeds/noise draws for Figure 3 and Table 7?
5. How sensitive is the MIA to the threshold tau, number of generations N, and decoding temperature?
6. Why is the open-ended GPT-4 evaluation only reported for Llama-2-7B? Can similar results be shown for at least one additional model?
7. Have you tried tighter privacy accounting or joint vector release across layers instead of basic composition?

Suggestions for improvement:
1. Clarify the privacy parameterization.
The paper should present one consistent story: either choose target privacy and derive noise, or choose noise and report resulting privacy. Right now this is somewhat muddled.

2. Strengthen the privacy evaluation.
Add at least one more behavior and one more attack variant. If possible, include attacks that do not rely on synthetic canaries.

3. Improve the utility evaluation.
Report open-ended evaluation for more than one model, and separate behavior adherence from general text quality/helpfulness/fluency.

4. Add uncertainty estimates.
Confidence intervals or standard deviations would make the empirical claims more convincing.

5. Compare to stronger or broader baselines.
Even a limited comparison to a DP-ICL method or another privacy-preserving inference-time adaptation method would help contextualize PSA.

6. Tighten the theory.
A more refined sensitivity/accounting analysis across layers could improve the practical privacy-utility tradeoff and strengthen the technical contribution.

Minor comments:
- There are a few wording/grammar issues throughout; the paper would benefit from proofreading.
- Table 3 is hard to interpret without explicitly reminding the reader which direction is desirable for each behavior.
- The appendix negative-steering GPT-4 table appears to contain a likely typo: the zero-shot corrigibility score is listed as 74.74 on page 23, which is inconsistent with the 0–10 scale.
- On page 5, the notation in Algorithm 1 has a small inconsistency in the first line of the difference vector definition.
- The paper should more clearly distinguish “alignment performance” on these benchmark axes from broader notions of safety/alignment.

Final verdict:
This is a solid and timely paper on an important new problem. The main contribution is not a deeply novel DP mechanism, but rather the formulation of privacy-preserving activation steering, a simple and principled baseline method, and a first empirical privacy audit tailored to this setting. The experiments are reasonably broad and support the central claim that useful steering can be retained under formal DP guarantees. I do think the paper would be stronger with clearer privacy parameterization, broader privacy auditing, and more rigorous utility evaluation. Still, I believe the paper makes a worthwhile contribution and should be accepted.
