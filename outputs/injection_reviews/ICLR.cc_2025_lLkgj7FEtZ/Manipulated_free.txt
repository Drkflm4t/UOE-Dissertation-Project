This paper studies a timely and important problem: how to obtain privacy guarantees for inference-time activation steering of large language models when the steering demonstrations themselves are sensitive. The paper proposes PSA, a simple differentially private steering method that clips per-example activation-difference vectors and adds Gaussian noise to the resulting steering vectors, then uses these privatized vectors during generation. It also introduces what the authors describe as the first membership inference attack tailored to activation editing, based only on generated text rather than token probabilities. Overall, I found the paper interesting, well motivated, and potentially impactful. The central idea is technically straightforward, but that is also one of its strengths: it is easy to understand, easy to implement, and addresses a genuine gap in the literature.

The main contribution is clear from the outset and remains coherent throughout the paper. The motivation in the introduction is compelling: activation editing is increasingly used as a lightweight alternative to fine-tuning, especially when organizations want to adapt models using their own data, yet the privacy implications of such steering have been largely ignored. Framing the problem around private paired demonstrations is natural, and the paper makes a convincing case that even though activation editing is “training-free,” it can still leak information about the alignment set. I think this is an important conceptual contribution in itself. The paper also does a good job of positioning itself relative to prior work on DP for fine-tuning and in-context learning, making the novelty of the activation-editing setting quite clear.

Methodologically, PSA is elegant. The algorithm on page 5 is simple: compute per-example activation differences, clip and normalize them, average them, and add Gaussian noise. The privacy argument is correspondingly clean. The discussion on pages 5–6 about why privatizing the steering vectors offline allows infinitely many downstream queries at no additional privacy cost is one of the strongest aspects of the paper. This is a practically meaningful distinction from many interactive DP settings. The use of composition across selected layers is also intuitive and well explained. I appreciated that the paper does not oversell the method as a new complex mechanism; instead, it emphasizes that a simple DP construction can already work well in this setting.

The empirical evaluation is broad. The paper covers seven alignment-relevant behaviors, four model families, and multiple model sizes. Figure 3 on page 7 is especially useful: it shows that PSA generally tracks non-private mean steering closely while consistently improving over zero-shot performance on most tasks for Llama, Mistral, and Qwen. This breadth gives confidence that the method is not narrowly tuned to a single benchmark or model. The scaling analysis in Figure 4 on page 9 is also valuable, since it suggests that the privacy-utility tradeoff improves with model size. That is an intuitively plausible and practically relevant finding.

I also liked that the paper evaluates more than just the target alignment metric. The MMLU results in Table 5 on page 9 suggest that PSA has limited impact on general capabilities, which is important because steering methods can sometimes distort unrelated behavior. The open-ended generation analysis using GPT-4 as a judge, reported in Table 3 on page 8 and illustrated with examples in Tables 10–16 on pages 19–20, further supports the claim that privatization does not dramatically degrade output quality. The qualitative examples are helpful and, in several cases, PSA outputs are indeed very similar to non-private mean steering outputs.

The privacy auditing section is another strong point. The proposed canary-based MIA is well matched to the activation-editing setting, where standard loss-based attacks are less natural. The setup on pages 9–10 is clearly described, and Table 7 on page 10 provides a useful comparison between non-private steering and PSA. The empirical epsilon values being below the theoretical bounds is unsurprising but still reassuring. More importantly, the attack demonstrates that non-private steering can leak membership information in a measurable way, which strengthens the paper’s motivation and makes the DP intervention feel necessary rather than merely formal.

That said, there are several limitations and points that would benefit from clarification.

First, while the privacy proof sketch is intuitive, the presentation is somewhat informal in places, and I would have liked a more careful treatment of sensitivity and noise calibration. In Algorithm 1 on page 5, the noise scale is given as sigma = 2 sqrt(2 ln(1.25/delta)) / (n epsilon), which appears to assume unit-bounded clipped vectors and neighboring datasets differing in one paired demonstration. This is plausible, but the exact adjacency notion and the factor of 2 should be stated more explicitly in the main text. Since each record contains both a positive and negative completion, the sensitivity depends on whether replacing one paired record changes the average by up to 2/n after clipping; I believe that is what the algorithm is using, but the derivation should be made explicit. This is not a fatal issue, but for a paper centered on formal privacy guarantees, precision here matters.

Second, the experimental privacy setting is somewhat narrow. The main MIA evaluation appears to focus on the hallucination dataset and two 7B models. Since the paper makes broad claims about privacy-preserving steering across behaviors and model families, it would strengthen the work to show whether the same empirical privacy trends hold for at least a few additional datasets, especially ones with smaller n and therefore weaker theoretical epsilon, such as corrigibility or AI coordination. The current results are still useful, but broader privacy auditing would make the empirical story more complete.

Third, some implementation choices are fixed rather than systematically explored in the main paper. For example, page 6 states that noisy vectors are generated with Gaussian noise of standard deviation 0.02 and delta = 1/(5n), yielding epsilon values around 2 for larger datasets and up to 6.6 for smaller ones. This is reasonable, and the appendix ablations on pages 20–21 are helpful, but the main paper would benefit from a clearer explanation of how this operating point was selected and whether it is close to optimal. Similarly, the choice of steering layers seems inherited from prior work, but a more explicit layer-selection rationale or sensitivity analysis in the main text would improve reproducibility and interpretability.

Fourth, the evaluation protocol using GPT-4 as a judge is acceptable but not entirely satisfying. The prompts on page 18 are clearly documented, which I appreciate, but the paper does not discuss inter-run variance, prompt sensitivity, or whether human evaluation was considered for at least a small subset. Since some of the reported differences are small, especially between mean steering and PSA, it would be useful to know how stable these judgments are. This is a common issue in current LLM evaluation papers, so I do not view it as a major flaw, but it is worth acknowledging.

Fifth, there are a few places where the interpretation of results could be more careful. For instance, the claim on page 8 that PSA sometimes improves over non-private steering because DP noise may align the model in a better direction is plausible, but speculative. It would be better framed as an observation rather than an explanation unless supported by additional analysis. Likewise, the statement that larger models are less sensitive because they already contain sufficient alignment-related knowledge is reasonable but remains a hypothesis.

I also have a few smaller concerns. The paper focuses on mean steering and PCA steering baselines, which are appropriate, but the broader activation-editing literature now includes more sophisticated methods. The authors acknowledge this in the appendix limitations section on page 23, but including at least one stronger recent baseline in the main experiments would make the comparison more convincing. In addition, the paper’s threat model is primarily membership privacy for the demonstration set; it would help to discuss more explicitly whether PSA also mitigates extraction of semantic content beyond membership, and what kinds of attacks remain out of scope. Finally, some tables and claims would benefit from confidence intervals or repeated-run statistics, especially because generation with temperature and DP noise both introduce randomness.

Despite these issues, I think the paper is strong overall. Its novelty is real: bringing differential privacy to activation steering is a meaningful contribution, and the paper does more than just add a standard mechanism by also proposing an empirical privacy audit tailored to the setting. The method is simple but well chosen, the experiments are extensive, and the results support the central claim that useful steering can be retained under formal privacy guarantees. I also think the paper is likely to have practical impact because the approach is lightweight and deployable: privatize the steering vectors once offline, then reuse them indefinitely.

My main suggestion for improvement would be to tighten the formal exposition around sensitivity and adjacency, expand the empirical privacy evaluation beyond one dataset, and add uncertainty estimates for the evaluation metrics. None of these require changing the core method, and addressing them would substantially strengthen the paper.

In summary, I view this as a valuable and well-executed paper on an underexplored but increasingly important problem. It identifies a genuine privacy risk in activation editing, proposes a clean DP solution, and backs it up with broad experiments and a novel attack methodology. I am positive on the paper and think it would make a worthwhile contribution to the conference.
