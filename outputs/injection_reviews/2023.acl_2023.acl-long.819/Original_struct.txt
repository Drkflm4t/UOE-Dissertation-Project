Summary: The paper studies false negatives in vision-language contrastive pretraining and argues, from a mutual-information perspective, that standard InfoNCE effectively maximizes a lower bound on positive-pair MI minus negative-pair MI when negatives are not independent of the anchor. Motivated by this, it proposes Similarity-Regulated Contrastive Learning (SRCL), which assigns each negative a weight inversely proportional to estimated cross-modal similarity, with similarity estimated by a mixture of a frozen auxiliary model trained on human-annotated data and the evolving VLP model. The method is implemented on top of ALBEF and evaluated on image-text retrieval, zero-shot retrieval, VQA, and NLVR, showing consistent gains over the ALBEF baseline on the reported benchmarks.

Strengths: Addresses an important and practically relevant issue in multimodal contrastive learning: false and partial false negatives arising from many-to-many image-text correspondences.; Provides a clear high-level intuition, supported by figures (pages 1, 4, 7, 17, 18), that uniform repulsion of all negatives is suboptimal in vision-language settings.; Empirically shows consistent improvements over the ALBEF baseline across several downstream tasks, including retrieval, zero-shot retrieval, VQA, and NLVR.; The proposed modification is relatively simple and can be integrated into an existing VLP framework without changing the overall architecture.; The analysis of the tradeoff between removing too few vs. too many suspected false negatives (page 7) is insightful and helps motivate why soft regulation may be preferable to hard filtering.; Qualitative retrieval examples suggest the learned representation space may better preserve graded semantic similarity than the baseline.

Weaknesses: The theoretical claims are stronger than what the derivations rigorously justify; several proof steps rely on approximations and assumptions that are not well formalized.; The method depends on an auxiliary model trained on human-annotated in-domain data to estimate similarity early in training, which weakens the claim of a generally applicable self-supervised solution and introduces extra supervision/computation.; Empirical evaluation is narrow in scope: the method is only instantiated on ALBEF, despite the paper claiming broader relevance to cross-modal contrastive learning.; Ablation studies are limited. Key design choices such as the auxiliary model Hβ, the schedule for α, the normalization function, scaling factor δ, and sensitivity to batch size/temperature are not thoroughly isolated.; Comparisons are mostly against ALBEF and broad prior systems with different data scales/settings; there is limited comparison to more directly related false-negative mitigation or soft-label/distillation approaches.; Some experimental claims about data efficiency and semantic structure are suggestive rather than conclusive, since they rely mainly on zero-shot retrieval trends and qualitative examples rather than controlled diagnostics.

Methodological Flaws: The MI derivation in Appendix A.2/A.3 is not fully rigorous. In particular, the transition from sample sums to expectations uses an approximation without quantifying error, and the argument that false negatives are a small proportion is invoked to justify inequality (21), which is not generally proven.; There is a likely notation/logic inconsistency in Appendix A.3: the covariance condition is stated in the main text as involving wi,j and P(ti|vi)/P(ti), but the appendix alternates between positive-pair and negative-pair density ratios, making the proof difficult to validate as written.; The claim that optimizing the loss implies the left-side term in Eq. (36) is minimized to exactly zero (Eq. 37) is unjustified. Minimizing the loss or maximizing a lower bound does not in general force that term to equal zero.; The central modeling assumption that cross-modal semantic similarity positively correlates with MI is plausible but unverified; the method’s correctness depends on this proxy quality, yet no direct validation is provided.; Using a separately trained model on MSCOCO human annotations to estimate similarity introduces additional supervision and possible domain leakage toward downstream benchmarks, especially since MSCOCO is also used in pretraining and evaluation-related settings.; The pilot experiment and false-negative filtering analysis use the model’s own contrastive weights as a proxy for false negatives, which risks circular reasoning: the same mechanism used to define false negatives is then used to validate the benefit of handling them.; The empirical section lacks statistical significance estimates or multi-run variance, so it is unclear how robust the reported gains are, especially where improvements are modest.; The fairness of some baseline comparisons is limited because competing methods use different pretraining data scales, architectures, and supervision regimes; stronger controlled comparisons to directly related methods would be needed to substantiate state-of-the-art claims.
