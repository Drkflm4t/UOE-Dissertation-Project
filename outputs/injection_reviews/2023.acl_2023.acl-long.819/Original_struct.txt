Summary: This paper studies false negatives in vision-language pretraining with cross-modal contrastive learning. The authors revisit InfoNCE from a mutual information perspective and argue that, when false negatives are non-negligible, optimizing InfoNCE effectively maximizes a lower bound on MI(anchor, positive) minus expected MI(anchor, negative), which can improperly suppress semantically related negatives. To address this, they propose Similarity-Regulated Contrastive Learning (SRCL), which assigns each negative a weight inversely proportional to estimated cross-modal similarity, with similarity progressively refined during training using a frozen auxiliary model early on and the target VLP model later. Implemented on top of ALBEF and evaluated on retrieval, zero-shot retrieval, VQA, and NLVR2, SRCL consistently improves over the ALBEF baseline and yields competitive results overall.

Strengths: Addresses an important and practically relevant issue in contrastive vision-language pretraining: partial/false negatives arising from many-to-many image-text correspondences.; Provides a clear conceptual motivation and a theoretically oriented MI-based reinterpretation of InfoNCE under false negatives, which helps explain why vanilla contrastive learning can be suboptimal in this setting.; The proposed method is simple and easy to integrate into an existing VLP framework: it modifies contrastive weighting rather than requiring major architectural changes.; Empirical results are consistently positive across multiple downstream tasks, including image-text retrieval, zero-shot retrieval, VQA, and NLVR2; gains over ALBEF are modest but steady.; The analysis of false negatives versus hard negatives is thoughtful and useful; the thresholding experiments and weight-distribution visualizations provide intuition for why partial downweighting can be preferable to outright removal.; The paper includes qualitative examples and scaling observations suggesting improved semantic structure and better data efficiency as pretraining data size increases.

Weaknesses: The empirical validation is narrow in scope because the method is only instantiated on ALBEF; broader validation on other modern contrastive VLP frameworks would strengthen the claim of generality.; The method depends on an auxiliary similarity estimator trained on human-annotated data and sharing the same architecture, which adds complexity, extra supervision, and potential fairness concerns when comparing against baselines that do not use such an auxiliary model.; The theoretical development is suggestive but not fully rigorous in places; several derivation steps rely on approximations or assumptions about expectations, covariance, and optimization behavior that are not thoroughly justified.; The central assumption that cross-modal similarity is a good proxy for mutual information is plausible but not directly validated.; Ablation coverage appears limited: there is not enough isolation of the contributions of the auxiliary model Hβ, the progressive mixing schedule α, the normalization/scaling choices, or alternative weighting functions.; Reported improvements, while consistent, are generally incremental rather than transformative, especially given the added machinery.; Some presentation issues reduce clarity: notation is occasionally inconsistent, there are typographical errors, and parts of the proof/implementation description are harder to follow than necessary.

Soundness Issues: The MI derivation relies on approximations that replace sums with expectations and then uses inequalities whose conditions are only informally argued; this weakens the strength of the theoretical guarantee.; The claim in Appendix A.3 that optimizing the weighted loss drives the final term to zero and therefore sets expected negative MI to E[log(1/w)] is not fully established; it appears to assume a particular optimum behavior rather than proving it.; The use of a frozen auxiliary model trained on annotated data to estimate similarity may confound the source of gains: improvements may come partly from injected supervised semantic knowledge rather than solely from the proposed MI-consistent weighting principle.; Condition 1 requires negative covariance between weights and density ratio / similarity-related quantities, but in practice the paper uses inverse similarity heuristics rather than directly verifying the condition empirically or theoretically.; The heuristic assumption that false negatives are a relatively small proportion of negatives is important for some derivation steps, but the paper does not quantify when this assumption holds in the datasets used.; Comparisons are mainly against ALBEF and prior published numbers; stronger soundness would come from matched ablations against simpler alternatives such as hard-negative filtering, soft-label distillation, or temperature/logit adjustments under identical settings.
