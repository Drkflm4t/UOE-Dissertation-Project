Summary: The paper studies false negatives in vision-language contrastive pretraining, arguing that standard InfoNCE not only increases mutual information (MI) between matched image-text pairs but also implicitly minimizes MI between anchors and negatives, which is problematic when negatives are semantically related or partially matched. To address this, the authors propose Similarity-Regulated Contrastive Learning (SRCL), which assigns progressively refined cross-modal similarity-based weights to negatives so that semantically closer negatives are down-weighted rather than uniformly repelled. The method is implemented on top of ALBEF and evaluated on image-text retrieval, zero-shot retrieval, VQA, and NLVR, where it consistently improves over the ALBEF baseline and is competitive with or better than several prior VLP systems.

Strengths: The paper addresses an important and practically relevant issue in vision-language pretraining: false and partial false negatives arising from many-to-many image-text correspondences.; The work is well motivated both intuitively (e.g., Figure 1) and empirically via a pilot experiment showing that simply removing likely false negatives already improves ALBEF.; A notable strength is the attempt to ground the method in a mutual-information perspective, deriving a generalized lower-bound interpretation of InfoNCE in the presence of non-negligible false negatives.; The proposed SRCL method is simple, conceptually intuitive, and easy to integrate into an existing VLP framework by modifying only the contrastive weighting over negatives.; The progressive similarity estimation strategy, combining an auxiliary human-annotated model early in training with the evolving VLP model later, is a thoughtful design choice to mitigate unreliable early-stage similarity estimates.; Empirical results are consistently positive across multiple downstream tasks: retrieval on COCO/Flickr30K, zero-shot retrieval, VQA, and NLVR, with clear gains over ALBEF under matched architecture and pretraining setup.; The paper includes useful analyses beyond headline numbers, including the tradeoff between false negatives and hard negatives, scaling with pretraining data size, and qualitative retrieval examples.; The method appears data-efficient: the gap over ALBEF grows with larger pretraining data sizes, supporting the claim that better handling of false negatives improves utilization of noisy web-scale data.

Weaknesses: The theoretical development is interesting but not fully rigorous in several steps; some approximations and assumptions are introduced without sufficient justification, especially around expectations over negatives and the transition from weighted InfoNCE to a controllable positive MI target.; The central assumption that cross-modal similarity is a reliable proxy for mutual information is plausible but not directly validated.; The method is evaluated primarily by plugging into ALBEF; broader validation on additional architectures would strengthen claims of generality.; The auxiliary similarity model Hβ is trained on human-annotated data, which introduces extra supervision and additional training complexity; the fairness of comparing against baselines without analogous auxiliary resources is not deeply discussed.; Ablation studies are somewhat limited: the paper would benefit from clearer sensitivity analyses for α scheduling, scaling factor δ, normalization choices, and the contribution of Hβ versus Sγ individually.; Some comparisons are against methods with different pretraining data scales or architectures, which makes state-of-the-art claims less clean than direct matched-setting comparisons.

Methodological Flaws: In Appendix A.2, the derivation relies on approximating the sum over negatives by (N-1) times an expectation and then applying inequalities that depend on assumptions such as false negatives being a small proportion. These assumptions are not formalized precisely, so the claimed generalized MI lower bound should be interpreted cautiously rather than as a fully rigorous theorem.; In Appendix A.3, the covariance argument appears imprecise: the text states the weight is inversely proportional to P(ti)/P(ti|vi), but the indexed quantity in the covariance and expectation over negatives mixes positive-pair and negative-pair terms in a way that is not fully consistent. This weakens the mathematical claim that the weighted objective guarantees optimization toward a controllable positive MI value.; The statement around Equation (37)-(40) effectively assumes that minimizing the loss drives the extra term exactly to zero, which does not generally follow from the inequality alone. This is a scientific-logic gap in the proof sketch.; Empirically, the method uses an auxiliary model trained on MSCOCO-style human annotations to estimate similarity early in training. Because this adds extra curated supervision, it is difficult to isolate whether gains come purely from the proposed weighting principle versus the additional signal from Hβ.; The false-negative versus hard-negative analysis is suggestive but uses the model's own contrastive weights as a proxy for false negatives, which risks circularity: the analysis partly validates the criterion using itself rather than an external ground-truth notion of false negatives.; The paper does not report variance across runs or statistical significance, so it is unclear how robust the observed gains are, especially where improvements are modest on some metrics.
