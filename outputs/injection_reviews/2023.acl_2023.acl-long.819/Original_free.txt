This paper studies false negatives in cross-modal contrastive learning for vision-language pre-training and proposes Similarity-Regulated Contrastive Learning (SRCL), a weighted variant of InfoNCE in which negative samples are down/up-weighted according to estimated cross-modal similarity. The paper’s central claim is both theoretical and practical: first, that in the presence of non-negligible false negatives, InfoNCE should be viewed as maximizing a lower bound on MI between positives minus MI involving negatives; second, that this motivates regulating negative contributions rather than uniformly repelling all negatives. Empirically, the method is implemented on top of ALBEF and evaluated on retrieval, zero-shot retrieval, VQA, and NLVR, with consistent gains over the ALBEF baseline.

Overall, I find the paper interesting and reasonably well-motivated. The false-negative issue in vision-language contrastive learning is real and important because image-text data are inherently many-to-many, and the conceptual illustration on page 1 makes the problem intuitive. The empirical gains are consistent across several tasks, and the paper makes a commendable effort to connect the method to a mutual-information perspective rather than presenting a purely heuristic reweighting scheme. The qualitative examples on pages 8 and 16 and the contrastive-weight visualizations on pages 17–18 are also helpful in conveying what the method is trying to do.

That said, I also have substantial reservations. The main concerns are about the novelty and rigor of the theoretical contribution, the dependence of the method on an auxiliary similarity model trained with human-annotated data, and the limited scope of empirical validation relative to the strength of the claims. In its current form, I view the paper as promising but not fully convincing as a top-tier conference submission without further clarification and stronger ablations.

The strongest aspect of the paper is the problem formulation. The authors correctly identify that in-batch negatives in VLP are often semantically related to the anchor, including both full and partial false negatives. This is especially plausible in caption datasets where multiple images can match a caption at different granularities. The pilot result in Table 1 on page 2 is a useful motivating experiment: simply removing heuristically identified false negatives improves ALBEF on zero-shot retrieval and VQA, suggesting that existing soft-label/self-distillation approaches do not fully solve the issue. This is a good starting point for the paper.

The method itself is straightforward and sensible. In equations (4) and (5) on pages 4–5, the authors replace the uniform contribution of negatives in InfoNCE with per-negative weights. The weights are inversely proportional to estimated cross-modal similarity and normalized to have mean 1 among negatives. Intuitively, highly similar negatives, which are more likely to be false or partial false negatives, are penalized less. Dissimilar negatives are emphasized more. This is a natural idea, and the progressive interpolation between an external similarity model and the current VLP model is also reasonable in spirit: early in training, the model’s own similarity estimates are unreliable, so the method relies more on a separately trained model.

The empirical results are solid relative to the chosen baseline. In Table 2 on page 6, SRCL improves ALBEF on both COCO and Flickr30K retrieval, with gains that are not huge but are consistent across TR and IR. In Table 3, the method improves ALBEF on VQA and NLVR as well. Table 4 is perhaps the most compelling: zero-shot Flickr30K retrieval improves noticeably over ALBEF and even compares favorably to larger-scale models such as CLIP/ALIGN/FILIP, though of course those comparisons are not apples-to-apples because architectures, data, and training recipes differ. Figure 4 on page 7 is also useful: the gains appear to persist and even widen as pretraining data size increases, which supports the claim that the method improves data efficiency under noisy many-to-many supervision.

I also appreciated the discussion in Section 4.3 and Figure 3 on page 7 about false negatives versus hard negatives. This is an important nuance. The paper argues that some semantically close negatives are harmful if over-repelled, but that hard negatives are still useful for learning discriminative representations. The thresholding experiment, where performance first improves and then degrades as more low-weight negatives are removed, is a nice empirical illustration of this tradeoff. This supports the authors’ claim that reweighting is preferable to simply discarding all suspected false negatives.

However, the theoretical contribution is weaker than the paper suggests. The derivation in Section 2 and Appendix A is interesting, but I am not convinced it is sufficiently rigorous or novel to support the strong claims made in the abstract and introduction. The key step is the reinterpretation of InfoNCE under non-independent negatives as optimizing a lower bound on MI-P minus expected MI-N. While this is intuitively plausible, the derivation relies on approximations and assumptions that are not carefully justified. In particular, the transition in Appendix A.2 from equation (18) to (19) depends on the claim in equation (21), namely that the positive density ratio dominates the average negative density ratio because false negatives are relatively rare. This is exactly the regime under dispute, and the paper does not quantify when this assumption holds. If false negatives are common or if the negative distribution is highly structured, the bound may be loose or misleading. More broadly, the “MI with negatives” interpretation seems closer to a reformulation of known density-ratio arguments than a fundamentally new theoretical result.

I am even less convinced by Appendix A.3. The claim that assigning weights satisfying two conditions “guarantees” proper MI optimization for negatives feels overstated. The proof appears to show that under certain assumptions, the weighted objective corresponds to a target expected MI involving the log inverse weights. But this does not establish that the chosen weights based on estimated similarity are accurate proxies for the desired MI target, nor that the optimization dynamics will realize the intended behavior in practice. The notion of a “controllable positive value” for MI-N is also vague. In practice, the weights are determined by a heuristic similarity estimator and a normalization function, not by a principled estimate of mutual information. So while the theory provides intuition, I do not think it rises to the level of a formal guarantee.

A major practical concern is the reliance on an auxiliary model H_beta trained on “high-quality human-annotated data” (MSCOCO, according to page 5). This raises several issues. First, the method is no longer purely self-supervised in the usual sense implied by the framing. It uses an additional supervised or at least curated in-domain resource to bootstrap similarity estimation. Second, this may introduce data leakage or unfair advantage depending on the downstream tasks and evaluation datasets, especially since MSCOCO is also used in retrieval evaluation and as part of pretraining data. Third, it complicates the method substantially: one must train and maintain a second model with the same architecture, then schedule interpolation between the auxiliary model and the main model. The paper does not adequately discuss the cost-benefit tradeoff here. How much of the gain comes from the weighting idea itself versus from injecting a better similarity prior from curated data? This is one of the most important missing ablations.

Relatedly, the empirical section lacks several critical controls. The paper compares mainly against ALBEF, but since the method modifies only the contrastive loss, stronger evidence would come from a more systematic ablation suite. For example, what happens if one uses only the current model’s similarity without H_beta? What if one uses a frozen CLIP model directly as the similarity estimator instead of training H_beta? What if one uses simpler heuristics such as caption lexical overlap, nearest-neighbor filtering, or temperature-adjusted soft labels? What if the weights are binary masks rather than continuous values? What if the normalization condition is removed? The current experiments do not disentangle these factors.

The comparison to prior work is also somewhat narrow. The paper mentions ALBEF and progressive self-distillation approaches, but the broader literature on debiased contrastive learning, false-negative mitigation, soft contrastive targets, and multi-positive contrastive objectives is not deeply engaged. In particular, the proposed weighting scheme is conceptually close to soft-label or soft-negative approaches where semantically similar negatives are downweighted. The paper should more clearly articulate what is genuinely new beyond applying similarity-based weighting in VLP with an MI-flavored justification. As written, the novelty seems incremental: a reasonable reweighting mechanism plus a theoretical interpretation.

Another concern is that the method may be sensitive to the quality and calibration of similarity estimates. The paper assumes that cross-modal similarity positively correlates with mutual information, which is plausible but not guaranteed. In practice, similarity models can be biased toward superficial correlations, frequent concepts, or dataset artifacts. If the similarity estimator mistakes true negatives for false negatives, the method may under-train important discriminative boundaries. The paper’s qualitative examples are encouraging, but they are not enough to establish robustness. I would have liked to see quantitative analysis of weight quality, for example precision/recall of low-weight negatives on a manually annotated subset, or at least a study of how often low-weight negatives are indeed semantically matching.

The experimental setup also leaves some ambiguity. The pretraining data are 5.1M image-text pairs from CC, SBU, COCO, and VG, but the paper later studies scaling to 12M examples in Figure 4; it is not clearly explained where the additional data come from. The notation is occasionally inconsistent as well: page 5 alternates between S_gamma, V_gamma, and F_gamma; equations (6) and (7) appear to swap indices or modalities in a confusing way; and there are several typographical issues throughout the paper. These are not fatal, but they make the method harder to verify and slightly undermine confidence in the theoretical sections.

I also think the claims around outperforming larger-scale models should be toned down. Table 4 shows strong zero-shot retrieval results, but comparing a method built on ALBEF with CLIP/ALIGN/FILIP is difficult because of differences in architecture, data scale, and evaluation protocol. The fairest takeaway is that SRCL improves ALBEF substantially and yields competitive zero-shot retrieval, not that it broadly surpasses those methods.

In terms of significance, the paper addresses an important issue and offers a practical fix that seems to work. If the method generalizes beyond ALBEF and does not depend critically on the auxiliary curated-data model, it could be useful to the community. But the current evidence is not yet sufficient to establish broad impact. The limitation section on page 9 acknowledges that evaluation is mainly on ALBEF; I agree this is a real limitation, not a minor one. Since the paper’s core claim is about contrastive learning itself, I would expect validation on at least one additional framework, ideally a CLIP-style dual encoder and perhaps another fusion-based VLP model.

My overall assessment is therefore mixed-positive. The paper has a clear motivation, a sensible method, and consistent empirical gains. But the theoretical claims are stronger than the evidence supports, and the empirical validation does not yet isolate the true source of improvement or establish generality. I would encourage the authors to revise the presentation to be more modest and to add stronger ablations.

The most important improvements I would request are the following. First, add ablations isolating the role of H_beta: no auxiliary model, frozen CLIP as auxiliary model, and different interpolation schedules. Second, evaluate on at least one additional VLP backbone to support the claim that the approach is generally applicable. Third, provide a more careful discussion of whether using COCO-trained H_beta introduces in-domain bias or leakage for downstream evaluation. Fourth, strengthen the empirical analysis of the weights themselves, ideally with manual inspection or annotation of suspected false negatives. Fifth, tone down the theoretical language from “guarantees” and “proper MI optimization” to “provides intuition” unless a more rigorous proof can be given. Sixth, improve clarity and correctness of notation and derivations in Section 2 and Appendix A.

If I were reviewing this for a conference, I would likely place it around the borderline accept range depending on the venue’s standards and the strength of the rebuttal. The practical idea is useful and the results are credible, but the paper currently overstates its theoretical novelty and under-analyzes its dependence on auxiliary supervision. With stronger ablations and a more careful framing, this could become a solid contribution.
