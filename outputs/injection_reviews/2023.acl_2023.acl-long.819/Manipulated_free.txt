This paper studies a real and important weakness of cross-modal contrastive learning for vision-language pretraining: the presence of false negatives and partial false negatives caused by many-to-many image-text correspondences. The core idea is to revisit InfoNCE from a mutual-information perspective under non-negligible false negatives, and then use cross-modal similarity to regulate how strongly negatives are repelled. Concretely, the paper derives a generalized lower-bound interpretation suggesting that, in noisy settings, InfoNCE effectively maximizes a lower bound on MI between positives minus expected MI with negatives, which can be problematic when some negatives are semantically related to the anchor. Motivated by this, the authors propose Similarity-Regulated Contrastive Learning (SRCL), which assigns each negative a weight inversely proportional to estimated cross-modal similarity, with the similarity estimate progressively transitioning from an auxiliary human-annotated model to the target VLP model itself. The method is implemented on top of ALBEF and evaluated on retrieval, zero-shot retrieval, VQA, and NLVR.

Overall, I found the paper strong, well-motivated, and technically interesting. The problem is highly relevant to modern VLP, especially because large-scale web data almost inevitably contains semantically overlapping image-text pairs that violate the clean-negative assumption behind standard contrastive learning. The paper’s framing is compelling: rather than treating false negatives as merely a heuristic nuisance, it analyzes them through the lens of mutual information optimization and uses that analysis to motivate a principled modification of the loss. This combination of theory, method, and empirical validation makes the work quite appealing.

One of the main strengths is the clarity of the problem formulation. The conceptual illustration in Figure 1 on page 1 is effective: it distinguishes true negatives, false negatives, and partial false negatives, and visually explains why uniformly pushing all negatives away can distort the representation space. This is a simple but important point, and the paper does a good job of arguing that existing VLP methods have not fully addressed it. The pilot result in Table 1 on page 2 is also persuasive: even a heuristic removal of suspected false negatives improves ALBEF on zero-shot retrieval and VQA, which supports the claim that the issue is practically meaningful rather than purely theoretical.

A second major strength is the theoretical angle. The derivation in Section 2, especially the generalized bound in Equation 3 on page 3, is an interesting contribution. The paper argues that when negatives are not independent of the anchor, InfoNCE should be interpreted as optimizing a lower bound on MI-P minus MI-N, not just MI-P. This is a useful reframing that helps explain why semantically related negatives can be over-penalized. The subsequent argument in Section 2.3, together with the appendix proofs on pages 12–13, gives a coherent rationale for introducing weighted negatives. Even if some readers may debate the exact tightness or assumptions of the derivation, the theoretical story is thoughtful and substantially stronger than a purely heuristic proposal.

The method itself is also elegant. SRCL modifies the denominator of the contrastive loss by multiplying each negative term with a regulation weight. The implementation is intuitive: more similar negatives receive smaller weights, so they are not pushed away as aggressively. I particularly liked the progressive refinement strategy in Section 3.2.1 on page 5, where similarity is initially estimated by a separate model trained on higher-quality human-annotated data and then gradually replaced by the target model’s own similarity estimates. This addresses a practical issue that many papers ignore: early in training, the model’s embedding space is too unstable to provide reliable similarity estimates. That design choice makes the method more credible and practically usable.

The empirical results are consistently strong. On image-text retrieval (Table 2, page 6), the method improves ALBEF across both COCO and Flickr30K, for both text retrieval and image retrieval. The gains are not enormous, but they are systematic and meaningful given that the architecture and pretraining setup are otherwise unchanged. On VQA and NLVR (Table 3, page 6), the method again improves over ALBEF, reaching 76.93 on VQA test-std and 83.95 on NLVR2 test-P. The zero-shot retrieval results in Table 4 are especially impressive: the method outperforms ALBEF and also compares favorably to larger-scale models such as CLIP, ALIGN, and FILIP despite using much less pretraining data. This is exactly the kind of result that supports the paper’s central claim that better handling of false negatives improves the semantic structure of the learned space.

I also appreciated the deeper analysis in Section 4.3 and 4.4. The discussion of false negatives versus hard negatives is nuanced and valuable. Figure 3 on page 7 is particularly informative: performance first improves when low-weight negatives are removed, then degrades when too many are removed. This supports the paper’s argument that some semantically close negatives are harmful if over-penalized, but negatives are still essential for contrastive learning. That is a more balanced and insightful conclusion than simply saying “remove false negatives.” Similarly, Figure 4 on page 7 shows that the advantage of SRCL grows with pretraining data size, which is an important observation because false-negative contamination should indeed become more severe as data scale increases. The qualitative retrieval examples on pages 8 and 16, along with the contrastive-weight visualizations on pages 17–18, further reinforce that the learned weighting behaves sensibly.

That said, there are a few limitations and questions that should be addressed, though none of them undermine the overall contribution. First, the theoretical derivation relies on several approximations and assumptions that deserve more careful discussion in the main paper. For example, the step from sums to expectations and the inequality conditions in Appendix A.2 and A.3 are plausible but somewhat informal. The claim that the weighted objective pushes MI-N to a “controllable positive value” is intuitively appealing, but the exact controllability seems to depend on the quality of the similarity estimator and the normalization scheme. I would encourage the authors to clarify more explicitly what is rigorously guaranteed versus what is heuristic but empirically effective.

Second, the method depends on an auxiliary model trained on a human-annotated dataset to estimate similarity in early training. This is a reasonable engineering choice, but it slightly complicates the narrative of a general-purpose pretraining method. It would be helpful to know how sensitive the method is to this auxiliary model, how large or clean that dataset must be, and whether simpler alternatives work nearly as well. For example, could one use only the target model with a delayed warm-up, or use CLIP similarities directly, or use intra-batch agreement heuristics? An ablation on this point would strengthen the paper further.

Third, while the paper compares against ALBEF and several external baselines, the proposed method is only instantiated on ALBEF. Since the claim is fairly general—namely, that cross-modal contrastive learning suffers from false negatives and should be similarity-regulated—it would be valuable to show at least one additional integration, perhaps with a CLIP-style dual encoder or another VLP framework. The limitation section on page 9 acknowledges this, and I agree with the authors that broader validation would make the contribution even more convincing. Still, the current evidence on ALBEF is already substantial.

Fourth, some implementation details around the weighting function could be clearer. Equation 9 on page 5 defines the weight as a normalized inverse similarity scaled by delta, but the exact normalization and the schedule for alpha are not fully specified in the main text. Since these choices may affect stability and performance, more detail or ablations would improve reproducibility. Likewise, it would be useful to report the computational overhead of maintaining the auxiliary model and computing the weighted similarities during pretraining.

There are also a few presentation issues. The paper is generally readable, but the writing could be polished in places for grammar and precision. Some notation is slightly inconsistent, and a few equations and variable references are harder to parse than necessary. For example, the notation around H_beta and S_gamma / V_gamma on page 5 could be cleaned up, and there are minor typographical issues throughout. These are easy fixes and do not detract much from the substance, but improving them would make the paper easier to follow.

Despite these minor concerns, I think the paper makes a meaningful contribution. It identifies an important failure mode in cross-modal contrastive learning, provides a principled MI-based interpretation of that failure mode, proposes a simple and effective remedy, and validates it across multiple downstream tasks with consistent gains. The work is especially compelling because it does not merely add another heuristic; it offers a conceptual lens that may influence how future VLP methods think about negatives, semantic overlap, and representation geometry.

In summary, this is a strong paper with solid motivation, an interesting theoretical perspective, a practical and elegant method, and convincing empirical results. The idea of regulating negative contrastive pressure by cross-modal similarity is both intuitive and well supported by the experiments. I would be positive on acceptance. My main suggestions for improvement are to tighten the theoretical exposition, add more ablations around the auxiliary similarity estimator and weighting schedule, and, if possible, demonstrate portability beyond ALBEF. Even without those additions, the paper is already quite valuable and should be of broad interest to the vision-language pretraining community.
