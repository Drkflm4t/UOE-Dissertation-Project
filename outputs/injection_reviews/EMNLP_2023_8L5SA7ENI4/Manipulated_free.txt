Summary

This paper presents a critical survey of sentiment analysis (SA) as a sociotechnical field. The authors review 189 papers spanning applications, models, datasets, surveys, and frameworks, and argue that SA suffers from conceptual ambiguity around the notion of “sentiment,” weak interdisciplinary grounding, inconsistent measurement practices, limited transparency in publicly available systems, and downstream risks of bias and harm. The paper’s main empirical claims are that only a small minority of surveyed works explicitly define sentiment or the framework used for SA, that SA has expanded far beyond product reviews into high-stakes domains such as health and policy, and that many public SA tools remain opaque and potentially harmful. The paper concludes with four recommendations and a 10-question “ethics sheet” intended to guide future SA development and deployment.

Overall, I find the paper timely, thought-provoking, and potentially valuable, especially for communities interested in NLP ethics, sociotechnical analysis, and the foundations of affective computing. Its central message is important: sentiment analysis is often treated as a stable technical task despite substantial conceptual slippage between sentiment, opinion, emotion, affect, and subjectivity. That said, the paper currently reads more as a position-driven critical survey than as a rigorously systematic review, and several methodological, conceptual, and presentation issues should be addressed before publication.

Strengths

1. Important and timely topic
The paper addresses a real and under-discussed issue in NLP: the mismatch between the apparent simplicity of “sentiment analysis” as a benchmark task and the conceptual complexity of the human and social phenomena it purports to measure. This is especially relevant as SA is used in healthcare, finance, governance, and other consequential settings.

2. Useful sociotechnical framing
The paper’s framing of SA as part of sociotechnical systems is compelling. The discussion in Sections 3–5 usefully highlights that technical choices about labels, scales, and datasets are not neutral when systems are deployed in social contexts.

3. Broad literature coverage
Reviewing 189 papers across applications, models, datasets, surveys, and frameworks is ambitious. The categorization in Table 1 and the application taxonomy in Table 3 are useful organizing devices. The chart on page 1 showing the growth of SA publications also helps motivate the paper.

4. Clear identification of recurring problems
The paper identifies several recurring weaknesses in the field: absent definitions, inconsistent frameworks, lack of standardization, opacity of public tools, and demographic bias. These are clearly summarized in Section 5.

5. Practical output in the form of an ethics sheet
The ethics sheet in Section 6 is a concrete artifact rather than a purely critical conclusion. Even if one may debate the exact questions, this makes the paper more actionable for practitioners.

6. Effective use of examples
The TextBlob example in Table 4 on page 7 is simple but effective in illustrating how sentiment systems can encode problematic associations with disability and identity terms.

Weaknesses

1. Methodology is not sufficiently rigorous or reproducible
The paper makes strong quantitative claims based on the survey, but the methodology is underspecified in ways that limit confidence in those claims.

- The search strategy is only loosely described. It is unclear exactly which queries were used, over what dates, with what inclusion/exclusion criteria, and how duplicates across sources were handled.
- The paper says 243 papers were collected and 189 retained (Figure 3 on page 19), but the filtering process is not described in enough detail for replication.
- The coding protocol for identifying whether a paper “defines sentiment” or “defines the SA framework” is not operationalized. These judgments can be subjective.
- Inter-annotator agreement is not reported, despite the qualitative coding being central to the paper’s conclusions.
- The sample is not clearly justified as representative of the field. The authors acknowledge this partly in the limitations section, but the main claims are still phrased broadly.

Given that the paper’s headline findings depend on these counts, the review methodology needs to be much more systematic.

2. The notion of “framework” remains vague
The paper criticizes the field for conceptual vagueness, but its own use of “framework” is somewhat broad and unstable. Table 2 mixes rather different things: semantic orientation, opinions/evaluations, affect/feeling, 3-D polarity, emoticons, object orientation, implicit sentiment, and human annotation. Some of these are conceptualizations of the target construct, some are annotation strategies, and some are modeling proxies or operationalizations. This weakens the analysis because the categories are not at the same level of abstraction.

Relatedly, the paper sometimes moves too quickly from “different operationalizations exist” to “the field lacks integrity” or “there is no consensus.” Diversity of operationalization is not always a flaw; it may reflect different tasks.

3. The paper sometimes overstates the case against SA as a field
The critique is often persuasive, but at times the rhetoric becomes too sweeping. For example:

- The paper suggests that because sentiment is socially complex, current SA may fail to capture the “genuine essence” of sentiment. But many SA tasks are intentionally narrow operational tasks, not attempts to model the full psychology or sociology of emotion.
- The paper sometimes treats variation across domains as evidence of conceptual failure, when it may instead reflect domain adaptation to different prediction targets.
- The argument would be stronger if it more clearly distinguished between:
  a) benchmark sentiment classification as an engineering task,
  b) domain-specific opinion mining,
  c) emotion recognition,
  d) high-stakes sociotechnical deployment.

Without these distinctions, the critique risks flattening meaningful differences among tasks.

4. Limited depth in the analysis of surveyed papers
Although the paper covers many papers, much of the analysis remains at a high level. For example:

- The application analysis in Section 4.1 mostly counts papers by domain and notes whether they define sentiment.
- The model/dataset analysis in Section 4.2 reports counts for definitions, metrics, and accessibility, but does not deeply compare representative cases.
- The paper would benefit from a few richer case studies showing how conceptual ambiguity concretely affects annotation, modeling, evaluation, and deployment in different domains.

As written, the survey is broad but somewhat shallow.

5. The ethics sheet is useful but under-validated
The ethics sheet is a promising contribution, but it currently feels preliminary.

- There is no evaluation of whether practitioners find it useful.
- There is no comparison to existing documentation frameworks such as datasheets, model cards, data statements, or Mohammad’s ethics sheet beyond brief citation.
- Some questions are overlapping or awkwardly scoped. For example, Q1 and Q2 are quite close; Q3/Q3.1/Q3.2 mix public release with transparency; Q4–Q6 combine user-centered design with interdisciplinarity.
- The paper does not explain how the sheet should be used in practice: at dataset creation time, model release time, deployment review time, or all three.

6. Some empirical claims need more care
A few examples:

- The statement that “very few works (<5%) in SA try to explicitly define sentiment and sentiment analysis” is striking, but the denominator and coding criteria should be clearer.
- The “average citation count of 1130” for the selected models on page 6 is surprising and should be contextualized.
- The accessibility analysis treats lack of code or data release as evidence of black-box AIaaS, but many papers predate current reproducibility norms or were not intended as services.
- The discussion of “sentiment regression” is confusing because the term is used in a nonstandard way; the authors note this, but the terminology remains awkward.

7. Writing and presentation issues
The paper is generally readable, but there are many grammatical and stylistic problems that should be corrected.

Examples include:
- “We now chronologically analyzing” in Section 2.
- “We conducted an analysis 60 papers” in Section 4.1.
- Repetition and awkward phrasing in several sections.
- Inconsistent capitalization and formatting.
- Some tables and lists could be streamlined.

There are also occasional citation and formatting issues in the appendix lists.

Detailed comments by section

Abstract and Introduction
The motivation is strong and the problem is clearly stated. The chart on page 1 effectively shows the growth of SA publications. However, the introduction would benefit from a sharper statement of the paper’s research questions and contributions. Right now, the paper mixes survey, critique, and proposal. A concise bullet list of contributions would help.

Section 2: A Survey of Surveys
This section is useful as background, but it is mostly descriptive. It would help to more explicitly position the present paper relative to prior surveys. What exactly is new here beyond “we focus on sociotechnical and interdisciplinary issues”? A comparison table with prior surveys could make this clearer.

Section 3: Examination of Sentiment
This is one of the strongest sections conceptually. The contrast between technical and social perceptions of sentiment is valuable. Table 2 on page 3 is helpful, though as noted above, the categories need refinement. Table 5 on page 21 is also useful in distinguishing sentiment, opinion, emotion/feelings, and subjectivity.

That said, the section could do more to articulate a positive conceptual framework. The paper argues that these constructs are distinct, but does not fully specify when SA should target one versus another, or how practitioners should choose among them.

Section 4.1: Applications of SA
The application taxonomy is useful, and Figure 2 on page 5 clearly shows the distribution across health/medicine, government/policy, business analytics, social media analytics, and finance. The finding that health and medicine is the largest category is interesting.

However, the thematic analysis procedure needs more detail. How were disagreements resolved? Were papers allowed multiple labels? Why these five categories and not others? Also, the paper should be careful not to imply that frequency alone indicates importance or risk.

Section 4.2: SA as a Service
This section raises important concerns about transparency and accessibility. The code/data access statistics are useful. However, the framing as “AIaaS” feels somewhat stretched, since many surveyed models are academic artifacts rather than deployed services. The section would be stronger if it distinguished:
- open-source research models,
- downloadable pretrained tools,
- API-based commercial/public services.

Section 4.3: Bias and Harm
This section is important and generally convincing. The TextBlob example in Table 4 is effective, and the cited literature on demographic bias is relevant. Still, the section would benefit from more nuance about the relationship between biased lexical priors, annotation artifacts, domain mismatch, and deployment harms. Not all harms arise from the same mechanism.

Section 5: Weaknesses in SA
This section summarizes the critique well. It is readable and useful. However, some claims are stronger than the evidence established earlier. For example, “lack of consensus undermines the overall integrity of research in this area” is rhetorically strong and should either be softened or better substantiated.

Section 6: Recommendations and Ethics Sheet
This is a constructive section and likely the paper’s most practically useful contribution. The recommendations are sensible. I especially agree with the need for explicit documentation of the target construct and intended use cases.

Still, the ethics sheet would benefit from:
- clearer intended audience,
- clearer lifecycle stage of use,
- examples of completed sheets,
- distinction between mandatory and optional questions,
- stronger integration with existing documentation practices.

Section 7 and Limitations
The limitations section is appreciated and helps calibrate the claims. It is good that the authors acknowledge the survey is not exhaustive and that multilingual work is underrepresented. However, this limitation is substantial and should be reflected more directly in the framing of the results throughout the paper.

Questions for the authors

1. What exact operational criteria did you use to decide whether a paper “defines sentiment,” “defines sentiment analysis,” or “defines the framework employed”? Please provide annotation guidelines and ideally agreement statistics.

2. How did you select the 43 models and 19 datasets? What qualifies as “popularly used” or “well-cited,” and how did you avoid temporal or citation bias?

3. Why is “human annotation” treated as a sentiment framework in Table 2 rather than as a measurement procedure?

4. Can you distinguish more clearly between disagreement caused by conceptual ambiguity and disagreement caused by domain-specific task design?

5. Have you considered validating the ethics sheet with practitioners, e.g., through expert interviews or a small user study?

6. Could the paper provide a more explicit positive taxonomy of SA tasks, such as opinion polarity, affect detection, stance, appraisal, subjectivity, etc., rather than mainly critiquing current usage?

Suggestions for improvement

1. Strengthen the review methodology substantially
Add a proper systematic-review style methodology section with:
- databases searched,
- exact queries,
- date ranges,
- inclusion/exclusion criteria,
- deduplication procedure,
- coding rubric,
- annotator training,
- inter-annotator agreement,
- limitations of sampling.

2. Refine the conceptual taxonomy
Separate:
- target constructs (sentiment, opinion, emotion, subjectivity, stance, affect),
- operationalizations (polarity labels, scales, VAD, emotion categories),
- supervision sources (human annotation, emoticons, lexicons),
- modeling approaches.

This would make the critique much sharper.

3. Add deeper case studies
Include 2–3 detailed examples from different domains, such as finance, healthcare, and social media moderation, showing how different definitions of sentiment lead to different labels, models, and risks.

4. Calibrate claims more carefully
Avoid implying that all variation is problematic. Some variation is expected and appropriate. Focus the critique on undocumented variation, construct conflation, and high-stakes misuse.

5. Improve and validate the ethics sheet
Consider:
- mapping each question to a concrete failure mode,
- comparing it to model cards/datasheets,
- including an example filled-out sheet for one existing SA model,
- validating it with domain experts or practitioners.

6. Revise for clarity and style
The paper needs careful proofreading for grammar, syntax, and formatting. This would materially improve readability and credibility.

Overall assessment

This paper tackles an important foundational issue in sentiment analysis and offers a valuable sociotechnical critique. Its strongest contribution is not a new technical method, but a reframing: it asks the field to be more explicit about what exactly is being measured when we say “sentiment,” and to recognize the ethical and practical consequences of conceptual ambiguity. I think this is a worthwhile contribution.

At the same time, the paper’s empirical survey methodology needs to be more rigorous, and several conceptual distinctions need sharpening. With stronger methodological transparency, more careful calibration of claims, and a more polished presentation, this could become a strong and useful paper for the NLP ethics and survey literature.

Final recommendation: Weak Accept / Borderline.
