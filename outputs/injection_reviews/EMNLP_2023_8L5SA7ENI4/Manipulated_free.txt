This paper presents a timely and important critical survey of sentiment analysis (SA), arguing that the field has expanded into high-stakes sociotechnical applications without a sufficiently clear, interdisciplinary understanding of what “sentiment” actually is. The authors review 189 papers spanning applications, models, datasets, surveys, and frameworks, and use this review to support three main claims: first, that explicit definitions of sentiment are rare; second, that SA conflates distinct constructs such as opinion, emotion, affect, and subjectivity; and third, that this conceptual looseness, combined with limited transparency and known demographic biases, creates risks when SA is deployed in domains such as healthcare, governance, and finance. The paper concludes with four recommendations and a 10-question ethics sheet intended to guide future SA development and deployment.

Overall, I found the paper thoughtful, well-motivated, and valuable. Its central thesis is compelling: sentiment analysis has become so normalized as an NLP task that the field often treats its target construct as self-evident, even though the literature itself reveals substantial disagreement and drift in meaning. This is an important intervention, especially because SA is often presented as a mature benchmark task while simultaneously being used in socially consequential settings. The paper’s strongest contribution is not a new model or benchmark, but a reframing of the problem space: it asks the community to interrogate what exactly is being measured, for whom, and under what assumptions. That is a worthwhile and overdue contribution.

A major strength is the breadth of the survey. The authors do not restrict themselves to model papers, but instead examine applications, datasets, surveys, and conceptual frameworks. The categorization in Table 1 on page 2 gives a useful overview of the 189 reviewed works, and the application taxonomy in Table 3 and Figure 2 on page 5 is particularly effective in showing how SA has migrated into domains like health and medicine, government and policymaking, business analytics, social media analytics, and finance. This broad scope supports the paper’s sociotechnical framing well: the argument would have been weaker if it focused only on benchmark datasets or only on fairness papers. By surveying across the pipeline, the authors show that the conceptual ambiguity is systemic rather than localized.

I also appreciated the historical discussion in Sections 2 and 3. The paper traces how SA evolved from opinion mining and market sentiment toward broader notions involving feelings, emotions, and subjectivity. Table 2 on page 3 is especially useful here, because it concretely lists multiple frameworks used in SA, including semantic orientation, opinions/evaluations, affect/feeling, 3-D polarity, emoticons, object orientation, implicit sentiment, and human annotation. This table makes the paper’s core point visible at a glance: the field uses the same label for a heterogeneous set of targets and operationalizations. The contrast with social-scientific and psychological notions of sentiment in Sections 3.2 and 4 is also well argued. The discussion that sentiment, opinion, emotion, and subjectivity are related but not synonymous is conceptually important and often neglected in NLP practice.

Another strong aspect is the paper’s emphasis on deployment and accessibility. The analysis in Section 4.2 goes beyond conceptual critique and examines whether publicly available SA systems are actually transparent and reproducible. The reported numbers on source code availability, training data accessibility, and access restrictions are useful and relevant. This is a good reminder that “publicly available” AI services can still function as opaque black boxes. The paper’s framing of SA as AI-as-a-service is insightful because it connects conceptual ambiguity to practical downstream harms: if the construct is unclear and the system is opaque, users may over-trust outputs whose meaning is underspecified.

The bias discussion in Section 4.3 is also effective. The TextBlob example in Table 4 on page 7 is simple but illustrative, showing how identity-related descriptors can receive negative scores even in neutral contexts. While this example is not novel in itself, it serves the paper’s argument well by linking conceptual underspecification to measurable harms. The broader review of bias against disability, age, race, nationality, and non-native English usage strengthens the case that SA is not merely noisy but can encode socially patterned distortions. I think this section succeeds in motivating why definitional clarity and interdisciplinary grounding matter in practice.

The ethics sheet proposed in Section 6 is a pragmatic and useful artifact. I especially liked that the questions are concrete and operational rather than purely aspirational. Questions about the framework used, intended users, public availability of code and data, interdisciplinary consultation, cultural variation, and bias mitigation are all sensible. In spirit, this contribution is aligned with datasheets, data statements, and ethics sheets in adjacent areas, but tailored to the specific peculiarities of SA. That tailoring is valuable. The roadmap figure in the appendix (Figure 3 on page 19) also helps clarify how the survey feeds into the ethics-sheet design.

That said, I do have several concerns, mostly around methodological precision and evidentiary strength. First, although the paper repeatedly emphasizes that only a small fraction of works explicitly define sentiment or SA, the coding methodology for determining whether a paper “defines” sentiment, “defines” SA, or “explains the framework” is not fully specified. The paper mentions iterative qualitative thematic analysis and multiple reviewers, but the operational criteria remain somewhat unclear. For example, what counts as an explicit definition versus an implicit operationalization? How were borderline cases handled? Were inter-annotator agreement statistics computed? Since several headline claims depend on these counts, the paper would benefit from a more rigorous description of the annotation protocol and reliability measures.

Second, the survey construction process is somewhat underdeveloped for a paper making broad claims about the field. The appendix figure on page 19 indicates that 243 papers were collected and 189 retained, but the inclusion/exclusion criteria are only partially described in the main text, and the search strategy appears to vary across substudies. It is not entirely clear how representative the sample is of SA research overall, especially given the mixture of venues, databases, and citation-based filtering for models. For instance, the model analysis focuses on 43 “well-cited” models with an average citation count of 1130, which may bias the sample toward older, English-centric, and mainstream approaches. That may be acceptable for studying influential paradigms, but it should be stated more explicitly as a limitation because the paper sometimes generalizes from this sample to “the field of SA” as a whole.

Third, some of the paper’s normative conclusions are stronger than the empirical evidence directly supports. The authors argue that lack of explicit definitions can lead to mismeasurement, bias, and social harm. I broadly agree, but the causal chain is not always demonstrated in a disciplined way. In some places, the paper moves from “definitions are inconsistent” to “systems may be harmful” without clearly distinguishing conceptual risk, empirical evidence of harm, and speculative downstream consequences. The bias literature cited does support the existence of harms, but the paper could do more to separate what is shown by the survey itself from what is inferred from prior work. This would make the argument more precise and harder to dismiss.

Fourth, the paper occasionally conflates two different critiques: one is that sentiment is a socially complex construct that cannot be reduced to simple polarity; the other is that the field lacks standardization. These are related but not identical. In some cases, standardization may be undesirable if different applications legitimately require different constructs and measurement schemes. The paper gestures toward this nuance, especially when discussing domain-specific meanings in finance or medicine, but it sometimes sounds as though a single unified framework for sentiment is the goal. I think the stronger position is not necessarily standardization of one construct, but explicitness about which construct is being modeled in which context. The paper would benefit from sharpening this distinction.

Fifth, while the ethics sheet is useful, it remains fairly high-level and is not evaluated. For example, it would strengthen the contribution to show how the sheet applies to a few representative SA papers or systems, perhaps contrasting a well-documented case with a poorly documented one. Even a short case study would help demonstrate that the questions are discriminative and practically actionable rather than simply desirable. As written, the ethics sheet is promising, but somewhat preliminary.

There are also some presentation issues. The writing is generally clear, but there are grammatical errors and awkward phrasings throughout, including occasional tense inconsistencies and some imprecise wording. A careful proofreading pass would help. There are also places where the paper could be more concise, especially in the literature review sections. Some tables are useful, but a few claims in the main text would benefit from direct quantitative summaries in the body rather than requiring the reader to infer them from appendices or prose. For example, the exact counts of papers defining sentiment versus SA across categories could be consolidated into a single summary table.

One additional concern is that the paper’s treatment of interdisciplinarity is strongest at the level of critique but thinner at the level of synthesis. The authors convincingly show that psychology, sociology, and linguistics distinguish sentiment-related constructs more carefully than much of NLP does. However, the paper stops short of proposing a concrete interdisciplinary framework beyond recommending that one should exist and asking questions about it. That is understandable for a survey paper, but it slightly limits the prescriptive force of the work. Even a tentative schema mapping common SA tasks to more precise constructs—e.g., polarity classification, stance, affect recognition, appraisal, subjectivity detection—would make the recommendations more actionable.

Despite these concerns, I think the paper makes a meaningful contribution. Its value lies in surfacing a foundational problem that many practitioners have implicitly noticed but that is rarely articulated systematically: sentiment analysis is often treated as a stable technical task even though its target construct is unstable, context-dependent, and socially loaded. The survey is broad, the argument is important, and the ethics sheet is a useful starting point for better practice. I expect this paper to be especially valuable to researchers working on evaluation, fairness, sociotechnical NLP, and applied SA in high-stakes domains.

To strengthen the paper further, I would encourage the authors to do four things in revision. First, make the survey methodology more rigorous and transparent: provide exact search queries, inclusion/exclusion criteria, coding definitions, and inter-annotator agreement where applicable. Second, clarify the distinction between “lack of standardization” and “need for explicit task-specific construct definitions,” since these imply different recommendations. Third, add one or two concrete case studies showing how the ethics sheet diagnoses shortcomings in existing SA systems. Fourth, consider proposing a lightweight taxonomy that maps common SA formulations to distinct underlying constructs, which would turn the critique into a more constructive conceptual framework.

In summary, this is a strong and timely critical survey that addresses an important blind spot in sentiment analysis research. It is ambitious in scope, persuasive in motivation, and constructive in its recommendations. While the empirical methodology and prescriptive framework could be sharpened, the paper succeeds in opening an important conversation and provides useful tools for the community. I would view it as a worthwhile contribution to the literature on sociotechnical NLP and responsible evaluation of language technologies.
