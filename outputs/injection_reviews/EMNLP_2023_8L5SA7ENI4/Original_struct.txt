Summary: This paper presents a critical survey of sentiment analysis (SA) from a sociotechnical perspective. The authors review 189 papers spanning applications, models, datasets, surveys, and frameworks, and argue that SA lacks clear, consistent, and interdisciplinary definitions of "sentiment." They contrast technical uses of sentiment with psychological/sociological notions, analyze 60 application papers, 43 models, and 19 datasets, and conclude that most works do not explicitly define sentiment or the SA framework they use. The paper further discusses accessibility/transparency issues in public SA systems, highlights known demographic and disability-related biases, and proposes an ethics sheet with 10 questions intended to guide future SA development and deployment.

Strengths: Timely and important topic: the paper addresses a real gap in NLP by questioning the conceptual foundations and sociotechnical implications of sentiment analysis.; Broad scope: the survey covers 189 papers across applications, models, datasets, surveys, and frameworks, giving a useful high-level map of the field.; Interdisciplinary framing is valuable: the comparison between computational, psychological, and sociological notions of sentiment is thought-provoking and relevant.; The paper identifies practically important concerns around transparency, accessibility, and demographic bias in deployed SA systems.; The proposed ethics-sheet style recommendations are actionable and may be useful for practitioners and reviewers.; The paper is clearly motivated and generally well organized, with helpful summary tables and appendices.

Weaknesses: The survey methodology is not rigorous enough for the strength of the claims made. Search strategy, inclusion/exclusion criteria, coding protocol, and inter-annotator agreement are insufficiently specified.; Many conclusions are descriptive and qualitative, but are presented with strong normative force without enough empirical grounding.; The paper conflates several distinct issues—definition ambiguity, measurement heterogeneity, transparency, and bias—without always establishing causal links between them.; The analysis of models/datasets is shallow relative to the breadth of the claims; counts of definitions and metrics are informative but do not by themselves demonstrate scientific invalidity of the field.; The proposed ethics sheet is sensible but not validated; there is no evidence that it improves practice, reliability, fairness, or interpretability.; Some terminology is imprecise or overstated, e.g., referring to score-based sentiment outputs as 'regression to the mean' is incorrect and conceptually confusing.; The paper occasionally overgeneralizes from selected examples (e.g., TextBlob outputs) to the broader SA landscape.; There are writing and presentation issues, including grammatical errors, duplicated references/categories, and occasional lack of precision in tables and claims.

Methodological Flaws: The literature review protocol is under-specified. The paper mentions sources such as SCOPUS, Semantic Scholar, ACL, NeurIPS, AAAI, and ACM, but does not provide reproducible search strings, date ranges, screening criteria, or a PRISMA-style accounting sufficient for replication.; The thematic analysis of 60 application papers lacks formal reliability reporting. Although the authors state that multiple authors iteratively classified papers, no inter-annotator agreement statistic or adjudication protocol is reported, weakening confidence in the derived categories.; The claim that '<5%' of SA works explicitly define sentiment appears to depend entirely on the authors' own coding scheme, but the operational definition of what counts as an explicit definition is not formalized. This makes the headline statistic potentially unstable.; The paper argues that lack of explicit definitions leads to bias and harm, but this causal inference is not demonstrated. Bias in SA systems may arise from data imbalance, annotation artifacts, model inductive biases, or deployment mismatch independent of whether a paper explicitly defines sentiment.; The accessibility analysis uses code availability, dataset availability, and paywall restrictions as proxies for transparency and explainability. These are related but not equivalent constructs; absence of code does not by itself imply a model is a 'black box' in the scientific sense.; The empirical demonstration using TextBlob (page 7, Table 4) is anecdotal and insufficient as evidence for broad claims. The sentence set is tiny, lacks controls, and does not compare across models, prompts, or contexts. It illustrates a concern but does not validate the paper's stronger conclusions.; The discussion of sentiment metrics groups heterogeneous label spaces (polarity, emotions, VAD, subjectivity) under one umbrella and treats their diversity primarily as a flaw. However, this heterogeneity may reflect different tasks rather than invalid measurement; the paper does not adequately distinguish task mismatch from methodological inconsistency.; There is a mathematical/scientific terminology error: the paper states that by 'sentiment regression' it refers to 'regression to the mean' techniques applied in measurement, not machine learning regression. This is incorrect usage and undermines technical precision.; The recommendation that explainability 'can be done by making sure the training data and code of the model are available to all' is scientifically weak: openness aids auditability and reproducibility, but does not itself provide explainability of model decisions.; The survey does not sufficiently control for temporal effects. Older SA papers naturally used simpler polarity formulations and weaker documentation norms; the paper's aggregate critique may therefore conflate historical evolution with current practice.
