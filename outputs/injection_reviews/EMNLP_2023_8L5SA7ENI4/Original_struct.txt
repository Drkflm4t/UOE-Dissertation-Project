Summary: This paper presents a critical survey of sentiment analysis (SA) from a sociotechnical and interdisciplinary perspective. Reviewing 189 papers spanning applications, models, datasets, surveys, and frameworks, the authors argue that SA lacks clear and consistent definitions of 'sentiment' and often conflates sentiment with opinion, emotion, subjectivity, or tone. They compare technical notions of sentiment with psychological and sociological perspectives, then analyze 60 application papers, 43 models, and 19 datasets to show limited definitional clarity, inconsistent measurement schemes, restricted transparency/accessibility, and documented demographic biases. Based on these findings, they propose four recommendations and a 10-question ethics sheet intended to guide more responsible development and deployment of SA systems.

Strengths: Timely and important topic: the paper addresses a real gap in NLP by interrogating the conceptual foundations and sociotechnical implications of sentiment analysis rather than only model performance.; Broad survey scope: the review covers 189 papers across multiple categories, including applications, models, datasets, surveys, and frameworks.; Useful interdisciplinary framing: the comparison between computational, psychological, and sociological notions of sentiment is valuable and helps motivate the critique.; The paper identifies concrete empirical patterns in the literature, such as the rarity of explicit definitions, heterogeneity of sentiment metrics, and limited code/data accessibility.; The ethics sheet is a practical artifact that could be useful for practitioners, reviewers, and downstream users of SA systems.; The discussion of bias and harm is well motivated and supported by examples from prior literature as well as a simple illustrative TextBlob example.

Weaknesses: The paper is primarily descriptive and argumentative; the methodology for paper selection, coding, and analysis is not rigorous enough for some of the stronger claims made.; The survey protocol lacks sufficient detail for reproducibility: search strings, inclusion/exclusion criteria, time windows, inter-annotator agreement, and coding procedures are only partially described.; Several conclusions are based on counting whether papers define sentiment/frameworks, but the operational criteria for what qualifies as a definition or framework are not sharply specified.; The proposed ethics sheet is sensible but not validated; there is no user study, expert evaluation, or evidence that it improves practice.; Some arguments overreach from observed inconsistency to claims about lack of integrity or unsuitability of SA in sociotechnical settings without a sufficiently systematic causal demonstration.; The accessibility analysis is somewhat shallow: code/data availability and paywalls are useful indicators, but they do not fully characterize transparency, documentation quality, or deployability.; The paper occasionally conflates the need for standardization with the possibility that different tasks legitimately require different sentiment formalisms.; There are writing and presentation issues, including grammatical errors, repetition, and some imprecision in terminology, which weaken the polish of the paper.

Soundness Issues: The literature review methodology is not fully systematic. Although the authors report counts and categories, the search and filtering process is not documented at the level expected for a rigorous systematic review.; The thematic analysis of applications is plausible, but no inter-annotator agreement statistics or formal coding reliability measures are reported, limiting confidence in the categorization.; The claim that fewer than 5% of works explicitly define sentiment/SA depends heavily on subjective coding decisions that are not sufficiently formalized.; The TextBlob case study is illustrative but not a strong methodological basis for broader claims about harms of SA systems; it functions more as anecdotal evidence than systematic evaluation.; The recommendation for standardized sentiment measures is only partially justified; task diversity may warrant multiple measurement schemes, so the paper should distinguish harmful ambiguity from legitimate task variation.; The paper argues that lack of explicit definitions leads to bias and harm, which is plausible, but the causal chain is not empirically established within the study itself.
