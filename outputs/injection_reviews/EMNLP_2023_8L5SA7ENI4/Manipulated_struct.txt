Summary: This paper presents a critical survey of sentiment analysis (SA) from a sociotechnical perspective. The authors review 189 peer-reviewed papers spanning applications, models, datasets, surveys, and frameworks, and argue that SA lacks a coherent interdisciplinary definition of "sentiment." They contrast technical usages of sentiment with psychological, sociological, and linguistic notions, then analyze 60 application papers, 43 models, and 19 datasets to show that explicit definitions, standardized measurement frameworks, and transparency are often missing. The paper further discusses bias and harm in deployed SA systems and proposes four recommendations plus a 10-question ethics sheet intended to guide future SA development and deployment.

Strengths: Timely and important topic: the paper addresses a foundational but under-examined issue in NLP, namely the conceptual ambiguity of "sentiment" and its consequences in real-world sociotechnical deployments.; Broad scope: the survey covers 189 papers across applications, models, datasets, surveys, and frameworks, giving the paper substantial breadth.; Interdisciplinary framing is a major contribution: the paper usefully contrasts NLP notions of sentiment with perspectives from psychology, sociology, and linguistics.; The paper identifies a concrete empirical pattern: very few surveyed works explicitly define sentiment or the sentiment-analysis framework they use.; The analysis of applications is useful and well organized, especially the thematic categorization into health/medicine, government/policy, business analytics, social media analytics, and finance.; The discussion of transparency and accessibility of public SA systems is valuable, especially the examination of code availability, dataset accessibility, and access restrictions.; The paper highlights fairness and bias concerns with relevant examples and connects them to broader concerns in AI ethics.; The proposed ethics sheet is practical and actionable, and is likely to be useful for both researchers and practitioners.; The paper is clearly motivated and the overall narrative—from conceptual ambiguity to downstream harms to recommendations—is coherent and compelling.; The work makes a meaningful community-level contribution by encouraging more explicit task definitions, documentation, and interdisciplinary collaboration in SA.

Weaknesses: The survey methodology is only partially systematic: search strategy, inclusion/exclusion criteria, and screening protocol are described at a high level, but not with enough rigor for full reproducibility.; Some quantitative claims are based on manual qualitative coding, yet inter-annotator agreement statistics are not reported.; The paper sometimes moves from observations about heterogeneity in definitions to stronger normative conclusions without fully establishing when diversity is harmful versus appropriate for domain-specific tasks.; The analysis of models and datasets is descriptive rather than deeply comparative; for example, there is limited breakdown by time period, task type, language, or modeling paradigm.; The empirical bias demonstration using TextBlob is illustrative but limited; it is not a systematic experiment and does not support broad causal claims on its own.; The proposed ethics sheet is sensible, but it is not validated through user studies, expert evaluation, or case studies showing that it improves practice.; Some terminology is occasionally conflated in the paper itself (e.g., sentiment, emotion, opinion, subjectivity are distinguished conceptually, but parts of the critique aggregate them at a broad level).; The paper acknowledges limited multilingual coverage, which reduces the generality of its conclusions for global SA research.

Methodological Flaws: The literature review is not conducted as a fully systematic review: the paper does not provide sufficient detail on database coverage, exact search strings, deduplication, screening stages, or exclusion rationale to ensure reproducibility and reduce selection bias.; The thematic categorization of application papers relies on iterative qualitative analysis, but no inter-rater reliability or adjudication statistics are reported; this weakens confidence in the stability of the categories and counts.; The claim that '<5%' of works explicitly define sentiment/SA is important, but the operational criteria for what counts as a definition versus a framework mention are not formalized, making this result somewhat subjective.; The accessibility/transparency analysis treats absence of public code or data as evidence of black-box behavior, but this inference is not always scientifically warranted; some systems may be well documented without open release, so the logic overreaches.; The section labeled 'sentiment regression' is conceptually imprecise: it refers to scalar scoring schemes rather than regression modeling per se, which may confuse measurement scale with learning methodology.; The TextBlob case study on biased outputs is anecdotal and lacks controls, statistical analysis, or comparison across models; therefore it demonstrates possibility rather than prevalence or severity of bias.; The paper argues for standardization of sentiment measurement, but does not sufficiently justify why standardization is preferable to domain-specific operationalization in all or most settings; this is a scientific logic gap in the normative recommendation.; No formal evaluation is provided for the proposed ethics sheet, so claims about its practical effectiveness remain unverified.
