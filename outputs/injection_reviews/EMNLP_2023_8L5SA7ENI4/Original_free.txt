This paper presents a critical survey of sentiment analysis (SA) through a sociotechnical lens. The authors review 189 papers spanning applications, models, datasets, surveys, and frameworks, and argue that the field has evolved rapidly into high-impact social domains without a sufficiently explicit, interdisciplinary understanding of what “sentiment” actually is. The paper’s central claims are that SA research often leaves sentiment undefined, conflates sentiment with neighboring constructs such as opinion, emotion, and subjectivity, uses inconsistent measurement schemes, and deploys opaque or biased systems in socially consequential settings. To address this, the paper proposes four recommendations and a ten-question “ethics sheet” intended to guide future SA development and use.

Overall, I find the paper timely, important, and intellectually valuable. Its main contribution is not a new algorithm or benchmark, but a reframing of sentiment analysis as a conceptually unstable and ethically consequential area of NLP. That is a worthwhile contribution, especially given the widespread use of SA in healthcare, finance, governance, and social media analytics. The paper is strongest when it synthesizes technical and social-scientific perspectives and shows that the field’s operationalizations of sentiment are often underspecified relative to the claims made about what is being measured. The paper is also commendably ambitious in scope: it surveys applications, models, datasets, and bias literature, and it attempts to convert critique into practical guidance via the ethics sheet.

A major strength is the paper’s motivating premise. The authors correctly observe that SA has expanded far beyond product reviews and market signals into domains where outputs may influence decisions about people, institutions, and public policy. The bar chart on page 1 showing the growth of SA publications in SCOPUS helps motivate the scale of the field, and the thematic breakdown of applications on page 5 usefully illustrates that health/medicine and government/policy are prominent application areas. This supports the paper’s argument that conceptual looseness is no longer a merely academic issue. If SA is used in sociotechnical systems, then ambiguity about what is being measured becomes a substantive validity problem.

Another strength is the paper’s conceptual analysis in Sections 3 and 5. The comparison between technical and social perceptions of sentiment is useful and, in my view, the most original part of the paper. Table 2 on page 3 is especially effective in showing the heterogeneity of frameworks used in SA, ranging from semantic orientation and opinions/evaluations to affect, emoticons, implicit sentiment, and human annotation. The discussion on pages 3–4 then argues persuasively that these are not interchangeable constructs. The summary table on page 21, contrasting definitions of sentiment, opinion, emotion/feelings, and subjectivity, reinforces this point clearly. This is an important intervention because many NLP papers do indeed slide between these terms without acknowledging the theoretical consequences.

The paper is also strong in its critical discussion of measurement inconsistency. The analysis of metrics in Section 4.2 highlights that “sentiment” is operationalized using a wide variety of label spaces and scales, including binary polarity, ternary polarity, five-point scales, emotion categories, valence-arousal-dominance, and regression-like scalar scores. The appendix on page 20 makes this diversity concrete. The authors are right that these are not simply different parameterizations of the same latent variable. This observation has implications for comparability, benchmarking, and construct validity, and the paper is right to foreground that.

The discussion of bias and harm is another positive aspect. The TextBlob example in Table 4 on page 7 is simple but effective as an illustration of how identity-related descriptors can receive systematically negative scores. The paper also appropriately connects this to prior work on demographic bias, disability bias, age bias, and non-native English bias. While none of these findings are individually new, the paper’s contribution is to tie them back to the conceptual instability of SA and to the risks of deploying such systems as black-box services.

I also appreciate the attempt to move from diagnosis to intervention. The recommendations in Section 6 are sensible: interdisciplinary grounding, explicit documentation, clear use-case specification, and transparency/explainability are all appropriate. The ethics sheet is practical and likely to be useful pedagogically or as supplementary documentation. In particular, questions about the framework used, intended users, public accessibility, bias testing, and cultural/contextual variation are well chosen.

That said, I have several substantial concerns, mostly about methodological rigor, evidentiary support, and the precision of some claims.

My biggest concern is that the survey methodology is not described with enough rigor for a paper whose main contribution rests on empirical claims about the literature. The paper states that it reviews 189 peer-reviewed papers and gives category counts in Table 1 on page 2, but the inclusion/exclusion criteria, search strategy, time boundaries, deduplication process, and coding protocol are only partially specified. For example, the application study says 95 papers were collected and 35 excluded, leaving 60, but analogous details are not given with equal clarity for the full 189-paper corpus. It is also not fully clear how categories overlap or whether they are mutually exclusive. The roadmap figure in the appendix (page 19) helps at a high level, but for a survey making quantitative claims such as “very few works (<5%) explicitly define sentiment,” the methodology needs to be much more reproducible. I would expect a clearer PRISMA-style accounting, explicit search strings, date ranges, venue lists, annotation guidelines, and inter-annotator agreement statistics for the qualitative coding.

Relatedly, some of the paper’s numerical claims feel under-supported or potentially unstable because the coding criteria are not sufficiently operationalized. What counts as “explicitly defining sentiment”? What counts as “explaining the employed framework”? These are central variables in the paper’s argument, yet the distinction between a definition, a framework, and an operationalization is not always sharp. For instance, some papers may not define sentiment philosophically but may still specify a task formulation precisely enough for their intended use. The paper tends to treat absence of explicit conceptual discussion as a deficiency, which is often fair, but the coding should distinguish between lack of theoretical definition and lack of task specification. Without that distinction, the critique risks overgeneralizing.

A second major concern is that the paper sometimes moves too quickly from heterogeneity to normative deficiency. It is certainly true that SA encompasses multiple constructs and task formulations. But not all heterogeneity is necessarily a problem. In some cases, different domains may legitimately require different operationalizations: financial sentiment, review polarity, stance toward policy, affective state, and emotion recognition are not the same task, and perhaps should not be forced into a single standardized framework. The paper acknowledges domain variation, but its argument occasionally implies that the field ought to converge on a unified notion of sentiment. I think the stronger and more defensible claim is not that SA needs one universal definition, but that each study should explicitly state which construct it targets, why that construct is appropriate for the domain, and what limitations follow. The paper would benefit from sharpening this distinction. Otherwise, it risks criticizing pluralism per se rather than underspecification.

A third concern is that the paper’s treatment of “social perception” of sentiment, while valuable, is somewhat selective and occasionally too coarse. The authors draw on psychology, sociology, and linguistics, but the synthesis remains relatively high-level. There is room for a more careful treatment of construct validity: what exactly is the latent construct in each SA setting, how is it observed, and what evidence would support validity? The paper gestures toward this issue but does not fully develop it. In particular, the critique would be stronger if framed using established measurement language from psychometrics or HCI, such as construct validity, content validity, convergent/discriminant validity, and measurement invariance across groups. That would give the paper a firmer analytical backbone than the current broader claim that sentiment is “complex” and “interdisciplinary.”

I also found the analysis of accessibility and transparency somewhat uneven. The paper reports percentages of models with source code and training data availability, and notes that some systems are behind paywalls or require credentials. This is useful, but the framing as “AI as a Service” is not always fully aligned with the surveyed artifacts. Many cited works are research papers proposing models or datasets, not necessarily deployed services. The paper would be stronger if it separated open research artifacts from commercial or API-based SA systems, since the transparency expectations and constraints differ. As written, the section sometimes conflates public availability of a paper with public accessibility of a service.

The bias discussion, while important, is also somewhat broad relative to the paper’s own evidence. The TextBlob example is illustrative, but it is only one model and one small set of prompts. Since the paper’s main contribution is a survey, that is acceptable, but then the claims should be framed more carefully as synthesis of prior evidence rather than as a demonstrated empirical pattern from the paper itself. More generally, the paper sometimes attributes harms to “lack of explanation” or “lack of interdisciplinary framing” when the causal pathway is more complex. Bias can arise from data imbalance, annotation practices, lexical priors, model architecture, deployment mismatch, or social context, not only from conceptual ambiguity. The paper should avoid implying a stronger causal link than it establishes.

There are also some issues of organization and writing quality. The paper is generally readable, but there are grammatical errors, awkward phrasings, and occasional imprecision. For example, Section 2 begins with “We now chronologically analyzing various surveys,” which should be corrected. There are several places where singular/plural agreement or article usage is off. Some terminology is used inconsistently, including “framework,” “definition,” “metric,” and “measurement.” The paper would benefit from careful copyediting. In addition, some sections feel repetitive: the claim that sentiment is vaguely defined and conflated with adjacent constructs appears multiple times with limited incremental development.

I also think the ethics sheet, while useful, is currently more of a checklist than a validated framework. That is not inherently a problem, but the paper should present it modestly. At present, the ethics sheet is proposed as a practical solution, yet there is no evaluation of whether it improves documentation quality, user understanding, or model selection. Even a small expert study, case analysis, or retrospective application of the sheet to a few representative SA papers would strengthen this contribution considerably. As it stands, the ethics sheet is promising but preliminary.

A further issue is scope. The limitations section acknowledges underrepresentation of multilingual work, but this is not a minor omission. Since the paper’s central argument concerns contextuality, culture, and sociotechnical deployment, the relative absence of multilingual and cross-cultural analysis weakens the universality of its conclusions. The same applies to multimodal sentiment analysis, speech-based affect analysis, and non-English social media contexts. If the paper remains scoped to mainstream English-language text SA, it should say so more explicitly and avoid broader claims where unsupported.

I also wonder whether the paper could do more to distinguish sentiment analysis from neighboring tasks such as stance detection, emotion recognition, toxicity detection, and subjectivity analysis. The paper criticizes the field for conflating these constructs, but at times the survey itself groups them together as evidence of conceptual drift. A more systematic taxonomy would help: for example, define a hierarchy of tasks, indicate where SA historically sits, and show how datasets/models have migrated across boundaries. This would make the critique more precise and more actionable.

In terms of novelty, the paper’s contribution is primarily synthetic and normative. It is not the first to argue that SA is conceptually messy or ethically fraught; the paper itself cites Mohammad (2022), Stark and Hoey (2021), and broader critical NLP work. However, I do think this paper adds value by assembling evidence across applications, models, datasets, and bias studies into one argument focused specifically on sentiment. The breadth is useful. The main question for acceptance is therefore not whether the topic matters—it clearly does—but whether the survey methodology and analysis are rigorous enough for a top-tier venue. In its current form, I am not fully convinced they are.

To strengthen the paper, I would recommend the following concrete revisions. First, substantially improve the survey methodology section. Provide exact search queries, databases, date ranges, venue filters, deduplication rules, inclusion/exclusion criteria, category definitions, and inter-annotator agreement. A transparent appendix or repository is good, but the paper itself should contain enough detail to assess rigor. Second, refine the central claim from “SA lacks a unified definition” to “SA often lacks explicit construct specification appropriate to domain and use case.” This would avoid overclaiming and better accommodate legitimate task diversity. Third, introduce a more formal construct-validity framework, perhaps borrowing from psychometrics, measurement theory, or HCI. Fourth, separate analysis of research artifacts from analysis of deployed services. Fifth, evaluate the ethics sheet in some way, even if only through expert feedback or application to a few case studies. Sixth, tighten the writing and reduce repetition.

My overall assessment is positive about the paper’s goals and significance, but mixed about its current execution. The paper raises important questions that the NLP community should take seriously, and it offers a useful conceptual critique of sentiment analysis as currently practiced. However, because its strongest claims depend on a literature analysis that is not yet methodologically robust enough, and because some arguments overgeneralize from heterogeneity to deficiency, I would currently view it as promising but not fully mature.

If I were reviewing this for a conference, I would likely recommend a borderline reject or weak reject in its present form, while emphasizing that the paper has clear potential and could become a strong contribution with methodological tightening and sharper framing. The core idea is compelling, the topic is important, and the paper is constructive rather than merely critical. With stronger empirical rigor and more precise argumentation, it could make a meaningful contribution to responsible NLP research on sentiment analysis.
