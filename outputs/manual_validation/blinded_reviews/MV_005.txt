Review ID: MV_005
Validation Track: RQ1
============================================================

Summary: The paper studies privacy-preserving activation steering for LLM alignment. It proposes PSA, a simple method that computes mean steering vectors from paired positive/negative demonstrations, clips per-example activation differences, and adds Gaussian noise so that the released steering vectors satisfy differential privacy. The private vectors are then reused at inference time for arbitrarily many queries via post-processing. The paper evaluates PSA on seven alignment-related multiple-choice benchmarks across several open-source instruction-tuned LLMs (mainly 2B–7B), compares against zero-shot, mean steering, and PCA steering, and reports modest utility degradation. It also introduces a canary-based membership inference attack tailored to activation steering and shows empirically reduced attack success under PSA.

Strengths: Timely and novel problem formulation: bringing differential privacy to inference-time activation steering is an interesting and underexplored direction.; Method is simple, easy to implement, and computationally lightweight compared with DP fine-tuning; the offline privatization of steering vectors is practically appealing.; The privacy argument is conceptually clean: privatize the released steering vectors once, then rely on post-processing for unlimited downstream queries.; Broad empirical coverage across seven alignment behaviors and multiple model families/sizes, with both alignment and general-capability evaluations.; The paper includes an empirical privacy audit via a tailored canary-based MIA, which is a useful contribution for this setting.; Ablations on noise scale, clipping threshold, and model scaling provide some insight into the privacy-utility tradeoff.

Weaknesses: The DP accounting and mechanism specification are underspecified/inconsistent in important ways, weakening confidence in the claimed guarantees.; The noise calibration appears disconnected from the chosen clipping thresholds and from the dimensionality/layerwise release, making it unclear whether the implemented mechanism matches the stated theorem.; Empirical evaluation is narrow in several respects: mostly one steering strength, one fixed noise level in main experiments, limited reporting of variance/significance, and limited analysis of sensitivity to layer selection.; The qualitative evaluation relies on GPT-4-as-judge with prompts that are not obviously calibrated or validated; reported differences are often very small and may not be statistically meaningful.; The MIA is highly specialized to synthetic canaries and threshold tuning, so it is not clear how representative it is of realistic privacy leakage from activation steering.; Some claims are overstated, e.g., that PSA achieves DP with minimal loss in performance broadly, despite several datasets/models showing noticeable drops and some privacy budgets being fairly weak (e.g., total epsilon up to 6.6).

Methodological Flaws: There is a likely mathematical inconsistency in Algorithm 1 / Fact 2.1. After clipping and scaling, each processed vector has norm at most 1, so replacing one record changes the average by at most 2/n. The Gaussian mechanism should therefore add noise with standard deviation proportional to (2/n)*sqrt(2 ln(1.25/delta))/epsilon. However, Equation (3) / Algorithm 1 states v_l^priv = (1/n) sum dbar_i^l + N(0, sigma^2) with sigma = 2 sqrt(2 ln(1.25/delta)) / (n epsilon), omitting any dependence on the clipping threshold C_l because vectors are divided by C_l. Yet the implementation section says they add Gaussian noise with fixed standard deviation 0.02 and then infer epsilon from dataset size. This creates ambiguity about whether the actual experiments use the theorem-calibrated sigma or an ad hoc fixed sigma.; The notation for the Gaussian mechanism is imprecise for vector-valued outputs. N(0, sigma^2) is written as if scalar, but the steering vector is d-dimensional; the covariance structure should be specified as isotropic Gaussian in R^d. This matters for reproducibility and formal correctness.; The privacy composition argument only accounts for releasing one vector per selected layer, but the paper does not discuss hyperparameter tuning/privacy leakage from selecting layers, clipping thresholds, or steering strengths using private data. If these choices were informed by private validation, the stated privacy budget would be incomplete.; The threat model claims protection of each demonstration record, but each record is a paired triple (p_i, c_i^+, c_i^-). The neighboring relation is not fully formalized for this structured record, and the privacy implications of repeated prompts or correlated pairs are not discussed.; The empirical privacy metric epsilon_empirical is derived from FPR/FNR of a specific attack, but comparing this directly to theoretical DP epsilon can be misleading. Failure of one attack does not imply stronger privacy in the DP sense; the paper occasionally phrases this too strongly.; The MIA evaluation uses synthetic canaries inserted into demonstrations and thresholds chosen per model (tau=40 for Llama, 70 for Qwen). This attack may mainly detect direct lexical anchoring rather than broader memorization/privacy leakage from steering vectors, limiting the validity of conclusions about real-world privacy.; Utility evaluation on MMLU is reduced to an A/B reformulation and average probability of the correct answer, which is a weaker and somewhat nonstandard proxy for general reasoning performance; this may mask broader degradation.; No confidence intervals, repeated-run statistics, or hypothesis tests are reported for the main utility results, despite stochastic generation, noisy privatization, and small differences between PSA and baselines. This weakens empirical validity of claims of comparable performance.
