Review ID: MV_037
Validation Track: RQ1
============================================================

This paper tackles an important and timely problem in NLI evaluation: standard benchmarks such as SNLI and MultiNLI are known to contain annotation artifacts and spurious correlations that make reported accuracy an unreliable proxy for genuine inference ability. The core idea is to automatically characterize existing test examples by difficulty using training dynamics, rather than constructing synthetic adversarial examples by hand. Concretely, the authors extend dataset cartography by collecting eight training-dynamics features per instance from two settings—premise+hypothesis and hypothesis-only—and then clustering examples with a Gaussian mixture model into easy, ambiguous, and hard subsets. They evaluate this on SNLI, MultiNLI, and FEVER, and also study whether the same characterization can be used to filter training data.

Overall, I found the paper interesting, well motivated, and empirically substantial. The problem is real, the proposed method is intuitive yet nontrivial, and the results are compelling enough to suggest that the approach captures meaningful variation in example difficulty and artifact prevalence. The paper’s strongest contribution is not merely another stress test, but a methodology for deriving a more challenging and arguably more realistic evaluation subset from existing data. That is a useful perspective, especially because much prior work either builds synthetic challenge sets or relies on expensive human-in-the-loop collection. Here, the authors aim to preserve natural examples while reducing shortcut-driven evaluation.

The technical proposal is clear. The workflow shown in the diagram on page 3 is easy to follow: train on the target split for a small number of epochs in two regimes, extract confidence, variability, correctness, and AUM in each regime, concatenate these into an 8-dimensional feature vector, and cluster with a 3-component GMM. This is a sensible generalization of data maps. I particularly appreciated the inclusion of the hypothesis-only dynamics, since that directly operationalizes a known source of NLI artifacts. That design choice makes the method more tailored to NLI than a generic training-dynamics approach, and it helps explain why the resulting hard subsets appear to suppress hypothesis-only shortcuts.

The empirical evidence is generally strong. Table 1 shows the expected hypothesis-only bias on SNLI and MultiNLI, while FEVER behaves differently, which already supports the paper’s framing. Table 3 is one of the most convincing results: performance drops monotonically across easy, ambiguous, and hard splits for both SNLI and MultiNLI, and the hypothesis-only model falls close to chance on the hard subsets. That is exactly what one would hope from a characterization intended to isolate artifact-light, genuinely difficult examples. The figures on page 6 are also informative. Figure 2 shows that the clusters are meaningfully separated in terms of confidence, variability, margin, and correctness. Figure 3 further strengthens the claim by showing that heuristic measures of spurious correlations diminish in the hard split, and the statistical testing discussed on page 7 suggests that class-specific heuristic differences largely disappear there. This is a nice triangulation: the hard split is not only harder for models, but also less obviously structured by known shortcuts.

I also think the cross-model analysis is a meaningful addition rather than a superficial robustness check. Table 4 indicates that splits derived from RoBERTa and DeBERTa transfer reasonably across models, especially in preserving the relative hardness of the subsets. This supports the claim that the characterization is not merely overfitting to one encoder’s idiosyncrasies. The paper is careful not to overclaim perfect model invariance, which is appropriate.

The training-set characterization experiments are another strength. Tables 5 and 6 suggest that selecting subsets based on the proposed characterization can match or nearly match full-data performance on the Naik et al. stress test while using substantially less data, and often outperform simpler baselines such as Data Maps and AUM. Even if this is somewhat secondary to the main test-set contribution, it increases the practical value of the work. It suggests that the method is not only diagnostic but potentially useful for data curation and efficient training.

The qualitative examples on page 12 are helpful and align with the quantitative story. In SNLI, some easy contradiction examples indeed look suspicious or mislabeled, while harder examples involve subtler distinctions and more plausible neutral cases. In MultiNLI, easy examples often reduce to simple negation or paraphrase patterns, whereas hard examples require more nuanced interpretation. This kind of qualitative grounding is important for a paper making claims about dataset quality and difficulty.

That said, there are several issues that should be addressed or at least discussed more carefully.

The biggest conceptual concern is the decision to train on the test set in order to characterize it. The authors are explicit that this is done only to collect training dynamics and not to report classifier performance, but this still raises methodological questions. Difficulty here is defined partly in terms of how quickly a model can fit the examples when directly trained on them. That is a legitimate operationalization, but it differs from the usual notion of test difficulty as out-of-sample generalization challenge. In other words, the method may identify examples that are hard to memorize or optimize under a particular fine-tuning setup, not necessarily examples that are intrinsically hard for inference. The paper partly mitigates this concern through cross-model transfer and heuristic analyses, but the issue deserves a more direct discussion. For example, it would help to clarify whether similar splits emerge if training dynamics are collected from a held-out model trained on the training set and then tracked over the test set without fitting on it.

Relatedly, the claim that the hard split provides “a more accurate measure of model performance in the real world” is somewhat stronger than the evidence supports. The paper convincingly shows reduced correlation with a handful of known heuristics and lower hypothesis-only performance, but that does not automatically imply better real-world validity. It would be more precise to say that the hard split appears less contaminated by several known artifacts and therefore may offer a stricter evaluation. This is still a strong result, but the current wording occasionally overreaches.

A second concern is the reliance on a small set of heuristic artifact measures. Word overlap, antonyms, negation, misspellings, and length mismatch are reasonable and interpretable, but they capture only a subset of known NLI shortcuts. The conclusion that the hard split has “few spurious correlations” should therefore be framed more narrowly as “few spurious correlations according to these measures.” There may be other lexical, syntactic, discourse, or annotation-process artifacts that remain. This does not invalidate the paper, but a more cautious interpretation would improve rigor.

The clustering design also raises some questions. The choice of exactly three clusters is inherited from the easy/ambiguous/hard framing, but it is not clear whether the data naturally support three components across all datasets. Since the authors motivate GMM partly as a way to avoid fixed thresholds, it would be useful to justify the number of clusters empirically, perhaps via BIC/AIC, stability analysis, or sensitivity experiments. Likewise, ranking clusters solely by average premise+hypothesis confidence is simple and understandable, but it may collapse richer structure in the 8-dimensional feature space. Some discussion of cluster stability across random seeds would also strengthen confidence in the method.

There are also some fairness-of-comparison issues in the training-set characterization section. For Data Maps and AUM, the paper uses percentile-based selections intended to mimic “ambiguous” subsets, but these baselines were not originally designed around the exact same subset sizes or downstream objective. The comparison is still informative, but it would be stronger if the authors matched subset budgets more systematically or reported multiple thresholds for the baselines. As written, the results are promising, though not fully definitive.

Another limitation is computational cost. The method requires training two models for each dataset split to collect dynamics, including on the test set. Although only five epochs are used for characterization, this is still nontrivial overhead compared with static heuristics. The paper would benefit from a brief discussion of runtime and whether lighter-weight approximations are possible. This matters for the claim of general applicability to other datasets.

I also think the FEVER setup could be explained more carefully. The paper notes that FEVER is repurposed as NLI and that the premise/hypothesis roles differ somewhat from standard NLI, with evidence text length affecting the length-mismatch heuristic. Since FEVER is structurally different from SNLI/MultiNLI, it would help to clarify exactly how labels and inputs are mapped, and whether the same interpretation of “difficulty” and “spurious correlation” carries over unchanged. The FEVER results are interesting, especially because the hard split seems genuinely difficult rather than artifact-heavy, but the paper could do more to unpack that distinction.

There are a few smaller presentation issues. Some claims would benefit from tighter wording and clearer separation between evidence and interpretation. The paper occasionally uses “denote spurious correlations” where “identify” or “detect” would read more naturally. There are also minor grammatical issues throughout. None of these are serious, but polishing would improve readability. In addition, the appendix examples on page 12 include question marks in the “Correct?” column for some suspicious SNLI examples; it would be helpful to explicitly state whether these are suspected annotation errors identified by the authors’ manual inspection.

Despite these concerns, I think the paper makes a meaningful contribution. The central idea is novel enough relative to prior NLI stress-test work, the methodology is coherent, and the empirical results support the main claims. I especially value the combination of training-dynamics characterization, hypothesis-only bias modeling, heuristic artifact analysis, cross-model transfer, and training-data filtering. Together these make the paper more than a simple repackaging of dataset cartography. It offers a practical framework for deriving harder and cleaner evaluation subsets from existing benchmarks, which could be useful beyond NLI.

For improvement, I would encourage the authors to add a stronger discussion of the implications of fitting on the test set for characterization, include sensitivity analyses for the number of clusters and random seeds, temper claims about “real-world” validity, and clarify the FEVER formulation. If space permits, an additional experiment comparing against a characterization derived without training directly on the test set would be especially valuable. Even absent that, the current paper is solid and thought-provoking.

In summary, this is a strong paper on benchmark diagnosis and evaluation methodology for NLI. It addresses an important problem with a principled and reasonably general approach, and it provides convincing evidence that the resulting hard subsets are both more challenging and less driven by obvious shortcuts. I would be positive on acceptance.
