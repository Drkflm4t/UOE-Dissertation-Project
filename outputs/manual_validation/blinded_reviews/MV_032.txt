Review ID: MV_032
Validation Track: RQ2
============================================================

This paper studies a timely and underexplored problem: how to provide formal privacy guarantees for inference-time activation steering when the steering demonstrations are private. The core proposal, PSA, is simple and appealing: compute per-layer steering vectors from positive/negative demonstration pairs, clip the per-example activation differences, and add Gaussian noise so that the released steering vectors satisfy differential privacy. The paper also proposes a membership inference attack tailored to activation editing, based on canary-triggered generations, and evaluates utility/privacy tradeoffs across several models and alignment benchmarks.

Overall, I find the paper interesting and potentially impactful. The problem is well motivated, the proposed mechanism is conceptually clean, and the empirical scope is fairly broad in terms of models and behaviors. The main positive is that the paper identifies a realistic privacy risk in a growing alignment paradigm and offers a lightweight DP solution that is compatible with inference-time steering. That said, I also have several concerns, some of them fairly substantial, around the precision of the DP formulation, the correctness/completeness of the privacy accounting as presented, the strength and interpretation of the empirical evaluation, and some missing baselines/ablations. My assessment is that the paper has a strong idea and promising initial evidence, but the current version would benefit from tighter theory, clearer experimental methodology, and more careful claims.

The novelty is credible. To my knowledge, there has indeed been little to no prior work on differential privacy for activation editing specifically, despite substantial work on DP training and DP in-context learning. Framing steering vectors as a privatized release that can then be reused for arbitrarily many downstream queries is a useful perspective. The proposed MIA is also a meaningful contribution, especially because it does not rely on logits or losses and is tailored to the activation-editing setting. Even if the attack is somewhat synthetic, it is a reasonable first auditing tool.

The technical core is straightforward: privatize the average of clipped activation-difference vectors. This is a natural application of the Gaussian mechanism. The main theorem-level claim is that once the private steering vectors are released, any number of subsequent generations incur no additional privacy cost by post-processing. At a high level this is correct, and it is one of the strongest aspects of the paper: unlike repeated private prompting or online mechanisms, the privacy cost is paid once offline. This is practically attractive.

However, the privacy analysis as written is not fully satisfactory. The most important issue is that the sensitivity and noise calibration are presented somewhat loosely and may be incorrect or at least under-specified in their current form. In Algorithm 1, the clipping/scaling step is written as d_bar = d / max{C, ||d||_2}. If ||d|| <= C, this yields d/C rather than d, so all vectors are scaled down by at least 1/C. This is not standard clipping to radius C; standard clipping would be d * min(1, C/||d||), or equivalently d / max(1, ||d||/C). The text says “project to an L2 ball of radius C and then scale by the same constant C,” but the formula does not match that description cleanly. As written, the output vectors appear to lie in the unit ball, which may be intentional, but then the exposition should say so explicitly and consistently. Right now it is ambiguous whether the mechanism clips to radius C and averages those vectors, or normalizes by C after clipping. This matters for both utility and sensitivity.

Relatedly, the Gaussian noise is written as N(0, sigma^2), but the output is a d-dimensional vector, so this should presumably be isotropic Gaussian noise N(0, sigma^2 I_d). More importantly, the sensitivity constant used in sigma seems to assume neighboring datasets differ by replacement of one record, giving sensitivity 2/n after normalization to the unit ball. But the paper should state the neighboring relation explicitly and consistently. Definition 1 says datasets differ in one record, but does not specify add/remove versus replace-one. Since the sensitivity differs by a factor of two depending on the convention, this should not be left implicit. If the intended neighboring relation is substitution, then the 2/n factor is plausible after unit-ball normalization; if add/remove, it should be 1/n. This is fixable, but the current presentation is too casual for a DP paper.

A second theoretical concern is composition across layers. The paper uses basic composition over the selected layers and reports total epsilon as five times per-layer epsilon when steering five layers. This is valid but conservative. The issue is not that the bound is loose, but that the mechanism may be jointly releasing multiple correlated vectors computed from the same private examples and same model activations. Basic composition still applies, but the paper should be clearer that this is a release of a vector-valued object across layers and that one could equivalently analyze the joint sensitivity of the concatenated release. In fact, a joint analysis might yield a more transparent privacy-utility tradeoff and perhaps better calibration if per-layer clipping thresholds differ. As written, the paper gives the impression that each layer is independently privatized with the same sigma, but it is not obvious whether this is optimal or even how C_l is chosen in practice.

The choice of clipping thresholds C_l is another underdeveloped point. The paper says these can be optimized or estimated using a small public dataset, but the experiments do not seem to report how this was actually done. Since clipping is central to both privacy and utility, the paper should specify whether C_l is fixed to 1, estimated from public data, tuned on validation performance, or chosen heuristically. If tuned on private data, that itself may consume privacy budget unless done carefully. This is especially important because the reported noise standard deviation is fixed at 0.02 in the experiments, while the theoretical sigma in Algorithm 1 depends on n, epsilon, and delta. The relationship between the practical noise used and the claimed epsilon values is not explained clearly enough. Did the authors choose sigma = 0.02 and then back out epsilon from the formula? If so, that should be stated explicitly. Also, if the vectors are d-dimensional, the effect of sigma = 0.02 depends strongly on the scale induced by clipping/normalization, which again makes the exact preprocessing crucial.

On the empirical side, the breadth is good: seven behaviors, multiple model families, and some scaling experiments. The headline claim that PSA preserves much of the utility of mean steering seems plausible from the figures and tables. The fact that PSA often remains above zero-shot and close to non-private mean steering is encouraging. The scaling result that larger models tolerate privatization better is also intuitively sensible and potentially useful.

Still, the empirical presentation has several weaknesses. First, many results are shown without uncertainty estimates. Given that steering performance can vary with layer choice, random seeds, generation stochasticity, and noise draws, confidence intervals or standard deviations are important. This is especially true for claims like “PSA sometimes improves on non-private steering,” which could easily be noise. The paper should report multiple runs over different Gaussian noise samples and, ideally, different demonstration subsamples. Without this, it is hard to know whether small differences are meaningful.

Second, the evaluation protocol for alignment behaviors is somewhat narrow. The multiple-choice datasets from Perez et al./Rimsky et al. are useful, but they are also highly stylized and may overstate steering effectiveness. Since the paper’s motivation includes realistic private domains like healthcare and finance, it would strengthen the work to include at least one more naturalistic setting where demonstrations are not just paired A/B completions but actual domain-specific examples. Even a smaller-scale experiment would help establish external validity.

Third, the GPT-4-based qualitative evaluation is weakly informative in its current form. Table 3 reports scores that are often extremely close across methods and in some cases close to zero or baseline. It is not entirely clear what these scores mean, how reliable they are, or whether they capture the intended behavior versus general fluency. The paper says prompts are in the appendix, but the main text should summarize the rubric more concretely. Also, using only GPT-4 as judge without human validation is acceptable for a preliminary study, but then the claims should be modest.

Fourth, the MMLU evaluation table appears incomplete or malformed. In Table 5, the zero-shot row seems truncated, with only one value shown. This makes it difficult to verify the claim that zero-shot remains the same in all settings. More broadly, evaluating “average probability of selecting the correct answer after reformatting into A/B format” is unusual relative to standard MMLU accuracy, and the paper should justify this choice more carefully. Since steering is applied at inference time, it could affect calibration differently from accuracy.

The membership inference attack section is interesting but also raises interpretational questions. The canary-based attack is tailored and demonstrates that non-private steering can encode membership information into generations. That is a useful proof of concept. However, the attack is highly synthetic: the canaries are inserted into demonstrations in a way designed to create a detectable association, and the attack threshold is chosen per model. This is acceptable as an auditing stress test, but the paper should be more careful not to overgeneralize from it to real-world privacy leakage. In particular, the attack seems closer to a targeted extraction or trigger-based leakage test than a generic membership inference attack on naturally occurring records. It would help to include additional attacks or at least discuss limitations more explicitly.

I also found the empirical epsilon calculation somewhat under-motivated. The formula based on FPR/FNR follows prior auditing work, but empirical epsilon estimates from a single attack are only lower bounds on privacy leakage under that attack class, not a comprehensive privacy characterization. The paper mostly phrases this responsibly, but some wording suggests stronger conclusions than warranted. For example, saying that empirical epsilon being smaller than theoretical epsilon “suggests stronger protection in real-world settings” is too broad. It only suggests that this particular attack did not saturate the theoretical bound.

Another concern is the baseline set. The paper compares PSA mainly to mean steering and PCA steering, plus zero-shot. That is reasonable for activation editing, but if the broader claim is about private alignment with lightweight inference-time methods, it would be useful to compare against a DP in-context learning baseline or a simple retrieval-plus-DP mechanism, at least conceptually or on a subset. Even if the methods are not directly equivalent, such a comparison would help contextualize where PSA sits in the privacy/utility/latency design space.

There are also several presentation issues. The manuscript contains many typos and grammatical errors, some duplicated figure/algorithm markup, and a few malformed tables. These do not invalidate the work, but they do reduce confidence in the care taken with the technical details. For a paper making formal privacy claims, precision in notation and exposition is especially important. Examples include inconsistent indexing of the dataset, notation glitches in Algorithm 1, and the incomplete MMLU table. These should be cleaned up thoroughly.

Despite these criticisms, I think the paper has real strengths. The problem is important and timely. The proposed solution is simple enough to be adopted in practice. The offline-DP-release framing is elegant and practically meaningful. The empirical results, while not yet fully convincing in all respects, are promising and suggest that privacy-preserving steering may indeed be feasible at modest utility cost, especially for larger models. The paper also opens a useful new direction at the intersection of alignment, interpretability, and privacy.

To strengthen the paper, I would recommend the following concrete revisions. First, fix and clarify the clipping/noise mechanism, including exact neighboring relation, vector-valued Gaussian notation, and a formal sensitivity proof. Second, explain precisely how C_l and sigma are chosen in experiments, and ensure the reported epsilon values correspond exactly to the implemented mechanism. Third, add variance estimates over multiple noise draws and seeds. Fourth, improve the empirical section with at least one more realistic dataset or task, or at minimum a stronger discussion of the limitations of the current A/B benchmarks. Fifth, expand the privacy auditing section with either additional attack variants or a clearer framing of what the canary attack does and does not show. Sixth, clean up the tables, notation, and writing substantially.

My overall recommendation is cautiously positive on the idea, but mixed on the current execution. If I were reviewing for a top-tier venue, I would place this around the borderline area: the contribution is novel and potentially impactful, but the paper needs tighter technical rigor and stronger empirical methodology to fully justify its claims. If the authors can address the DP specification issues and shore up the experimental evidence, this could become a strong paper.
