Review ID: MV_036
Validation Track: RQ1
============================================================

Summary: The paper proposes SyncLLM, a post-training framework that converts a pretrained autoregressive LLM (Llama3-8B) into a full-duplex spoken dialogue agent capable of synchronous interaction with real-world time. The core idea is to inject periodic synchronization tokens into interleaved dual-speaker HuBERT token streams, train on deduplicated speech-unit sequences, and predict short future chunks for both speakers to tolerate latency. The training recipe uses three stages: large-scale synthetic spoken dialogue from text data, synthetic full-duplex dialogue without overlaps, and final finetuning on about 1.9k hours of real dual-channel spoken dialogue. Empirically, the paper reports substantially better semantic quality than dGSLM via ASR-transcribed perplexity and human Meaningfulness MOS, while maintaining comparable Naturalness MOS and improving turn-taking statistics correlation. It also demonstrates simulated interaction between two SyncLLM agents under 160–240 ms latency and across different training domains.

Strengths: Addresses an important and underexplored problem: moving beyond turn-based spoken interfaces toward genuinely full-duplex dialogue with overlaps, backchannels, and latency-aware turn-taking.; Technically novel formulation of synchrony for LLMs via periodic synchronization tokens and chunked interleaved dual-speaker modeling, which is a clear and intuitive design.; Strong systems insight in explicitly modeling one-chunk-ahead user estimation to handle realistic network latency, rather than assuming immediate access to the interlocutor stream.; Practical and compelling three-stage training recipe that leverages 212k hours of synthetic spoken dialogue plus only ~2k hours of real spoken dialogue, making the approach scalable despite limited dual-channel speech data.; Good use of pretrained text LLM knowledge: the method preserves the standard autoregressive architecture instead of introducing a specialized speech-only model, which plausibly explains the strong semantic gains.; Empirical results are consistently strong across multiple axes: lower ASR-based perplexity than dGSLM, much higher human-rated Meaningfulness MOS, competitive Naturalness MOS, and improved turn-taking event correlations.; Includes both in-distribution and out-of-distribution evaluation (Fisher and CANDOR), with evidence of better OOD robustness than the baseline.; The interaction-mode experiments are valuable: they move beyond offline continuation and test simulated two-agent full-duplex interaction under realistic latency assumptions.; The paper is generally well motivated, and figures/tables clearly communicate the tokenization scheme, synchronization mechanism, and evaluation outcomes.

Weaknesses: The main baseline comparison is largely centered on dGSLM; the paper would be stronger with broader comparisons to more recent speech-text or spoken dialogue systems, even if they are turn-based, to better contextualize gains.; The semantic evaluation relies on ASR transcription followed by text perplexity, which may conflate dialogue quality with ASR errors and may under-represent overlapping speech phenomena.; Human evaluation uses 32 annotators and 180 items, which is reasonable but still modest for a claim about broad conversational naturalness; more detail on inter-rater agreement would help.; The simulated interaction setting uses LLM-LLM interaction as a proxy for LLM-human interaction, which is informative but not a full substitute for live user studies.; Speech quality itself is not a central evaluation target, and the authors acknowledge reliance on a relatively simple HiFi-GAN vocoder; this leaves open how much perceived quality is bottlenecked by synthesis rather than dialogue modeling.; Some implementation choices, such as interpolation of deduplicated tokens back to fixed-rate sequences, are heuristic and not deeply analyzed for failure cases.

Methodological Flaws: The paper contains a likely scientific/logical inconsistency in the interpretation of perplexity results. On page 6, it states that 'dGSLM has a perplexity drop of ~70 relative to the ground-truth, while SyncLLM only has a drop of ~15.' Since lower perplexity is better, the reported numbers in Figure 5 appear to indicate that dGSLM has substantially higher perplexity than ground truth, i.e., a degradation/increase rather than a 'drop.' This is a wording/interpretation error that should be corrected.; The interpolation procedure used to reconstruct fixed-rate HuBERT sequences from deduplicated chunks is heuristic (equal repetition within a chunk) and is not validated quantitatively against original token durations. This weakens the claim that timing is faithfully preserved, especially for fine-grained prosody or within-chunk duration patterns.; The semantic evaluation pipeline discards overlapping speech when converting generated spoken dialogues into turn-based text for ASR/perplexity analysis. Because overlap handling is a central claimed advantage of full-duplex dialogue, this evaluation may systematically omit part of the phenomenon the paper aims to model.; Turn-taking naturalness is evaluated via Pearson correlation of average event durations against ground-truth continuations given the same prompt. This metric captures coarse statistical similarity but does not fully validate whether generated turn-taking is conversationally appropriate at the local event level; thus the empirical validity of strong naturalness claims is somewhat limited.; The paper does not report statistical significance testing for many automatic comparisons (e.g., perplexity curves, turn-taking correlations across chunk sizes/baselines), making it harder to assess robustness of some reported gains.; The interaction experiments are based on simulated two-model conversations with one-chunk latency rather than real deployment measurements. Therefore, claims about robustness to Internet-scale latency are promising but only partially validated empirically.
