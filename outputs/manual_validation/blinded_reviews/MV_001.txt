Review ID: MV_001
Validation Track: RQ2
============================================================

This paper studies an important and, to my knowledge, genuinely underexplored problem: how to provide formal privacy guarantees for inference-time activation steering when the steering demonstrations are private. The core proposal, PSA, is simple and appealing: compute per-layer steering vectors from positive/negative demonstration pairs, clip the per-example activation differences, add Gaussian noise, and then use the privatized vectors for arbitrary downstream generations. The paper also introduces a membership inference attack tailored to activation editing, based on canary-triggered generations rather than access to logits. Empirically, the authors evaluate across several model families and alignment-style benchmarks, and argue that privacy can be obtained with modest utility loss.

Overall, I find the paper timely and potentially impactful. The problem formulation is well motivated, the method is conceptually clean, and the “offline privatize once, answer infinitely many queries by post-processing” framing is a strong practical advantage over many DP-at-inference approaches. The paper also does a good job connecting activation steering, mechanistic interpretability, and differential privacy. That said, I have several concerns about technical correctness, evaluation methodology, and clarity that currently prevent me from viewing the paper as fully convincing in its present form.

The main strength of the work is the problem itself. Activation steering is increasingly used as a lightweight alternative to fine-tuning, and privacy questions here are indeed under-addressed. The observation that steering vectors derived from private demonstrations may leak membership or content is plausible and worth formalizing. The proposed solution is also refreshingly straightforward. In contrast to many privacy papers that require substantial algorithmic machinery, PSA is easy to understand and implement. If the privacy accounting is correct, the post-processing argument that one can serve unlimited queries after privately releasing the steering vectors is compelling. The empirical scope is also reasonably broad in terms of model families and behaviors.

However, my biggest concern is that the DP mechanism and its presentation are not yet sufficiently rigorous. In Algorithm 1, the clipping/scaling formula appears incorrect or at least inconsistently described. The paper says vectors are projected to an L2 ball of radius Cl and then scaled by the same constant Cl, but the algorithm defines bar(d)_i^l = d_i^l / max{Cl, ||d_i^l||_2}. This indeed ensures norm at most 1, but it is not the standard “clip to radius Cl, then divide by Cl” presentation unless one rewrites it carefully. More importantly, the sensitivity argument is only implicit. Since each normalized vector has norm at most 1, replacing one record changes the average by at most 2/n in L2 norm, which explains the noise scale. But the paper should state this formally and prove the theorem cleanly. Right now, the privacy guarantee is asserted rather than derived. For a paper centered on DP, this is too informal.

Relatedly, the Gaussian mechanism statement is dimensionally sloppy. The notation N(0, sigma^2) for a vector-valued release is ambiguous; it should be isotropic Gaussian noise in R^d, e.g. N(0, sigma^2 I_d). This matters because the steering vectors are high-dimensional. The paper should also be explicit about whether independent noise is added per layer and whether privacy composition is only across selected layers. As written, the privacy theorem is plausible, but the exposition is not at the standard expected for a top ML venue.

A second technical concern is the treatment of hyperparameter selection. The clipping thresholds Cl, selected layers, and steering strength lambda all materially affect utility and possibly the practical privacy-utility tradeoff. The paper says Cl can be optimized using a small public dataset, but it is not clear whether this was actually done, how the public data was chosen, or whether any private validation leakage occurred. Likewise, the choice of five middle layers seems inherited from prior work, but if layer selection was tuned per dataset/model using private data, that would need to be accounted for. Since the paper’s central claim is formal privacy, all data-dependent tuning procedures need to be carefully separated into public-only or privacy-accounted steps.

The empirical evaluation is promising but has several weaknesses. First, the benchmarks are all binary multiple-choice behavioral datasets derived from prior steering work. These are useful for controlled comparisons, but they are narrow proxies for “alignment.” In particular, some of the behaviors, such as survival instinct or AI coordination, are idiosyncratic and not obviously representative of realistic enterprise alignment tasks involving private data. The paper’s motivating examples are healthcare and finance, but the experiments do not include domain-specific private datasets or realistic sensitive attributes. This weakens the practical relevance of the empirical story.

Second, the open-ended generation evaluation relies heavily on GPT-4-as-judge, and the reported scores are difficult to interpret. For example, in Table 3, some scores are extremely low across all methods, suggesting either the prompts or the rubric may not be well calibrated. It is also unclear whether higher is always better across all behaviors; for hallucination and sycophancy, lower scores seem preferable under the rubric, whereas the surrounding text sometimes discusses “quality” more generally. The paper should disentangle behavioral directionality from generation quality. A separate fluency/helpfulness/consistency evaluation would help. As it stands, the GPT-4 evaluation mixes behavior adherence with quality in a somewhat confusing way.

Third, the MMLU evaluation is underdeveloped and partially malformed in the provided table. Table 5 appears incomplete, with missing entries in the zero-shot row. More substantively, converting MMLU to A/B format and reporting “average probability of selecting the correct answer” is unusual and not directly comparable to standard MMLU accuracy. Since steering is applied globally at inference time, it would be useful to report standard downstream metrics on a broader suite, or at least clarify exactly how the reformulation was done and whether the results are stable across prompts and seeds.

The privacy attack section is interesting and arguably one of the paper’s most novel empirical contributions, but it also raises questions. The canary-based MIA is really closer to a targeted extraction or influence test than a generic membership inference attack. The attack inserts synthetic anchor-target associations into one demonstration and then checks whether the steered model preferentially emits the target given the anchor. This is a reasonable auditing tool, but the paper should be more precise about what it measures. It does not show that arbitrary natural private records are inferable; rather, it shows that specially planted rare associations can influence generations enough to be detected. That is still valuable, but the framing should be narrower.

I also worry that the attack thresholding procedure may be overfit to the models under study. The thresholds tau are chosen differently for Llama and Qwen, but the selection procedure is not well justified. Were these thresholds tuned on held-out attack trials? If so, how sensitive are the reported FPR/FNR and empirical epsilon values to this choice? Since the empirical epsilon estimates depend directly on FPR/FNR, confidence intervals are essential. Reporting only point estimates over 1000 trials is not enough, especially when some rates are very small. A bootstrap interval or exact binomial interval would make the privacy auditing claims much more credible.

The interpretation of empirical epsilon also needs more care. The paper notes that empirical epsilon is smaller than theoretical epsilon and concludes that the theoretical bounds are conservative. While this is often true, empirical epsilon from one specific attack is only a lower bound on worst-case privacy leakage under that attack family, not a direct estimate of the true DP parameter of the mechanism. The wording should avoid implying that the mechanism “has” empirical epsilon 0.6 in any formal sense. At best, the attack fails to witness a larger violation. This distinction is important.

There are also several inconsistencies and presentation issues that hurt confidence. The abstract says experiments span models from 0.5B to 7B and families including Llama, Qwen, Mistral, and Gemma, but the main quantitative tables shown in the text are often only for Llama or selected models. The paper mentions “three state-of-the-art LLMs” in one place and “four open-source LLMs” elsewhere. There are visible formatting artifacts and footnote numbering issues. Some qualitative examples appear contradictory: for hallucination steering, the example outputs in the appendix seem to reinforce false premises rather than reduce hallucination, which may reflect confusion about whether positive steering corresponds to truthful or hallucinatory behavior in that dataset. This is especially concerning because the paper’s central semantics depend on the designation of c+ and c-. The authors need to make the direction of each steering task absolutely explicit.

A deeper conceptual issue is whether the privacy guarantee meaningfully protects against all relevant leakage channels in deployment. The DP guarantee applies to the released steering vectors with respect to the demonstration dataset, assuming the base model is fixed and public. That is fine. But if the demonstrations are themselves generated or selected using private data-dependent preprocessing, or if multiple steering vectors are released over time for related tasks, composition across releases becomes important. The paper briefly mentions composition across layers but not across repeated deployments, tasks, or hyperparameter searches. Since practical alignment systems may maintain many steering vectors, this omission should at least be discussed.

I would also like to see stronger baselines. The paper compares PSA mainly to non-private mean steering and PCA steering. But a natural baseline is clipping-only without noise, or reduced-data non-private steering matched for utility, or even DP release of a lower-dimensional subspace rather than full vectors. Since the method is essentially “DP mean estimation of activation differences,” it would be useful to compare against standard private mean estimation variants, perhaps with better accounting or shrinkage. The current comparisons establish that PSA is close to mean steering, but not that it is the best private design.

Despite these concerns, I think the paper has real merit. The central idea is sound, the problem is important, and the empirical results suggest that privacy-preserving steering may indeed be practical. I especially appreciate the offline DP release perspective and the attempt to build an attack tailored to activation editing rather than reusing loss-based MIAs that do not fit the setting.

To strengthen the paper, I would strongly recommend the following revisions. First, add a formal theorem and proof for the privacy guarantee, including exact sensitivity derivation, vector-valued Gaussian notation, and explicit assumptions about neighboring datasets. Second, clearly document all hyperparameter selection procedures and ensure they use only public data or are privacy-accounted. Third, improve the evaluation methodology: add confidence intervals, clarify metric directionality, fix incomplete tables, and provide more standard downstream evaluations. Fourth, reframe the canary attack more carefully as a targeted privacy audit, and report sensitivity analyses over threshold tau, temperature, and number of generations. Fifth, clarify the semantics of positive versus negative demonstrations for each dataset and ensure examples are consistent with the claimed behavior. Finally, discuss broader deployment composition issues and limitations more explicitly.

In summary, I view this as a promising paper with a strong idea and potentially meaningful contribution, but one that currently falls short on rigor and experimental clarity in ways that matter for a privacy-focused ML paper. If the technical presentation and evaluation were tightened, I could see this becoming a solid contribution. At present, I am positive on the novelty and relevance, but only moderately convinced by the evidence as presented.
