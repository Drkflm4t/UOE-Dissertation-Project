# Review ID: MV_020

**Validation Track:** `RQ2`

---

This paper tackles an important and underexplored problem at the intersection of NLP, privacy, and HCI: helping users identify and mitigate privacy risks in voluntary online self-disclosures. The paper makes three main contributions: a span-level taxonomy and annotated corpus of self-disclosures across 19 categories, a token-tagging model for identifying disclosure spans, and a new generation task of “self-disclosure abstraction” aimed at rewriting disclosures to be less specific while preserving utility. The work is timely, practically motivated, and strengthened by a user study with Reddit users. Overall, I found the paper interesting and potentially impactful, especially because it moves beyond standard PII detection toward a broader notion of privacy-sensitive self-disclosure and because it attempts to close the loop from detection to actionable assistance.

The strongest aspect of the paper is the problem framing. The distinction between classic PII redaction and self-disclosure management is well articulated and genuinely useful. Many privacy harms online do not arise from explicit identifiers alone, but from combinations of demographic attributes, experiences, and contextual details that users voluntarily reveal. The paper correctly identifies that a practical assistant should operate at span level, not merely at sentence or post level, and should ideally support user agency rather than simply censor content. This framing is compelling and, to my knowledge, relatively novel in the NLP literature.

The dataset contribution is also meaningful. A corpus of 2.4K Reddit posts with 4.8K annotated spans across 19 categories is a useful resource, and the taxonomy is broader than prior work focused only on narrow domains such as health disclosures or author profiling. The examples in Table 1 make clear that the categories cover a mix of demographic attributes and lived experiences that are relevant to privacy risk in practice. The annotation setup appears thoughtful in some respects: in-house annotators, training, iterative monitoring, and adjudicated recent batches for testing are all positives. The chronological split is also a sensible design choice if the authors want to approximate deployment on future data.

The user study is another notable strength. It is refreshing to see benchmark-style modeling complemented by formative interviews with actual Reddit users. The study surfaces an important tension between annotator-defined “potentially risky” disclosures and user-perceived risk, as well as the importance of subreddit norms, personalization, and explainability. These findings are valuable in their own right and help motivate the abstraction task. In particular, the observation that users want rewriting assistance rather than only highlighting is plausible and practically important.

That said, while the paper is promising, I have several concerns about methodological rigor, evaluation validity, and the strength of some claims.

My biggest concern is that the abstraction task and its evaluation are not yet sufficiently grounded to support strong conclusions. The training and evaluation data for abstraction are generated by ChatGPT/GPT-4 rather than by humans. This creates a substantial risk that the task becomes “imitate another LLM’s rewriting style” rather than “produce abstractions that humans actually prefer or that truly reduce privacy risk.” The paper does include human evaluation, which helps, but the overall setup still depends heavily on synthetic references. Moreover, the train/eval/test split for abstraction is very small: 159 posts for training, 25 for validation, and 50 for testing. It is difficult to know whether the reported differences across prompting/fine-tuning strategies are robust or mostly noise. The paper would be much stronger if it included either human-written abstraction references for at least a subset, or a more direct task-based evaluation of whether the rewritten text actually reduces inferable personal specificity.

Relatedly, the notion of “privacy reduction” in the abstraction section remains underspecified and somewhat subjective. The paper reports a Likert score of around 3.2/5 for privacy reduction, but this is not tied to an operational privacy threat model. Does abstraction reduce identifiability, attribute inference, linkability across posts, or merely perceived specificity? These are not equivalent. In some cases, replacing “I am 32” with “I’m in my early 30s” may reduce specificity slightly, but in others, preserving the semantic essence may still leave the user highly identifiable when combined with context. Since the paper’s central claim is privacy risk reduction, it would benefit from a more formal or at least more explicit threat model and evaluation criterion. For example, one could test whether an attribute inference model or a retrieval/linkage model performs worse on abstracted text than on original text, while preserving downstream utility judged by humans.

The identification results are decent but should be interpreted more cautiously than the paper currently does. The headline “over 75% token F1” sounds strong, but token F1 is the most forgiving metric here and may overstate practical usefulness for span highlighting. Exact span F1 is much lower, around 49 for multi-class and 54 for binary in the best setting. Since the intended application is highlighting spans for users, exact or near-exact span localization matters a lot. The per-class breakdown also reveals substantial weakness on some sensitive categories, especially sexual orientation and relationship status. This does not invalidate the contribution, but the paper should be more balanced in presenting the system as an early-stage assistant rather than a broadly reliable detector.

I also found the modeling choices for identification somewhat uneven. Most categories are handled with a RoBERTa tagger, but names and contact information are delegated to external tools plus a sentence-level self-disclosure classifier. This hybrid approach is reasonable from an engineering perspective, but it complicates the scientific story. It is not entirely clear how these components are integrated in evaluation, whether the reported F1 includes these categories, and whether the comparison across categories is fair given that some are learned end-to-end and others are handled by external systems. Since name and contact are among the most severe categories, the paper should be much clearer about how often they occur in test data, how they are evaluated, and whether the final system’s reported performance includes them.

The annotation methodology also raises some questions. The paper says that only posts that both initial annotators agreed contained personal information disclosure were selected for subsequent span annotation. This likely improves annotation efficiency, but it may bias the corpus toward clearer, more salient disclosures and exclude borderline or ambiguous cases that are important in deployment. It would be useful to know how many potentially positive posts were discarded due to disagreement, and whether this filtering inflates downstream performance. More generally, the paper does not report inter-annotator agreement statistics for either the initial filtering stage or the span/category annotation stage. Given that the user study later reveals disagreement between annotators and users about what counts as risky, agreement statistics are especially important here.

The taxonomy is useful, but some category design choices deserve more discussion. There appears to be overlap or inconsistency between categories such as relationship status, husband/BF, wife/GF, and family. The distinction between “relationship status” and partner-specific mentions may be intuitive for annotation, but it is not obvious that it is optimal for modeling or user-facing explanations. Similarly, “health” and “mental health” are separated, but many disclosures may straddle both. The paper would benefit from a clearer account of category boundaries, confusion patterns, and whether a hierarchical or multi-label span scheme might better reflect the phenomenon.

The user study is valuable, but its evidentiary strength is limited. The sample size is small, which is acceptable for a formative HCI study, but the paper sometimes presents percentages such as “82% positive outlook” in a way that can sound stronger than warranted. More importantly, the study appears to rely on manually annotated images of model outputs shown to participants. I was not fully clear on whether participants saw raw model predictions, post-processed outputs, or researcher-curated visualizations. Since the study is used to support claims of real-world applicability, the exact protocol matters. Also, because participants supplied posts they already considered privacy-sensitive, the study may overrepresent users predisposed to value such a tool. None of this invalidates the study, but the claims should remain modest and explicitly formative.

There are also concerns around evaluation procedure in the abstraction section. Human evaluation is conducted by “one of the authors” on 60 sampled instances for the Rank & Rate stage, which is problematic. Single-rater author evaluation is vulnerable to bias and does not provide inter-rater reliability. The later Likert evaluation of the top two models is better in spirit, but the paper does not clearly state how many raters were used, whether they were blind to condition, or what agreement was achieved. For a generation task centered on nuanced tradeoffs among privacy, utility, and diversity, stronger human evaluation methodology is needed.

Another issue is reproducibility. The paper says the dataset and models will not be publicly released, only shared with researchers who adhere to ethical guidelines. I understand the privacy rationale, but this substantially limits reproducibility and downstream impact. Since the paper’s contributions are heavily empirical, the authors should consider whether a sanitized benchmark subset, annotation guidelines, model cards, or at least detailed statistics and scripts can be released. Otherwise, it will be difficult for the community to validate or build on the work.

The ethics discussion is well intentioned but somewhat too optimistic. The claim that model failures do not pose additional risks because they merely lead to “overprotection” is not fully convincing. False negatives could fail to warn users about risky disclosures, and false positives or poor abstractions could distort meaning, suppress identity expression, or disproportionately burden marginalized users whose self-disclosures are central to seeking support. The paper itself notes that marginalized populations face acute privacy risks, which makes it especially important to analyze disparate performance across categories and communities. The statement that no disproportionate harms were identified seems too strong without subgroup analysis.

I also think the paper could do more to connect the identification and abstraction components into a coherent end-to-end system evaluation. Right now, identification is evaluated separately, abstraction is evaluated assuming gold disclosure spans, and the user study uses manually prepared outputs. But in practice, users would interact with a pipeline: detect spans, choose which ones matter, and receive rewrites. Errors will compound. An end-to-end evaluation, even on a small subset, would significantly strengthen the practical claims.

Despite these concerns, I think the paper has clear merit. The problem is important, the framing is novel, the dataset is potentially valuable, and the user-centered perspective is a real asset. My overall assessment is positive but not unreserved: this feels like an ambitious and promising first step rather than a fully mature solution. The paper would be substantially stronger with tighter evaluation and more careful calibration of claims.

For improvement, I would most strongly recommend the following. First, add annotation agreement statistics and more detail on category definitions and confusion. Second, clarify exactly how the hybrid identification system is evaluated, especially for rare but severe categories like name and contact. Third, strengthen the abstraction evaluation with multiple independent human raters and, if possible, a more operational privacy metric tied to a threat model. Fourth, include at least a small end-to-end evaluation from detection through abstraction. Fifth, moderate claims about privacy protection and real-world applicability, emphasizing that the system supports reflection and rewriting but does not guarantee privacy safety.

In summary, this is a thoughtful and potentially impactful paper on an important problem that has received too little attention in NLP. Its main strengths are the problem formulation, the span-level dataset, and the integration of user feedback into task design. Its main weaknesses are the limited rigor of the abstraction evaluation, the lack of a clear privacy threat model, incomplete annotation/evaluation details, and somewhat overstated conclusions relative to the evidence. I would be inclined to support acceptance if the venue values novel, interdisciplinary, user-centered problem formulations and is comfortable with some methodological incompleteness. If the bar is primarily on empirical rigor and reproducibility, I would be more hesitant.
