# Review ID: MV_036

**Validation Track:** `RQ1`

---

This paper tackles an important and underexplored problem: helping users reduce privacy risks from online self-disclosure. The paper proposes a pipeline with three components: fine-grained span-level self-disclosure detection, span-level abstraction of disclosures into less specific alternatives, and importance rating to help decide which disclosures should be abstracted. The work combines dataset creation, model development, and an HCI-style user study. Overall, I found the paper timely, ambitious, and genuinely useful. The strongest aspects are the problem formulation, the breadth of the disclosure taxonomy, the effort to ground the work in user needs, and the introduction of span-level abstraction as a practical alternative to blunt anonymization. At the same time, there are several methodological and evaluation issues that limit how strongly I can trust the empirical claims, especially around annotation reliability, evaluation design, and the realism of the abstraction setting.

The paper’s central motivation is compelling. Existing privacy and PII tools often focus on canonical identifiers or sentence/document-level classification, whereas many real privacy risks in social media arise from softer, contextual self-disclosures. The examples in Figure 1 on page 1 and the taxonomy in Table 1 on page 2 make this concrete: disclosures such as age/gender, family role, occupation, mental health, or location are often not “PII” in the narrow sense, yet can still contribute to doxxing, regret, or unwanted profiling. Framing the task at the span level is a meaningful contribution because it aligns better with how users might actually edit text before posting. The paper also does a good job distinguishing span abstraction from sentence paraphrasing and sentence-level abstraction; Table 3 on page 5 and Table 5 on page 6 clearly illustrate why preserving surrounding text matters.

The dataset contribution is substantial. The authors curate 2.4K Reddit posts with 4.8K annotated disclosure spans across 19 categories, which is broader than most prior work. The taxonomy itself is useful and likely to be reused. Table 1 on page 2 is informative and shows both the breadth and the long-tail nature of the categories. I also appreciated the attempt to include both demographic attributes and personal experiences, since privacy risk often emerges from combinations of these. The annotation guidelines in Appendix G are reasonably detailed, and the authors appear to have taken privacy precautions in data handling.

That said, the annotation quality raises concerns. The overall Krippendorff’s alpha of 0.54 for span annotation, reported on page 3 and broken down in Table 6 on page 14, is only moderate, and several important categories are much lower: mental health 0.45, pet 0.48, relationship status 0.48, sexual orientation 0.58, health 0.53. For importance rating, alpha drops to 0.29, and for abstraction evaluation some dimensions also have fairly weak agreement, especially utility preservation at 0.28. The paper acknowledges subjectivity, which is fair, but these numbers materially affect confidence in both the training labels and the human evaluation conclusions. In particular, when the paper later claims that the best abstraction model “moderately reduces privacy while preserving high utility,” that claim rests on human judgments with limited reliability. I do not think this invalidates the work, but it does mean the conclusions should be stated more cautiously.

The detection task is well motivated and the modeling choices are sensible. Fine-tuning RoBERTa-large and DeBERTaV3-large for sequence tagging is a reasonable baseline, and the comparison to prompted GPT-4 is useful. The best partial span F1 of 65.71 for DeBERTa, shown in Table 2 on page 3 and Table 8 on page 16, is respectable given the difficulty of the task. I also found the sentence-level segmentation result in Table 7 on page 15 interesting; it suggests that local context is often sufficient and perhaps preferable for this task. The ShareGPT transfer experiment on page 4 and Table 9 on page 16 is a nice addition, since it broadens the relevance beyond Reddit.

However, the detection evaluation also has limitations. First, the paper emphasizes partial span F1, which is understandable, but full span F1 is much lower (around 48.88 average for DeBERTa in Table 8), indicating that exact localization remains difficult. Since the intended use case is highlighting specific spans for users, exact boundaries matter. The paper should foreground this gap more clearly. Second, the test set appears relatively small at the per-class level; several categories have very few spans, making class-wise conclusions unstable. Third, the handling of rare classes such as name and contact is somewhat ad hoc: a separate sentence classifier plus external NER/regex is practical, but it means the system is not really a unified 19-class detector. That is acceptable engineering, but the contribution should be framed accordingly. Fourth, the comparison to GPT-4 is not entirely apples-to-apples, since the prompted model is used zero/few-shot while the discriminative models are trained in-domain; the result is still informative, but not a strong statement about model capability in general.

The user study is one of the paper’s most distinctive strengths. Too many NLP privacy papers stop at benchmark metrics, whereas this paper actually asks whether users find the outputs useful. The qualitative findings on pages 4–5 are valuable: users appreciated word-level highlighting, wanted personalization, and in some cases found the model over-sensitive. The observation that users want help rewriting disclosures directly is especially important, and it motivates the abstraction task in a convincing way. The study also surfaces a key conceptual issue: “self-disclosure” is not the same as “privacy risk,” and users may disagree with annotators about what is risky. This is a strong insight.

Still, the user study has some weaknesses that should temper the claims. The sample size is small at 21 participants, interviews are long and intensive, and the participant pool is limited to U.S.-based Reddit users recruited via Prolific. That is acceptable for formative HCI work, but not enough to support broad claims of real-world applicability. Also, the paper reports percentages such as 82% positive outlook and 62% desire to use the tool, but the coding methodology for these qualitative outcomes is not described in enough detail in the main paper. Were these categories pre-defined? How were disagreements resolved? Without more transparency, these percentages read as stronger evidence than they may be. I would encourage the authors to present the study more explicitly as exploratory and formative.

The abstraction task is novel and probably the most exciting part of the paper. The formulation is practical: edit only the disclosure span, preserve the rest of the sentence, and generate multiple alternatives so users can choose. This is a meaningful departure from standard anonymization or full-sentence rewriting. The examples in Figure 1 on page 1 and Table 11 on page 17 are intuitive and show the promise of the approach. I also appreciated the explicit comparison against sentence paraphrasing and sentence abstraction in Table 5 on page 6, where span abstraction performs competitively while preserving surrounding text.

My main concern is that the abstraction models are trained on synthetic labels generated by GPT-3.5/GPT-4 rather than human-written abstractions. This is understandable from a cost perspective, but it creates a circularity problem: the model is distilled from another model’s outputs, and then evaluated partly against references generated by LLMs and partly by human judgments that have modest agreement. The paper does include human evaluation, which helps, but the core training signal still reflects the prompting choices and biases of GPT-3.5. This matters because “privacy-preserving abstraction” is not a purely lexical transformation; it involves nuanced judgments about what details matter, what can be generalized, and what social meaning is preserved. A distilled model may simply learn the style of GPT-generated generalizations rather than a robust notion of privacy-aware rewriting.

Relatedly, the automatic metrics for abstraction are not very convincing. BLEU and ROUGE matching metrics, even with Hungarian matching for multiple outputs, are weak proxies for privacy increase or utility preservation. The paper seems aware of this and relies more on human evaluation, which is appropriate. But then the human evaluation itself is based on only 60 sampled instances for the top-level comparison in Table 4 and Figure 2 on page 7. Given the subtlety of the task and the low-to-moderate agreement, I would be cautious about overinterpreting small differences between methods, such as end-to-end instruction tuning versus iterative generation with thought. The reported differences are interesting but not decisive.

The importance rating task is a sensible response to the user-study finding that the detector can be over-sensitive. Conceptually, it is useful to distinguish disclosures that can be removed, abstracted, or kept. However, this part currently feels less mature than the other two tasks. The annotation agreement is low, the label space is subjective, and the evaluation metric is unusually permissive: a prediction is counted as correct if it matches any one of the three annotators. That substantially inflates accuracy and makes the reported 80.37% on page 8 and Figure 3 on page 7 hard to interpret. Since only 24% of instances had full consensus and 11% had complete disagreement, “matching any annotator” may be closer to measuring plausibility than correctness. I think this task is promising, but the current empirical evidence is not strong enough to claim parity with GPT-3.5 in a meaningful sense.

I also have some concerns about the privacy framing. The paper repeatedly speaks of “reducing privacy risks,” but the abstraction evaluation measures perceived privacy increase on a Likert scale rather than any operational privacy metric. There is no adversarial or re-identification evaluation showing that the abstractions actually make users harder to identify or profile. For example, replacing “I live in New Mexico” with “I live in the Southwest of the United States” intuitively helps, but how much? Similarly, some abstractions may preserve enough correlated information to remain identifying. Without a threat model or downstream privacy attack evaluation, the paper demonstrates perceived privacy improvement rather than measured privacy protection. That is still valuable, but the claims should be narrowed accordingly.

Another subtle issue is that abstraction may sometimes distort meaning or social positioning in ways not captured by utility ratings. For instance, replacing “I am gay” with “I belong to the LGBTQ+ community” changes specificity and potentially identity salience; replacing “PTSD from the military” with “previous trauma” removes a causal and institutional context that may matter. Some examples in Table 11 on page 17 are good, but others arguably alter important semantics. This is exactly why user control is essential, and the paper does emphasize offering multiple alternatives rather than automatic replacement. Still, the evaluation should more directly examine semantic faithfulness and potential harm from over-generalization.

On the ethics side, the paper is thoughtful and more careful than average. The authors discuss IRB approval, in-house annotation, PII replacement with synthetic data, and restricted release. Given the sensitivity of the domain, these are appropriate choices. I do wonder, however, whether the claim in the ethics statement that model failures “do not pose additional risks but rather tend towards overprotection” is too strong. False negatives clearly pose risk by failing to flag sensitive disclosures, and misleading abstractions could create a false sense of safety. Overprotection is one failure mode, but not the only one.

In terms of presentation, the paper is generally clear and well organized. The figures and tables are helpful, especially Figure 1 on page 1, Table 1 on page 2, Table 3 on page 5, Table 4 on page 6, and Figure 2 on page 7. The appendices are extensive and useful. I did notice some minor issues: the category count differs between places because name/contact are excluded from some evaluations; this should be made more explicit earlier. There are also a few wording and grammatical rough edges, but nothing major.

To strengthen the paper, I would suggest several concrete improvements. First, clarify the threat model and narrow the privacy claims unless you can add a more operational privacy evaluation, such as human or model-based inference of original attributes before and after abstraction, or a re-identification/profiling reduction analysis. Second, improve the importance-rating evaluation by reporting agreement-aware metrics, calibration, or performance against majority labels in addition to the permissive “matches any annotator” accuracy. Third, provide more analysis of failure cases for both detection and abstraction, ideally broken down by category and by whether errors are under- or over-protective. Fourth, discuss more explicitly the implications of training abstraction on GPT-generated labels, and if possible include at least a small human-authored abstraction set for validation. Fifth, for the user study, add more methodological detail on coding and perhaps soften the generality of the conclusions. Sixth, since the paper envisions a pipeline, some end-to-end evaluation would be valuable: how often does the detector identify spans that the abstraction model can successfully rewrite, and how do users perceive the combined workflow?

My overall assessment is positive. This is a creative, socially meaningful paper that opens a worthwhile research direction at the intersection of NLP, privacy, and HCI. The dataset and task formulations are likely to be useful to the community, and the user-centered framing is a real strength. The main weaknesses are not in the idea but in the evidential strength of some claims: annotation reliability is modest, abstraction training relies on synthetic supervision, and privacy improvement is measured perceptually rather than operationally. If the paper is positioned as an initial step and the claims are calibrated accordingly, I think it makes a solid contribution.

In summary, I see this as a strong and promising paper with meaningful novelty and practical relevance, but with enough methodological caveats that I would stop short of calling the empirical validation definitive. My recommendation would be weak accept to accept, depending on the conference bar and how much weight is placed on user-centered problem formulation versus rigorous privacy measurement.
