Review ID: MV_042
Validation Track: RQ2
============================================================

This paper studies reward overoptimization in offline RLHF and proposes a theoretically motivated objective whose practical instantiation is simply DPO plus an SFT term, termed RPO. The central claim is appealing: the SFT loss can be interpreted as an implicit adversarial regularizer arising from a maximin formulation over uncertain reward models, and this regularization mitigates overoptimization under partial coverage. The paper combines a nontrivial theoretical development with empirical results on 7B chat models showing modest but consistent gains over DPO. Overall, I found the paper interesting and potentially impactful, especially because it connects a widely used practical heuristic—mixing preference optimization with supervised imitation—to a principled pessimistic objective. At the same time, there are several conceptual and empirical gaps that currently limit how convincing the paper is as a top-tier conference contribution.

The main strength of the paper is the attempt to bridge theory and practice in RLHF. The maximin objective in Section 3 is a sensible way to encode pessimism under reward uncertainty, and the derivation to a practical objective in Section 4 is elegant at a high level. The resulting RPO objective is simple, easy to implement, and directly relevant to current LLM alignment pipelines. This is a meaningful contribution because many theoretical offline RLHF methods are difficult to instantiate in modern language model training. The sample complexity result under partial coverage is also directionally valuable: it formalizes the intuition that overoptimization is fundamentally tied to distribution shift and uncertainty in poorly covered regions. The empirical section, while limited, does support the claim that adding an SFT term can improve DPO-like training in the tested settings.

That said, my biggest concern is the gap between the formal theory and the actual practical algorithm. The theoretical guarantees are for Algorithm 1, and then transferred to minimax-based algorithms via an equivalence theorem under regularity assumptions on the reward class. However, the practical RPO implementation does not optimize over the reward class at all; it directly optimizes a parameterized policy class. This is standard in DPO-style derivations, but the paper is not sufficiently precise about what assumptions are needed for the policy parameterization to faithfully represent the induced reward class and for optimization over neural policies to inherit the guarantees. As written, the theory justifies an idealized minimax objective, not necessarily the neural RPO training procedure used in experiments. This is not fatal, but the paper sometimes overstates the practical-theoretical connection.

Relatedly, the equivalence result relies on fairly strong and somewhat abstract assumptions. Assumption 5.5 requires compactness and a convex-like property of the reward class with respect to the objective. This is plausible for some function classes, but not obviously for the neural reward/policy parameterizations relevant to LLMs. The paper mentions Lipschitz continuous model classes and convex classes as examples, but this does not really address the practical setting. Since the headline claim is “provably mitigating overoptimization in RLHF” and the practical message is about LLM alignment, the paper should be much clearer that the proof applies to an idealized function-approximation regime, not to transformer fine-tuning as performed experimentally.

A second major concern is novelty relative to existing preference optimization methods that combine preference loss with supervised or imitation-style regularization. The paper cites ORPO and Smaug/DPO-positive style works, but the differentiation is not fully convincing. The claimed novelty is the theoretical interpretation of SFT as an adversarial regularizer. That may indeed be novel, but algorithmically the practical method is quite close to “DPO + weighted SFT on chosen responses,” which many practitioners would view as a natural baseline rather than a fundamentally new algorithm. For this reason, the paper’s value depends heavily on the strength and clarity of the theory. At present, the theory is interesting but not yet sharp enough to fully elevate the practical method beyond a well-motivated regularized baseline.

On the theoretical side, I also have some questions about the exact interpretation of the guarantees. Theorem 5.3 gives a gap bound against any comparator policy under a partial coverage coefficient and includes an additive term involving the KL from the comparator to the reference policy. This means the guarantee is strongest only for policies close to the reference and well covered by the data. That is reasonable, but it weakens the broad claim of mitigating overoptimization in RLHF. In particular, the guarantee does not show that RPO is better than DPO, only that the pessimistic objective is sample-efficient under certain conditions. The paper’s narrative sometimes suggests a stronger comparative statement than the theorem actually proves. It would help to explicitly state that the theory establishes safety/sample efficiency of the proposed objective, not a formal dominance result over DPO.

There are also some technical presentation issues that make the theory harder to trust than it should be. In Section 5.2, the notation appears inconsistent: equation (5.4) uses the baseline policy as pi^ref rather than pi^base, which seems at odds with earlier definitions. The theorem statement around equation (5.7) also appears to swap argument order in phi(hat r, pi) versus phi(pi, r). There are several typographical and mathematical imprecisions throughout, including the definition of the surrogate reward in equation (4.10), where the notation for the log-ratio seems malformed. These may be fixable, but in a theory-heavy paper they matter because they make it difficult to verify the derivations.

The empirical evaluation is promising but not yet strong enough. The experiments are limited to two 7B model families and compare mainly against DPO with matched hyperparameters. The gains on benchmark metrics are consistent but modest. More importantly, the paper does not sufficiently disentangle whether the improvements come from the specific adversarially motivated regularization or simply from adding extra supervised signal. Since the practical method is DPO plus SFT on chosen responses, stronger baselines are essential. At minimum, I would want comparisons to a generic mixed objective baseline where the SFT coefficient is tuned similarly but without the theoretical framing, and to recent methods such as ORPO or other monolithic preference optimization variants that already incorporate likelihood terms. As presented, the empirical evidence shows that one particular DPO+SFT recipe helps, but not that the proposed formulation is uniquely effective.

The choice and tuning of the regularization coefficient eta also raises concerns. The theory prescribes eta scaling like 1/sqrt(N), while the experiments choose eta=0.005 based on test-split performance. This is understandable in practice, but it further widens the theory-practice gap. Also, selecting eta on the test split of the training dataset is not ideal; even if separate prompts are held out from the final evaluation, this should be described more carefully to avoid concerns about overfitting model selection to the in-distribution benchmark. A validation split would be more appropriate.

The evaluation methodology relies heavily on GPT-4 judgments, including filtering out “undesired annotations” and retaining only 150 examples for in-distribution comparison. This is a fairly small sample, and the paper does not report confidence intervals or significance tests. Given that the pairwise win-rate improvements over DPO are only a few points, uncertainty estimates are important. The same applies to MT-Bench and AlpacaEval differences, especially since these benchmarks can have evaluator variance and length effects. The paper should report variance across seeds, confidence intervals, or bootstrap intervals for win rates. Without them, it is hard to assess whether the gains are robust.

I also found the overoptimization diagnosis somewhat underdeveloped empirically. The paper uses decreasing log-probability on chosen responses as evidence of overoptimization, following prior work. This is a useful signal, but it is indirect. If the central claim is that RPO mitigates reward hacking induced by distribution shift and reward uncertainty, stronger diagnostics would help: for example, analysis of reward-model exploitation if an explicit reward model is available, calibration or uncertainty analyses, or controlled synthetic experiments where coverage can be manipulated. Even a smaller-scale contextual bandit simulation aligned with the theory could substantially strengthen the paper by showing the mechanism more directly than LLM benchmark gains alone.

Another issue is that the baseline policy choice is underexplored. The theory emphasizes the role of pi^base and suggests several possibilities, but the experiments only use chosen responses from the dataset. Since the paper argues that the baseline distribution should be “suitably covered,” it would be valuable to compare different choices: chosen responses, reference model samples, SFT data, or mixtures. This would both test the theory and provide practical guidance. As it stands, one of the paper’s potentially interesting knobs is not really investigated.

The discussion of generalization to new prompt distributions is also weaker than it appears. Corollary 5.8 assumes bounded density ratio between d1 and d0, but the empirical support is a PCA plot of prompt embeddings. This is at best a qualitative visualization and does not meaningfully validate the assumption. I would recommend toning down the claim here. The benchmark improvements are useful evidence of transfer, but they do not substantiate the density-ratio-based theorem in any rigorous sense.

Despite these criticisms, I do think the paper has real merit. The core conceptual message—that supervised imitation can be derived as a pessimistic regularizer against uncertain rewards—is interesting and likely useful to the community. The practical objective is simple and likely to be adopted if the empirical case is strengthened. The theory, while idealized, is more substantive than many alignment papers and could form the basis for future work. My overall assessment is therefore positive but cautious: the paper is not yet fully convincing in its current form, mainly because the empirical validation is too narrow for a practical method that is algorithmically close to existing baselines, and because the theory-to-practice connection is looser than the title and framing suggest.

To strengthen the paper, I would strongly encourage the authors to add more competitive baselines, especially ORPO and a generic DPO+SFT mixture baseline with tuned coefficients; report confidence intervals and multiple seeds; include ablations over eta and the choice of baseline policy; and clarify the exact scope of the theoretical guarantees. A small synthetic or semi-synthetic experiment where coverage can be controlled would also be very valuable for validating the proposed mechanism. Finally, the paper needs a careful proofreading pass for notation consistency and mathematical precision.

In summary, this is an interesting and potentially impactful paper with a nice high-level idea and some promising evidence, but it currently falls short of fully substantiating its strongest claims. I would lean borderline positive if the venue values theory-practice bridging and simple deployable methods, but I think the paper needs clearer positioning and stronger empirical support to be a solid acceptance.

My main questions for the authors are as follows. First, how should one interpret the guarantees for neural RPO training, given that the proofs are for idealized reward/policy classes and exact optimization? Second, how does RPO compare empirically to ORPO and to a plain weighted DPO+SFT baseline tuned with the same budget? Third, how sensitive are the results to the choice of pi^base and to eta? Fourth, can the authors provide uncertainty estimates over the reported win rates and benchmark scores? Fifth, can the authors clarify the notation inconsistencies in Section 5.2 and confirm whether they are only typographical?

My tentative recommendation is weak reject to borderline weak accept, depending on the strength of revisions and rebuttal.
