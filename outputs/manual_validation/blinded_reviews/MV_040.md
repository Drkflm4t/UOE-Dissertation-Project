# Review ID: MV_040

**Validation Track:** `RQ2`

---

This paper tackles an important and underexplored problem: helping users identify and mitigate privacy risks in online self-disclosure. The work combines three components that are individually valuable and collectively quite compelling: a new span-level taxonomy and annotated corpus of self-disclosures, a token/span tagging model for disclosure identification, and a generation task for “self-disclosure abstraction” motivated by a formative user study. The topic is timely, societally relevant, and well aligned with growing interest in user-facing privacy tools rather than only adversarial/privacy-attack analyses. I found the paper ambitious and interesting, with clear practical motivation and several genuinely novel elements. At the same time, there are important methodological and evaluation limitations that currently weaken the strength of the empirical claims, especially around annotation quality, abstraction evaluation, and the interpretation of “privacy reduction.”

The strongest aspect of the paper is the problem framing. Much prior work either focuses on narrow disclosure types, sentence-level classification, or classical PII detection/redaction. This paper argues persuasively that user-facing privacy assistance requires finer granularity and broader semantic coverage: users need to know exactly which spans are risky, and many risky disclosures are not standard PII. The proposed 19-category taxonomy is therefore a meaningful contribution, and the distinction between attributes and experiences is intuitive. The examples make the task concrete, and the motivation for abstraction rather than blunt redaction is also convincing. In particular, the paper’s emphasis on preserving communicative utility is a real strength and differentiates it from standard anonymization pipelines.

The dataset contribution appears useful, but the presentation leaves several unanswered questions that are important for assessing quality and reproducibility. The corpus contains 2.4K posts and 4.8K annotated spans, which is a reasonable starting point, though not especially large for a 19-class span tagging problem with substantial class imbalance. The long-tail categories are extremely sparse, with only 21 name spans and 14 contact spans, and some other sensitive categories such as sexual orientation are also rare. This sparsity is reflected in the per-class results. More importantly, the paper does not report core annotation statistics that are essential for a dataset paper: inter-annotator agreement for the initial disclosure filtering stage, agreement for span boundaries, agreement for category labels, adjudication protocol details, and examples of difficult edge cases. The paper mentions that only posts both annotators agreed were disclosing were retained, but this introduces a conservative selection bias and may systematically exclude ambiguous but realistic disclosures. It would be important to know how many potentially positive posts were discarded due to disagreement, and whether this affects the downstream model’s calibration on borderline cases. Likewise, because the annotation instructions prefer contextualized spans such as “I live in the US” rather than minimal spans like “US,” the task definition departs from standard NER-like span extraction. That is a defensible design choice for usability, but it makes boundary agreement especially important to quantify.

The identification model results are promising but should be interpreted cautiously. The best token-level F1 of 75.3 for multi-class and 81.8 for binary is respectable, and the sentence-level segmentation finding is sensible and practically useful. However, the span-level F1 is much lower, around 49.0 multi-class and 54.5 binary, which is arguably the more relevant metric for a highlighting tool. Since the system is intended to show users exact spans, token F1 may overstate practical usefulness. The paper does include partial-span and exact-span metrics, which is good, but the narrative in the abstract and conclusion emphasizes the stronger token-level number. I would encourage the authors to foreground exact-span performance more clearly. The per-class breakdown also reveals substantial variability: age and age/gender are relatively easy, while relationship status and sexual orientation are much weaker. Since some of the most privacy-sensitive categories may also be among the hardest, the paper should discuss this mismatch more directly.

Methodologically, the identification setup is somewhat heterogeneous in a way that complicates interpretation. Most categories are handled by a fine-tuned RoBERTa tagger, but name/contact are delegated to LUKE, Presidio, regexes, and an additional sentence classifier. This hybrid engineering is reasonable for deployment, but then the paper should evaluate the full pipeline consistently and explain how outputs from these components are merged with the sequence tagger. Are the reported F1 scores for the 19-class task inclusive of these external detectors, or only for the RoBERTa-tagged subset? Table 3 excludes name and contact entirely, presumably due to low counts, but the paper’s framing suggests broad 19-category coverage. The distinction between what is learned from the corpus and what is inherited from external tools should be made much clearer.

The user study is a welcome addition and one of the paper’s most distinctive features. Too many NLP privacy papers stop at benchmark metrics, so it is valuable that the authors interviewed actual Reddit users and surfaced concerns around oversensitivity, subreddit norms, personalization, and explainability. These findings are plausible and useful. However, the study should be framed more carefully as formative and qualitative rather than as strong validation of real-world applicability. The sample size is small (21 participants), restricted to U.S.-based adult Reddit users, and the procedure includes manual preparation of model outputs for presentation. The claim that “82% of participants viewed the model positively” is encouraging but should not be overinterpreted as evidence of deployment readiness. Also, because participants brought posts that already raised privacy concerns, there may be selection effects toward users predisposed to value such a tool. I appreciated the paper’s honesty that some users found the model oversensitive; in fact, this may be one of the most important findings in the paper, because it highlights the gap between annotator-defined disclosure and user-perceived risk. That gap has major implications for both labeling and evaluation, and I would have liked to see it elevated from a user-study observation to a central methodological discussion.

The abstraction task is novel and potentially impactful, but it is also the least mature part of the paper. The idea of rewriting disclosure spans into less specific alternatives while preserving utility is compelling. The examples are intuitive, and the distinction from redaction is well motivated. However, the training and evaluation setup raises several concerns. First, the abstraction data are generated by ChatGPT/GPT-4 rather than written by humans. This is understandable as a bootstrapping strategy, but it means the models are largely trained to imitate another LLM’s notion of abstraction rather than human preferences or actual privacy outcomes. Since the test references are also GPT-4-generated, automatic overlap metrics mostly measure agreement with GPT-4 style. This is not fatal, but it substantially limits what can be concluded from BLEU/ROUGE. The paper does include human evaluation, which helps, but the dependence on synthetic references should be acknowledged more explicitly as a core limitation rather than a practical detail.

Second, the abstraction dataset is very small: 159 posts for training, 25 for validation, and 50 for test, drawn from the most recent 10% of the corpus. It is not entirely clear how many span instances this corresponds to, which matters more than post count for the generation task. Given the small scale, the exploration of many prompting/training variants may be underpowered, and differences in BLEU/ROUGE are likely not statistically meaningful. No confidence intervals or significance tests are reported. The paper also does not provide enough implementation detail for the Llama-2 fine-tuning setup: training epochs, learning rates, LoRA ranks, decoding parameters, and whether the same hyperparameters were used across all configurations.

Third, the evaluation of “privacy reduction” is conceptually weak at present. The paper reports Likert ratings such as 3.2/5 privacy reduction and 4.0/5 utility preservation, but there is no operational definition of privacy risk beyond “less specific details.” In some cases, abstraction may preserve enough semantic content to remain highly identifying when combined with context. For example, replacing “UCLA” with “a top university in California” may still be identifying in context; replacing “32” with “early 30s” may reduce specificity only marginally. More fundamentally, privacy risk is adversarial and contextual, not just lexical. A stronger evaluation would test whether abstracted text reduces attribute inference accuracy, re-identification risk, or human ability to recover the original sensitive attribute. Without such measures, the claim that the model “reduces privacy risks” remains suggestive rather than demonstrated. The current human ratings show that outputs are perceived as somewhat less specific, which is useful, but not equivalent to privacy protection.

Relatedly, the ethics statement contains a claim that model failures “do not pose additional risks but rather tend towards overprotection,” which I do not think is justified. Under-abstraction or misleading abstraction could plausibly create a false sense of safety, causing users to post content they otherwise would not. Incorrect highlighting could also erode trust or disproportionately burden users in communities where disclosure is normative or necessary. The statement that no harms were identified for marginalized populations is also too strong given that the paper itself motivates the problem partly through risks to marginalized users. These users may be especially affected by false negatives, false reassurance, or category-specific errors. I would encourage a more nuanced ethics discussion.

There are also several issues of clarity and reporting. The paper has a noticeable number of grammatical errors and typos, including “broad the scope,” “fine-graiend,” “perform better,” “the loss are ignored,” and others. These do not obscure the main ideas, but they do detract from polish. Some tables are difficult to interpret from the text alone. Table 5 in particular appears to contain formatting issues, and the meaning of some columns is not immediately clear. The human evaluation protocol for abstraction is underspecified: how many annotators, whether they were blinded to system identity, how disagreements were handled, and whether inter-rater reliability was measured. The statement that “one of the authors conduct human evaluation” is especially concerning; author-only evaluation is vulnerable to bias, particularly for subjective criteria like privacy reduction and utility. At minimum, multiple independent raters should be used, ideally with expertise or target-user relevance.

I also think the paper could do more to situate the taxonomy relative to existing privacy frameworks. The 19 categories are practical, but some category boundaries seem ad hoc or overlapping. For example, relationship status, husband/BF, and wife/GF are separated in a way that may encode unnecessary gender assumptions and complicate modeling. Why not a unified partner/relationship taxonomy with attributes for specificity? Similarly, health and mental health are separated, but family disclosures can contain age/gender/location information about relatives. The annotation rule to collapse disclosures about others into “family” is understandable, yet it may obscure privacy-relevant structure. A more principled discussion of taxonomy design tradeoffs would strengthen the contribution.

Despite these concerns, I think the paper has real merit. The combination of span-level disclosure detection, user-centered evaluation, and abstraction as a mitigation mechanism is novel and potentially impactful. The identification dataset and baseline models could become a useful resource for future work, and the user study surfaces important design tensions that the community should pay attention to. The abstraction task, while preliminary, opens an interesting direction beyond redaction. My overall assessment is that this is a promising paper with strong ideas and moderate empirical support, but it currently falls short of the rigor expected for a top-tier ML/NLP venue because several central claims are not yet fully substantiated.

To strengthen the paper, I would prioritize the following revisions. First, add thorough annotation quality analysis: agreement metrics for disclosure presence, span boundaries, and labels; adjudication details; and error examples. Second, clarify the exact evaluation pipeline for the 19-category identification system, especially the role of external detectors and whether reported scores reflect the full system. Third, improve the abstraction evaluation with independent human raters and, if possible, a more objective privacy metric such as reduction in attribute inference or re-identification performance before versus after abstraction. Fourth, temper claims about privacy protection and real-world applicability, framing the current results as promising but preliminary. Fifth, provide fuller implementation details and, where feasible, significance estimates or confidence intervals. Sixth, discuss taxonomy design choices and fairness implications more carefully, especially for sensitive and marginalized groups.

If I were scoring this paper, I would place it in the borderline accept / weak accept range for a venue that values socially meaningful, interdisciplinary, and exploratory work, but closer to weak reject for a venue that prioritizes airtight empirical validation. The ideas are strong and the problem matters; the main gap is that the evaluation, especially for abstraction, does not yet fully support the strength of the claims.
