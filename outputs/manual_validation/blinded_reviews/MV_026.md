# Review ID: MV_026

**Validation Track:** `RQ1`

---

This paper introduces X-Topic, a new multilingual multi-label topic classification benchmark for X/Twitter posts in English, Spanish, Japanese, and Greek, built around a 19-topic taxonomy inherited from prior TweetTopic work. The paper also provides a fairly extensive empirical study spanning zero-shot, few-shot, monolingual, cross-lingual, and multilingual settings, comparing both general multilingual encoders and social-media-specialized models, as well as prompted LLMs. Overall, I found the paper timely, useful, and well executed. The main contribution is clearly the dataset itself, but the accompanying analysis is also valuable and gives the paper substance beyond a pure resource release.

The strongest aspect of the paper is that it addresses a real gap in current NLP resources. There are many topic classification datasets for news and other formal domains, but multilingual labeled social media topic datasets remain scarce, especially with a reasonably broad taxonomy and explicit multi-label annotation. The choice to focus on social media is important because short, noisy, culturally situated posts are qualitatively different from standard document classification benchmarks. The paper makes a convincing case that existing unsupervised topic modeling approaches are often hard to compare across corpora and that supervised multilingual resources are lacking. In that sense, X-Topic fills a meaningful niche.

I also appreciated that the dataset design is not trivial or artificially simplified. The benchmark covers four typologically and culturally diverse languages, including a lower-resource European language (Greek) and a non-Indo-European language (Japanese), which makes the benchmark more interesting than a standard English-plus-major-European-language setup. The paper’s descriptive analysis supports the claim that the languages differ not only lexically but also in topic distribution, average post length, and emoji usage. The bar chart on page 4 and the statistics on page 5 make this concrete. This diversity strengthens the benchmark’s value for multilingual and cross-lingual evaluation.

The data collection and annotation pipeline is generally thoughtful. The authors collect a large raw pool per language, apply multiple preprocessing steps, and then annotate 1,000 tweets per language with five annotators each. The use of five annotators per instance is a strong design choice for a subjective multi-label task. The decision to assign a label when at least two annotators agree, rather than requiring majority agreement, is debatable but defensible, and the authors explicitly motivate it as a way to preserve ambiguity and realism. I also appreciated the transparency around annotation quality: Table 2 on page 4 reports Krippendorff’s alpha, percent agreement, overlap, and average number of topics per tweet. The alpha values are modest, but for a multi-label social media task with overlapping categories, they are not implausible, and the paper appropriately contextualizes them against prior work such as GoEmotions and Affect in Tweets.

The empirical section is another major strength. Rather than presenting a narrow benchmark with one or two baselines, the paper systematically studies several practically relevant settings: zero-shot, few-shot, cross-lingual transfer from English TweetTopic, monolingual fine-tuning on the target language, multilingual training on all languages, and variants that additionally include the original English TweetTopic data. This breadth makes the paper useful both as a dataset paper and as an analysis paper. The results in Table 3 on page 7 are rich and reveal several interesting findings: social-media-pretrained encoders outperform generic XLM-R; multilingual training substantially improves over monolingual training; cross-lingual transfer from the larger English TweetTopic dataset can outperform target-language-only training when target data are scarce; and few-shot GPT-4o is competitive but still does not clearly dominate fine-tuned encoder models. These are all practically relevant conclusions.

I found the comparison between domain-specific encoders and prompted LLMs particularly interesting. The paper avoids simplistic “LLMs solve everything” messaging and instead shows a more nuanced picture. GPT-4o performs strongly in few-shot settings and is competitive with fine-tuned models, but the best fine-tuned multilingual encoder still achieves the top overall macro-F1. This is a useful result for the community, especially because topic classification on short social media posts is a realistic industrial use case where efficiency and deployability matter. The precision/recall comparison in Table 5 on page 8 adds further insight by showing that some prompted models tend to overpredict labels, whereas the fine-tuned encoder is more calibrated.

The error analysis is also a positive aspect. It is not extremely deep, but it goes beyond aggregate scores and identifies difficult labels such as Arts & Culture and Other Hobbies. The per-topic tables on page 17 are useful for understanding where models fail. In particular, the weakness on “Other Hobbies” is unsurprising but important, since it reflects the inherent fuzziness of residual categories. The discussion of borderline examples also helps illustrate that some errors may reflect ambiguity in the annotation scheme rather than pure model failure.

The paper is generally well written and easy to follow. The motivation, dataset construction, and experimental settings are clearly organized. I also appreciate that the authors commit to releasing the dataset, annotations, and trained models, which materially increases the paper’s impact and reproducibility. The ethics section is brief but relevant, especially regarding user anonymization and annotator compensation.

That said, there are a few limitations and points that would benefit from clarification. First, the dataset size is somewhat modest: 1,000 annotated tweets per language, for 4,000 total. This is still useful, especially given five-way annotation, but it does limit the benchmark’s scale and may partly explain why multilingual transfer from the much larger English TweetTopic dataset is so strong. The paper acknowledges budget constraints, and I do not view this as a fatal issue, but it would be helpful if the authors more explicitly discussed expected use cases: is X-Topic primarily intended as an evaluation benchmark, a fine-tuning resource, or both?

Second, the sampling strategy may introduce a bias toward more popular or higher-visibility content. On page 3, the authors state that final sampling is weighted by retweet count and follower count under the assumption that higher-quality content is more popular. This is understandable from a quality-control perspective, but it may skew the dataset away from ordinary user content and toward more mainstream, polished, or influencer-driven discourse. Since the paper emphasizes realism and “closer to the real distribution,” this choice deserves a more careful discussion. At minimum, I would like to see the authors acknowledge that popularity-weighted sampling may alter the topical and stylistic distribution of the benchmark.

Third, some preprocessing decisions may affect ecological validity. The removal of tweets containing URLs and tweets with more than four emojis or mentions likely improves annotation quality, but these are common features of social media communication. Similarly, masking only non-verified users raises a small question about whether verified-user mentions remain identifiable. None of these choices are unreasonable, but the paper could better articulate the tradeoff between privacy/cleanliness and representativeness.

Fourth, while the annotation agreement is acceptable for this kind of task, the label assignment rule of “at least two annotators agree” may produce relatively permissive multi-label sets. This likely contributes to the average of 1.8 labels per tweet and may make the task harder and noisier. I would have liked a small ablation comparing alternative aggregation rules, such as majority vote or confidence-weighted labels, even on a subset. Such an analysis could help readers understand how much of the benchmark difficulty comes from genuine semantic overlap versus annotation permissiveness.

Fifth, the paper could do a bit more to justify the topic taxonomy itself in the multilingual setting. The taxonomy comes from prior English work and was designed by social media experts, which is a reasonable starting point. However, some categories may transfer less naturally across cultures or may have different salience in different languages. The topic distribution differences shown in Figure 1 suggest this possibility. A short discussion of whether any labels were especially difficult to translate or culturally adapt would strengthen the resource contribution.

There are also a few smaller presentation issues. Some wording in the analysis section is occasionally imprecise or slightly confusing. For example, the discussion of cross-lingual versus monolingual performance should more clearly separate the effect of transfer from the effect of training set size, since TweetTopic has over 11k examples while each target-language training fold has only 720 training instances. The paper does mention this, but the framing sometimes risks overstating cross-lingual superiority when data volume is a major confound. Likewise, the zero-/few-shot prompting setup is intentionally simple, but because prompt design can materially affect LLM performance, the conclusions about LLMs should be interpreted as applying to this prompting protocol rather than to the models’ full potential.

Despite these caveats, I think the paper makes a solid contribution. The dataset is useful, the experiments are broad and informative, and the findings are relevant to both academic and applied multilingual NLP. In particular, the paper’s demonstration that multilingual and domain-specific encoder models remain highly competitive for social media topic classification is valuable, and the benchmark itself should enable more systematic future work in this area.

My main suggestions for improvement are therefore relatively straightforward: expand the discussion of sampling bias and representativeness; clarify the implications of the two-annotator aggregation rule; discuss the taxonomy’s cross-cultural transferability in a bit more depth; and, if possible, add a small analysis of alternative label aggregation or calibration strategies. None of these issues undermine the core contribution.

Overall, I view this as a strong resource-and-analysis paper. It addresses an important gap, is empirically careful, and is likely to be useful to the community. I would be positive about acceptance.
