# Review ID: MV_048

**Validation Track:** `RQ1`

---

This paper proposes CoT-Influx, a plug-and-play framework for improving LLM mathematical reasoning by pruning few-shot chain-of-thought context at two levels: first selecting useful CoT exemplars (“shot pruning”), then removing unimportant tokens within retained exemplars (“token pruning”). The method is trained with reinforcement learning using a newly constructed GPT-4-assisted math reasoning dataset, MRD3, designed to diversify difficulty and reasoning-step distributions. Empirically, the paper reports gains across LLaMA2-7B/13B/70B and five math benchmarks, with particularly strong results on GSM8K, where LLaMA2-70B + CoT-Influx reportedly surpasses GPT-3.5 and several much larger models under prompting-only settings.

Overall, I found the paper interesting, well motivated, and technically meaningful. The central premise is simple but important: the bottleneck in few-shot CoT prompting is not only retrieval quality but also context inefficiency, and better use of the fixed context budget can materially improve reasoning. This is a valuable perspective. Much prior work either retrieves whole exemplars, compresses prompts with generic heuristics, or extends context windows. In contrast, this paper explicitly targets the “effective information density” of few-shot reasoning demonstrations. That framing is compelling, and the coarse-to-fine pruning design is a natural and elegant way to operationalize it.

A major strength is the paper’s empirical motivation. The pilot study in Section 3 is useful and convincing. The plot on page 3/Figure 1 shows that increasing the number of retrieved CoT examples can improve GSM8K accuracy up to a point, but eventually runs into context limitations and response-space tradeoffs. Table 1 on the same page also makes an important point: simply adding more examples at random can hurt performance. Figure 2 further illustrates why generic compression is brittle for math reasoning, since numerical and formatting tokens that appear redundant under standard compression criteria may actually be essential. These observations justify the method well and make the problem formulation feel grounded rather than contrived.

The method itself is reasonably clear. The overview diagram on page 4/Figure 3 is especially helpful: it shows the full pipeline from GPT-4 evolution of MRD3, to retrieval of candidate shots, to shot pruning, token pruning, and final prompting of a frozen target LLM. The decision to treat the target LLM as a black box is practically attractive. The use of a lightweight external encoder plus a small policy network makes the approach modular and potentially deployable across models without modifying the base LLM. I also appreciated that the authors explicitly discuss why RL is needed here: pruning decisions are discrete and occur outside the LLM tokenizer/forward path, so standard backpropagation is unavailable.

The paper’s experimental results are strong. On GSM8K, Table 2 shows consistent improvements over zero-shot, zero-shot-CoT, standard 8-shot CoT, retrieval baselines, and prompt compression baselines. The gains over TopK retrieval are not enormous in absolute terms for the largest model, but they are meaningful given the maturity of the benchmark and the fact that the method does not fine-tune the target LLM. The comparison against compression baselines is particularly striking: methods such as Selective Context and LLMLingua degrade badly in this setting, supporting the paper’s claim that math reasoning requires preserving structure and numerically salient tokens. Table 3 extends the story beyond GSM8K to AddSub, MultiArith, Svamp, and SingleEq, where CoT-Influx again performs best on average. Table 5 also shows compatibility with self-consistency and self-verification, which strengthens the claim that this is a complementary prompting module rather than a narrowly tuned standalone trick.

The ablations are another positive aspect. Table 6 suggests that the MRD3 dataset matters, not just the pruning architecture. Table 7 shows that both shot-level and token-level pruning contribute, with token pruning appearing especially important. Figure 5 on page 8 quantifies the compression ratio after each stage, which helps interpret what the model is actually doing. The appendix also contains useful additional analyses: Table 11 studies reward components, Table 12 examines sensitivity to token target and penalty coefficients, and Table 10 shows that the method can also help instruction-tuned models such as LLaMA2-13B-Chat and GPT-3.5-Turbo. These additions improve confidence that the gains are not due to a single fragile configuration.

That said, I do have several concerns, mostly around evaluation rigor, fairness of comparisons, and reproducibility.

The biggest issue is disentangling where the gains come from. CoT-Influx combines several ingredients: a new GPT-4-generated/evolved dataset, TopK retrieval of candidates, a difficulty-aware filtering scheme, a two-stage RL pruner, manual 8-shot CoTs appended during training for stabilization, and rule-based prompt reconstruction at inference time (Appendix C.5). Because many components are introduced together, it is difficult to isolate how much of the final improvement is attributable specifically to reinforced context pruning versus improved demonstration data engineering and prompt repair heuristics. The paper does include some ablations, but I still think the causal story is not fully clean. In particular, the rule-based reconstruction step seems important: if the pruner removes structural tokens like “Q:”, “A:”, “Let’s think step by step”, and “The answer is”, these are later repaired by hand-coded rules. This means the final system is not purely learned pruning; it relies on a nontrivial post-processing scaffold. The paper should be more explicit about how often reconstruction is triggered and how much it contributes to final accuracy.

Relatedly, the comparison to prompt compression baselines may not be entirely fair. The paper argues that generic compressors fail because they remove crucial math tokens, which is plausible and supported by examples in Appendix A.5. However, CoT-Influx is trained specifically for the downstream reasoning objective using target-model feedback, while LLMLingua, Selective Context, and GPT-4 compression are used more or less off the shelf. This makes the comparison somewhat asymmetric: one side is task- and model-adapted, the others are generic. That does not invalidate the result, but the framing should be more careful. A stronger comparison would include a task-adapted version of a compression baseline, or at least a discussion that the advantage may partly come from supervision specificity rather than the coarse-to-fine structure alone.

I also worry about the fairness of some retrieval comparisons. The paper compares against TopK/BM25 using the maximum number of full examples that fit in the context window, while CoT-Influx starts from a much larger candidate pool and then compresses it into a shorter final prompt. This is a reasonable use case, but it means the baseline is not allowed to exploit any compression or learned selection beyond whole-example retrieval. A more informative baseline would be something like learned retrieval plus truncation/compression, or a stronger supervised retriever tailored to math reasoning. The related work mentions such methods, but they do not appear in the main experiments.

Another concern is the use of GPT-4 in constructing MRD3. The dataset is central to the method, and the paper shows that it helps. But this raises questions about cost, reproducibility, and possible benchmark contamination. Since GSM8K training data is part of the seed corpus and GPT-4 is used to reformat/evolve examples, one would like more discussion of whether the resulting exemplars are simply better written demonstrations rather than evidence that pruning itself is the key innovation. More importantly, because GPT-4 is used both to generate CoTs and to assign difficulty scores, the method inherits the biases and possible errors of GPT-4 judgments. The paper would benefit from reporting some manual validation of difficulty labels and generated CoT quality.

The RL formulation is interesting but somewhat under-specified in practice. Equation 5 defines a reward combining inverse loss, exact-match accuracy, and a token-budget term, but the optimization details remain fairly high level. Since RL with sparse/discrete rewards can be unstable, I would have liked more detail on variance reduction, baselines, reward normalization, and training cost. Figure 7 in the appendix gives some training curves, which is helpful, but the main paper could better explain how robust training is across seeds. I did not see variance bars or multi-run statistics in the main results. Given that the reported gains are sometimes a few points, confidence intervals would be important.

There are also some methodological ambiguities. The notation and exposition are occasionally rough. For example, the paper says the action space is {0,1} but also refers to a “continuous categorical distribution” and “sampling from the categorical distribution π with a softmax function,” while Equations 3 and 4 use sigmoid outputs. This is not fatal, but the policy parameterization should be described more precisely. Similarly, the reward term involving (t/T)^w is a bit unintuitive as written, especially since the text says w<0 penalizes prompts that are too short and w>0 penalizes exceeding the limit; the exact behavior deserves clearer explanation. There are also a number of writing issues and typos throughout the paper and appendix that should be cleaned up.

On the experimental side, I would have liked broader evaluation beyond arithmetic/math word problems. The paper’s claims are framed around “LLM reasoning,” but all core experiments are on math reasoning benchmarks. That is a perfectly valid scope, but the title and some claims could be interpreted more broadly than the evidence supports. It would strengthen the paper to either narrow the claims consistently to math reasoning or include at least one non-math reasoning domain where structured few-shot CoT matters.

I also think the comparison to larger models in Table 4 should be interpreted cautiously. These cross-paper comparisons are inherently noisy because prompting setups, decoding strategies, and evaluation protocols differ. The result is still interesting, but it should not be overclaimed as a definitive superiority statement over GPT-3.5, PaLM, or Minerva.

Despite these concerns, I think the paper makes a meaningful contribution. The idea is novel enough, the motivation is strong, and the empirical evidence is substantial. I especially like that the work identifies a concrete failure mode of generic prompt compression for math reasoning and proposes a targeted alternative that jointly reasons about example selection and token retention. The method is also practically appealing because it is external to the LLM and compatible with black-box usage. Even if some of the gains are partly due to the high-quality MRD3 demonstrations and prompt reconstruction heuristics, that does not diminish the usefulness of the overall system contribution.

For improvement, I would encourage the authors to do the following in a revision. First, quantify the contribution of rule-based reconstruction and manual appended CoTs more explicitly. Second, add stronger adapted baselines, especially supervised retrieval and/or task-aware compression. Third, report variance across multiple runs or seeds. Fourth, provide more transparency on the cost and reproducibility of MRD3 creation, including examples of failure cases in GPT-4 evolution and difficulty scoring. Fifth, clarify the RL policy parameterization and reward behavior. Finally, tighten the claims so they match the demonstrated scope.

In summary, this is a strong and thoughtful paper on a timely problem. It offers a clear conceptual contribution, a technically reasonable method, and convincing empirical gains on multiple math reasoning benchmarks. While there are some concerns about attribution of gains, fairness of baseline adaptation, and reproducibility details, I view these as addressable and not fundamental flaws. My overall assessment is positive. I would lean toward acceptance, especially if the authors can clarify the role of the auxiliary heuristics and strengthen the discussion of evaluation fairness and reproducibility.
