Review ID: MV_020
Validation Track: RQ2
============================================================

This paper tackles an important and underexplored problem: moving spoken dialogue agents beyond turn-based, half-duplex interaction toward genuinely synchronous, full-duplex behavior with overlaps, backchannels, and latency-aware turn-taking. The central idea is to adapt a pretrained text LLM into a wall-clock-synchronous dialogue model by interleaving two speaker streams in fixed-duration chunks, inserting periodic synchronization tokens, and training the model to predict both sides of the dialogue. The paper further proposes a three-stage training recipe that relies heavily on synthetic spoken dialogue derived from text corpora, followed by a relatively small amount of real dual-channel conversational speech. The empirical claim is that this yields substantially better semantic quality than prior full-duplex spoken dialogue models while preserving natural turn-taking behavior, and that the resulting model can operate under realistic latency via short-horizon prediction of the interlocutor’s current chunk.

Overall, I found the paper novel, ambitious, and potentially impactful. The problem formulation is compelling, and the paper makes a serious attempt to bridge a real gap between current voice interfaces and human conversational dynamics. The synchronization mechanism is conceptually elegant: rather than redesigning the architecture around explicit timing modules, the authors encode time through periodic speaker/synchronization tokens and fixed chunking, allowing a standard autoregressive decoder to operate in a temporally grounded way. The latency-tolerant interaction mechanism is also interesting, especially the idea of predicting the user’s missing current chunk to stay synchronized with the wall clock. The training recipe is pragmatic and likely to be useful beyond this specific paper: stage 1 for speech-text alignment in dialogue context, stage 2 for learning the full-duplex sequence format from synthetic no-overlap data, and stage 3 for adapting to real conversational timing phenomena.

The strongest aspect of the paper is the semantic improvement over dGSLM. Both the ASR-based perplexity analysis and the human meaningfulness ratings suggest a large gain in dialogue coherence and plausibility. This is believable given the use of a pretrained Llama3 backbone and large-scale synthetic dialogue data. The human evaluation is especially persuasive on this point: the meaningfulness gap versus dGSLM is very large, while naturalness remains roughly comparable. The out-of-distribution results on CANDOR are also encouraging and support the claim that leveraging text-pretrained LLM knowledge plus general-purpose speech units improves robustness relative to speech-only approaches.

That said, I have several concerns, mostly around evaluation methodology, clarity of the claimed contribution, and whether the evidence fully supports the strongest claims.

First, the paper’s notion of “full-duplex” is partly simulated rather than demonstrated in a realistic end-to-end interactive setting. In the continuation setting, the model generates both sides of the conversation, which is useful for controlled evaluation but does not establish that the system can robustly interact with real users. In the interaction setting, the “user” is another SyncLLM instance, not a human or even a separately engineered speech pipeline. This is a reasonable first step, but it weakens the practical claim that the method enables full-duplex spoken dialogue agents under real Internet-scale latency. The paper would be stronger if it included at least a small real-time human interaction study, or even a controlled offline replay experiment where one side is fixed human speech and the model must respond causally with delayed access to the current chunk. As written, the interaction results show model-model compatibility more than real deployment readiness.

Second, the semantic evaluation via ASR transcription and text-model perplexity is only an indirect proxy for dialogue quality, and some details are underspecified. The paper says overlapping speech is ignored when converting to turn-based text dialogues. But overlap and backchanneling are central to the task, so discarding them may systematically favor models that produce cleaner turn segmentation rather than better full-duplex behavior. It is also unclear which ASR system is used, how robust it is to overlap, whether the same ASR errors affect all systems equally, and what text dialogue model is used for perplexity scoring. Since the main semantic claim relies heavily on this metric, these details matter. The human meaningfulness study partly addresses this concern, but the automatic metric should be better justified.

Third, the naturalness evaluation based on Pearson correlation of average event durations between generated continuations and ground-truth continuations is somewhat unusual and not entirely convincing as a standalone measure of turn-taking quality. Correlation of aggregate IPU/pause/FTO durations conditioned on the same prompt does not necessarily capture whether local turn transitions are appropriate, whether overlaps occur at sensible moments, or whether backchannels are pragmatically well placed. A model could match coarse duration statistics while still sounding unnatural in interaction. The human naturalness MOS is therefore important, but those results show only rough parity with dGSLM and are all clustered around 3.7–4.0, which makes it hard to assess whether the proposed synchronization mechanism truly improves turn-taking quality or mainly preserves it while improving semantics. In other words, the paper convincingly shows “better content, similar naturalness,” but less clearly “better full-duplex conversational behavior.”

Fourth, the comparison to dGSLM, while relevant, may not be sufficient to establish state of the art in 2024/2025-style spoken dialogue modeling. dGSLM is indeed the closest prior full-duplex model, but it is also a speech-only architecture from an earlier generation. Since the main advantage of SyncLLM comes from leveraging a strong pretrained text LLM and synthetic speech-text data, it would be useful to compare against stronger cascaded or multimodal baselines, even if they are half-duplex by design. For example, a streaming ASR + text LLM + TTS pipeline with interruption heuristics might be a meaningful practical baseline, especially since many deployed systems use such architectures. Without such a baseline, the paper demonstrates superiority over a specialized prior full-duplex model, but not necessarily over the strongest practical alternatives.

Fifth, some core design choices are plausible but insufficiently ablated. The chunk size is varied, which is good, but several other choices seem central: the periodic synchronization token design, the decision to always include one speaker tag per chunk, the deduplication strategy, and the interpolation heuristic that equally repeats deduplicated tokens within a chunk. The interpolation step in particular seems potentially lossy, and the paper asserts that the effect is imperceptible without presenting objective or subjective evidence directly isolating this factor. Likewise, the latency-tolerant mechanism depends on predicting the user’s missing chunk and later replacing it with the real chunk, but there is little analysis of how often these predictions are wrong, how harmful mismatches are, or whether exposure bias accumulates during long interactions. An ablation removing user-chunk prediction, or varying the prediction horizon, would help validate the mechanism.

Sixth, there are some presentation and reproducibility issues. Figure 7 is missing in the provided text, which makes it impossible to inspect an important result. Several experimental details are also absent or too brief: the ASR system, the text dialogue model used for perplexity, the vocoder and speech synthesis stack used at inference, decoding parameters, prompt sampling details, and whether dGSLM was retrained or evaluated from released checkpoints under matched conditions. The paper also uses large amounts of synthetic data generated with Bark TTS, but there is little discussion of how TTS artifacts or limited speaker diversity may bias the learned timing and prosody patterns. Since the method relies heavily on synthetic speech, this deserves more scrutiny.

I also have a conceptual concern about what exactly is learned in stage 2 versus stage 3. Stage 2 uses synthetic no-overlap dialogues formatted as full-duplex streams with silence on the non-speaking side. This teaches the sequence format and intra-utterance timing, but not genuine interactional timing. Stage 3 then uses only about 2k hours of real dialogue to learn overlaps, backchannels, and turn-taking. This may be enough in practice, but it suggests that the core full-duplex behavior still depends heavily on scarce real conversational data. The paper should be careful not to overstate how much of “full-duplex dialogue” is learned from synthetic text-derived data versus from real dual-channel speech.

Despite these concerns, I think the paper has substantial merit. The problem is important, the method is original, and the empirical gains in semantic quality are significant. The work also opens a promising direction: treating synchrony as a tokenized temporal structure that can be learned by standard autoregressive LLMs. Even if some claims about deployment readiness and full-duplex interaction are somewhat ahead of the evidence, the paper represents a meaningful step toward more natural spoken agents.

For improvement, I would strongly encourage the authors to add or clarify the following. First, include a more realistic causal interaction evaluation with human speech on one side, even if offline, and report latency-conditioned performance there. Second, provide stronger baselines, ideally including a streaming cascaded system. Third, expand the ablation section to isolate the contributions of synchronization tokens, deduplication, interpolation, and user-chunk prediction. Fourth, better specify the automatic semantic evaluation pipeline and discuss its limitations. Fifth, add more qualitative analysis of overlaps and backchannels, including examples of successful and failed interactions. Sixth, if possible, report computational cost and real-time factor, since practical full-duplex deployment depends not only on chunk size but also on inference throughput.

In summary, this is a strong and timely paper with a genuinely novel framing of synchronous spoken dialogue modeling. Its main contribution is not just better numbers, but a useful modeling abstraction for bringing wall-clock timing into autoregressive LLMs. The semantic improvements are convincing, and the naturalness results are promising, though less definitive. My main reservations concern the realism of the interaction evaluation, the limited baseline set, and the need for deeper ablations and methodological clarity. Subject to those caveats, I view this as a valuable contribution that is likely to stimulate further work in full-duplex conversational AI.

My recommendation is moderately positive. The paper is above the bar in novelty and significance, with some weaknesses in evaluation rigor and completeness that should ideally be addressed in revision.
