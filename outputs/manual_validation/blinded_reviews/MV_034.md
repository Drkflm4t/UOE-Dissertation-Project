# Review ID: MV_034

**Validation Track:** `RQ2`

---

This paper proposes CoT-Influx, a black-box prompting module for math reasoning that aims to fit more useful few-shot chain-of-thought demonstrations into a fixed context window by jointly pruning at the example level and token level. The method uses a coarse-to-fine pruner trained with REINFORCE, supported by a GPT-4-generated/evolved math CoT dataset (MRD3). Empirically, the paper reports gains over standard few-shot CoT, retrieval baselines, and prompt compression baselines across LLaMA2 models and several math benchmarks. The central claim is appealing: for math reasoning, carefully selected and compressed CoT demonstrations can outperform both standard prompting and generic compression methods, without modifying the target LLM.

Overall, I find the paper interesting and potentially impactful, but not yet fully convincing in its current form. The main idea is intuitive and practically relevant, and the empirical results are promising. However, there are several methodological ambiguities, fairness issues in the comparisons, and reproducibility gaps that weaken the strength of the evidence. My assessment is that this is a good paper with a compelling direction, but it needs tighter experimental control and clearer exposition before the claims can be fully trusted.

The strongest aspect of the paper is the problem framing. The authors correctly identify that few-shot CoT prompting for math is constrained not only by retrieval quality but also by prompt length, and that generic prompt compression can damage the structural and numerical cues that matter for arithmetic reasoning. The coarse-to-fine decomposition into shot pruning followed by token pruning is sensible. It also makes intuitive sense that different target LLMs may prefer demonstrations of different difficulty levels, and the paper provides some evidence in that direction. The plug-and-play nature of the approach is another practical advantage, especially compared with methods that require modifying or fine-tuning the target LLM.

The empirical results are encouraging. On GSM8K, the reported gains over standard few-shot CoT and retrieval baselines are nontrivial, especially for LLaMA2-13B and 70B. The comparisons against LLMLingua and Selective Context are striking, and the qualitative examples support the claim that preserving prompt structure and numerical tokens is important. The ablations on shot-only versus token-only pruning are useful and suggest that both levels contribute. The appendix also contains several analyses that help motivate the design choices.

That said, I have substantial concerns about the experimental setup and the fairness of the comparisons. The biggest issue is that CoT-Influx is not just a pruning method; it is a pruning method plus a newly constructed GPT-4-generated/evolved demonstration pool, plus model-specific RL training, plus rule-based prompt reconstruction, plus appended manual 8-shot CoTs during training. Several baselines appear to use only retrieval or compression over the same candidate pool, but they do not receive analogous learned adaptation or reconstruction. As a result, it is difficult to isolate where the gains come from. Are the improvements due primarily to the pruning policy, to the quality/diversity of MRD3, to the model-specific training, or to the reconstruction heuristics? The paper partially addresses the dataset contribution in Table 6, but the decomposition is still incomplete.

A related concern is that the method seems less “black-box plug-and-play” than advertised. The pruner is trained separately for each target LLM, and Table 14 even lists different “pruner LLM base” settings and different evolution subsets for different models. This means the method is model-specific and requires nontrivial tuning. That is not necessarily a flaw, but it should be stated more clearly. The current framing suggests broad plug-and-play applicability, whereas the actual setup appears to require per-model RL training and hyperparameter selection.

The reward design and RL formulation also need more scrutiny. Equation (5) is underspecified and somewhat confusing. The token penalty term uses [t/T]^w, but the text says w<0 penalizes prompts that are too short and w>0 penalizes prompts that exceed the limit. This is not a standard or fully clear formulation, especially since t/T can be less than or greater than 1 and the paper later reports w as “(-1,1),” suggesting piecewise behavior that is not formally defined in the main text. This should be made precise. More importantly, REINFORCE over binary keep/drop decisions for many tokens is high variance, and the paper’s stabilization tricks are somewhat ad hoc. The use of EMA, repeated sampling, difficulty filtering, and appending manual 8-shot CoTs may all help, but they also complicate the interpretation of what the learned policy is actually doing.

The appended manual 8-shot CoTs during training are particularly concerning. Algorithm 1 shows that the final training input includes both the pruned demonstrations and x_few-shot, i.e., the manually designed 8-shot CoTs. If the reward is computed on this combined prompt, then the policy is optimized in the presence of a strong fixed scaffold. At inference, the algorithm also appears to include x_few-shot in the final prompt. If so, then the reported “#Input CoT shots” may not be directly comparable to baselines unless those baselines also include the same manual 8-shot scaffold in addition to their retrieved/compressed examples. This point is critical and currently unclear. If CoT-Influx always includes the manual 8-shot prompt while retrieval baselines do not, the comparisons are not fair. The paper needs to explicitly state, for every baseline, exactly what prompt components are included.

There is also ambiguity around the token budget and context usage. The paper claims “no additional inference overhead” because everything remains within the original context window, but CoT-Influx requires running BERT and the pruner before the target LLM. Table 8 later acknowledges additional latency and memory. So the claim should be softened: there is no increase in target-LLM context length, but there is additional preprocessing overhead. Similarly, the statement that CoT-Influx enables “double the context window size in tokens” is rhetorically strong but somewhat misleading, since the actual prompt fed to the LLM still fits within the original window; what doubles is the size of the candidate pool before pruning.

The evaluation protocol raises further questions. The paper reports exact match on several datasets, which is standard, but many of the gains are modest in absolute terms. Given the stochasticity of generation and the RL-trained selection policy, confidence intervals or multiple-run variance estimates are important, yet none are reported. This is especially relevant because some improvements are around 1–2 points. The paper also uses temperature/top-p settings in Table 14, but it is not fully clear whether the main GSM8K results are deterministic greedy decoding or sampled decoding. Since self-consistency is evaluated separately, I assume the main results are single-sample, but this should be explicit.

The comparisons to larger models in Table 4 are not very meaningful scientifically. Those numbers come from different papers, likely with different prompting setups, decoding strategies, and evaluation protocols. Claiming that LLaMA2-70B+CoT-Influx “surpasses GPT-3.5 and a wide range of larger LLMs” is too strong unless the comparison is controlled under the same prompt budget, same number of shots, same answer extraction, and same evaluation script. At best, these are rough contextual comparisons, not definitive head-to-head results.

The dataset construction also deserves more critical discussion. MRD3 is built using GPT-4-generated CoTs and GPT-4-based difficulty scoring, with additional GPT-4 evolution. This may produce a demonstration distribution that is stylistically aligned with GPT-4 and potentially favorable for certain prompting behaviors. It also raises contamination-style concerns, not necessarily benchmark leakage, but at least distribution shaping. Since GSM8K training data is part of the source pool and the evaluation includes GSM8K test, the setup is not inherently invalid, but the paper should be more careful in explaining train/test separation and whether any evolved examples could become near paraphrases of evaluation items. The current description is too high level to rule this out confidently.

Another methodological concern is the use of rule-based prompt reconstruction. This is a nontrivial component: it repairs missing structural tokens such as “Q:”, “A:”, “Let’s think step by step”, and “The answer is”. In effect, the system is not purely learning which tokens to keep; it is learning under a post-processing regime that can restore important syntax. This may be entirely reasonable, but then the contribution is a hybrid learned-plus-rule-based compressor, and baselines should ideally receive analogous structural repair if the goal is to compare compression quality fairly. Otherwise, some of the large gap to LLMLingua or Selective Context may reflect the absence of reconstruction rather than fundamentally worse token salience estimation.

The paper’s exposition also has several technical inconsistencies and writing issues that make it harder to evaluate. Equation (6) is written in a compressed way and omits expectation notation and variance reduction details. The state definitions are dimensionally odd: the text says sentence-level embeddings are extracted, but then H_s_shot is k x N x D_BERT with N=512 token positions, which sounds token-level rather than sentence-level. The notation around s_shot and s_token is also inconsistent. Algorithm 1 says “Training the two-stage pruner (1 epoch)” while Table 14 says 3 epochs. There are many grammatical errors and typos throughout. None of these are fatal individually, but together they reduce confidence in the precision of the implementation description.

I also think the paper could do more to connect to prior work on demonstration selection and prompt optimization. The claim that retrieval methods are “model-agnostic” is only partially true; there are supervised retrievers and RL-based selectors that can be model-aware. The novelty here seems to be the joint shot/token pruning under a math-specific reward, rather than the general idea of learned demonstration selection. Positioning the contribution more narrowly would make the paper stronger.

There are several experiments that would substantially strengthen the paper. First, a fully controlled ablation where all methods use the same candidate pool, same total token budget, same structural reconstruction policy, and same fixed manual 8-shot scaffold or no scaffold at all. Second, an ablation removing the manual 8-shot CoTs from both training and inference, to show how much the method depends on them. Third, a comparison against stronger learned retrieval baselines, including model-aware or RL-based demonstration selection, not just TopK/BM25/random. Fourth, variance across multiple seeds/runs. Fifth, transfer experiments: train the pruner on one model and test on another, or train on GSM8K-style data and test on different math domains, to support the plug-and-play/generalization claims. Sixth, a comparison to a simpler heuristic that first retrieves many examples and then truncates or compresses only rationale text while preserving structure, to test whether RL is really necessary.

I would also like to see more analysis of what is being pruned. The qualitative examples are helpful, but a quantitative breakdown would be better: what fraction of question tokens, rationale tokens, answer tokens, numbers, operators, discourse markers, and formatting tokens are retained? How often does the shot pruner select examples of different difficulty levels for different target models? Does the token pruner mostly remove stopwords, or does it also shorten reasoning chains in systematic ways? Such analysis would make the method more interpretable and support the paper’s claims about math-specific token importance.

Despite these concerns, I do think the paper has merit. The core intuition is strong, the task is important, and the empirical trend that more carefully curated compressed CoTs can help math reasoning is believable and useful. The paper is also notable for highlighting a failure mode of generic prompt compression on mathematical demonstrations. If the authors can tighten the methodology and clarify the fairness of the comparisons, this could become a solid contribution.

My recommendation is borderline positive to borderline negative depending on the conference bar for empirical rigor. At present, I lean slightly negative because too many of the headline claims depend on components whose contributions are not cleanly disentangled, and because the fairness of the baseline comparisons is not sufficiently established. But I view this as a promising paper rather than a flawed idea.

The main strengths are the practical importance of the problem, the intuitive and plausible coarse-to-fine pruning design, the promising empirical gains across several models and datasets, and the useful observation that generic compression methods can break math CoT prompts by removing structural and numerical cues.

The main weaknesses are the confounding of multiple contributions, unclear fairness in prompt composition versus baselines, insufficiently specified RL/reward details, lack of variance reporting, reliance on GPT-4-generated/evolved data and rule-based reconstruction, and several exposition inconsistencies.

To improve the paper, I strongly encourage the authors to clarify exactly what prompt each baseline receives; isolate the effects of MRD3, RL pruning, reconstruction, and manual 8-shot scaffolding; report variance and significance; compare against stronger learned retrieval/selection baselines; and clean up the technical presentation. If those issues are addressed, the work would be much more convincing.
