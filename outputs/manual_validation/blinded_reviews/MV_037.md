# Review ID: MV_037

**Validation Track:** `RQ2`

---

This paper presents X-Topic, a multilingual multi-label topic classification dataset for X/Twitter covering English, Spanish, Japanese, and Greek, with 19 topic labels and 4,000 annotated tweets in total. The paper also benchmarks a range of fine-tuned multilingual encoders and prompted LLMs under zero-shot, few-shot, monolingual, cross-lingual, and multilingual settings. Overall, the paper addresses a relevant problem, namely the lack of multilingual labeled social media resources for topic classification, and it provides a useful empirical comparison across model families and training regimes. The work is timely and potentially valuable to the community, especially because multilingual social media benchmarks remain scarce. At the same time, there are several methodological and presentation issues that limit the strength of the claims and should be addressed for the paper to reach its full impact.

The main strengths are clear. First, the paper tackles an under-resourced but practically important task: multilingual topic classification on short, noisy, socially situated text. The choice to move beyond English and include typologically diverse languages is meaningful, and the inclusion of Greek is particularly welcome given its relative underrepresentation in NLP benchmarks. Second, the paper does more than release a dataset: it provides a broad experimental study spanning cross-lingual transfer, multilingual training, and prompted LLM baselines. This makes the paper more useful than a pure resource paper. Third, the comparison between generic multilingual encoders and social-media-adapted encoders is informative and supports an intuitively important conclusion: domain adaptation matters substantially for social media topic classification. Fourth, the dataset appears to be openly released, along with trained models, which improves reproducibility and practical utility.

That said, my overall enthusiasm is moderated by concerns about dataset construction, annotation quality, evaluation design, and the interpretation of results. The paper’s central contribution is the dataset, so the standards for documenting collection and annotation should be especially high. In its current form, several important details are either missing, weakly justified, or potentially problematic.

A first major concern is representativeness and sampling bias. The collection process starts from 50 tweets every two hours per language, without keyword filtering, which is presented as closer to the “real distribution” of X content. However, the final annotated subset is not sampled uniformly from this pool; instead, it is weighted by retweet count and author follower count under the assumption that more popular content is higher quality. This is a strong and contestable assumption. It likely biases the dataset toward more visible users, more mainstream topics, more institutional or celebrity discourse, and possibly language-specific popularity dynamics. It may also distort the natural topic distribution in ways that are not carefully quantified. Since one of the paper’s selling points is realism, this popularity-weighted sampling deserves much deeper analysis. At minimum, the paper should compare the topic distribution and lexical properties of the weighted sample against an unweighted sample from the same preprocessed pool, or at least discuss the likely consequences more explicitly. As written, the dataset is not simply “randomly extracted tweets” and the paper occasionally overstates that point.

A second concern is the small scale of the final dataset relative to the breadth of the task. The paper collects roughly 220k tweets per language but annotates only 1,000 per language. For 19 labels in a multi-label setting with substantial imbalance, this is quite limited. The resulting train split per fold is only 720 examples per language, which makes it difficult to disentangle model quality from data scarcity. This is especially important because one of the paper’s headline findings is that cross-lingual training on the much larger English TweetTopic dataset outperforms monolingual training on the target language. That result is interesting, but it is also unsurprising given the large training size mismatch. The paper acknowledges this, but still frames the result as evidence of strong cross-lingual capability. I think that interpretation is too strong. The experiments confound transfer ability with training set size, domain overlap, and temporal effects. A more convincing analysis would control for training size, for example by subsampling TweetTopic to match the target-language training size, or by plotting performance as a function of number of training examples in each regime.

A third concern is annotation quality and label aggregation. The reported Krippendorff alpha values around 0.21–0.26 are low, even if not unusual for subjective multi-label social media tasks. The paper argues that these are comparable to prior datasets, which is fair, but the aggregation rule is unusually permissive: a label is assigned if at least two of five annotators selected it. This can admit labels supported by only a minority of annotators, potentially increasing recall at the expense of precision and consistency. The authors justify this by citing prior multi-label work and by aiming for realism, but the consequences are not sufficiently analyzed. For a dataset paper, I would expect at least an ablation comparing alternative aggregation schemes, such as 2-of-5 versus 3-of-5, or soft labels versus hard labels. Since the authors plan to release all annotations, this seems feasible and would substantially strengthen the resource. As it stands, the benchmark may partly reward models that mimic annotation noise or broad topical associations rather than crisp topic assignment.

Relatedly, the agreement metrics are not fully satisfying for a multi-label task. Percent agreement is high but not very informative under sparse labels and many negatives. “Overlap” is more useful, but still coarse. It would help to report per-label agreement, prevalence, and perhaps label cardinality distributions by language. Some labels may be much more ambiguous than others, and this matters for interpreting both dataset quality and model performance. The paper later notes that categories such as Arts & Culture and Other Hobbies are difficult, but this should be connected back to annotation ambiguity more systematically.

A fourth concern is the preprocessing and filtering pipeline. Several steps are described only vaguely: “incomplete sentences,” “abusing words,” and normalization with lemmatization before deduplication. These choices can materially affect the linguistic character of the dataset, especially for social media where fragments, slang, code-switching, and non-canonical orthography are common. Removing URLs, tweets with more than four emojis or mentions, and certain short or incomplete tweets may improve cleanliness, but it also risks sanitizing away exactly the phenomena that make social media challenging. The paper should better justify these filters and quantify how much content they remove by language and by topic. The appendix may contain some counts, but the main paper should summarize the impact more clearly because these decisions shape the benchmark’s validity.

The experimental section is broad, but there are some issues in evaluation design and fairness of comparison. The fine-tuned models are evaluated with 5-fold cross-validation, which is good, but the prompted LLM baselines are less clearly controlled. It is not obvious whether prompting was tuned on validation data, whether outputs were normalized deterministically, how invalid outputs were handled, whether multiple prompt variants were tried, or whether API nondeterminism was controlled. Since the paper compares prompted proprietary models against fine-tuned encoders and draws conclusions about relative strengths, these details matter. In particular, the claim that LLMs underperform “more efficient but fully-trained multilingual masked language models” is directionally supported by the reported averages, but the comparison is not entirely apples-to-apples. The fine-tuned models benefit from supervised training on the benchmark labels, while the LLMs are restricted to zero/few-shot prompting with only five examples. That is a useful practical comparison, but the conclusion should be phrased more carefully.

The choice of baselines is reasonable but incomplete. Since the paper motivates itself partly against unsupervised topic modeling, it would be useful to include at least one unsupervised or weakly supervised topic baseline, even if only as a qualitative reference. More importantly, for a multi-label classification benchmark, simple discriminative baselines such as linear models over multilingual sentence embeddings or TF-IDF plus one-vs-rest classifiers could provide useful calibration. If such simple baselines perform competitively in some settings, that would be important to know; if they do not, that would strengthen the case for the proposed benchmark’s difficulty.

The statistical testing is also underexplained. Table 3 marks some differences as statistically significant relative to the second-best score, but the paper does not clearly state the test used, whether it is paired across folds, whether assumptions hold, or whether multiple comparisons were corrected. Given the large number of models, languages, and settings, significance claims should be made cautiously and documented precisely.

I also found some of the analysis claims overstated relative to the evidence. For example, the paper says multilingual models improve by around 17 macro-F1 points over monolingual ones on average. This is a striking number, but again it is largely expected because multilingual training simply uses much more labeled data. The more interesting question is whether multilinguality helps beyond data volume and domain overlap. Similarly, the cross-lingual results are presented as evidence that models may be used in languages without training data, but the benchmark only evaluates four languages, all of which are represented in the pretraining corpora of the tested models, and one of the strongest transfer sources is a large English in-domain dataset. This is promising evidence, but not enough to support broad generalization claims.

The error analysis is a useful start but remains fairly shallow. Reporting topics with highest false negative rates is informative, yet it would be more insightful to analyze confusion patterns between specific labels, especially semantically adjacent ones such as Business vs Science & Technology, Arts & Culture vs Other Hobbies, or Diaries & Daily Life vs Relationships. Since this is a multi-label task, co-occurrence-aware analysis would also be valuable: are models missing secondary labels more often than primary ones? Are some languages more prone to underprediction of multi-label outputs? The paper hints at precision-recall tradeoffs for LLMs versus fine-tuned encoders, which is interesting, but this could be developed much further.

There are also some concerns around reproducibility and clarity of presentation. The paper contains many grammatical errors, typos, and formatting issues, including corrupted Greek text in Table 1, inconsistent naming of models and settings, and several malformed sentences. These do not invalidate the work, but they noticeably reduce readability and polish. Some tables are difficult to parse in the current form, especially Table 3. The paper would benefit from careful proofreading and clearer table design. In addition, some important details appear relegated to appendices that are not fully visible here, such as prompts, hyperparameter ranges, and preprocessing counts. For a benchmark paper, the main text should still contain enough detail to assess methodological soundness without relying heavily on appendices.

The ethics section is appreciated, but there are a few points that deserve more scrutiny. Compensation at an average of £8/hour may or may not be fair depending on local minimum wage and annotator location; the paper should avoid asserting fairness without context. More importantly, the dataset contains user-generated content from X, and while mentions are masked, the text itself may still be searchable and potentially identifiable. The paper should discuss whether tweet redistribution complies with platform terms and whether only tweet IDs or full text are released. The stated CC BY-NC 4.0 license may not be compatible with redistribution constraints for social media text, depending on how the data are shared. This is an important practical issue for dataset release.

In terms of novelty, the paper’s main novelty lies in assembling a multilingual, multi-label social media topic dataset and benchmarking modern multilingual models on it. This is a worthwhile contribution, though not a highly conceptual one. The empirical findings themselves are mostly sensible rather than surprising: in-domain pretrained encoders beat generic ones; more labeled data helps; multilingual training helps low-resource settings; prompted LLMs are competitive but not dominant. The value therefore depends heavily on the dataset quality and benchmark rigor. That is why the concerns above matter.

My recommendation would depend on venue standards. For a resource- or application-oriented venue, I could see this paper being accepted after revision because the dataset fills a real gap and the experiments are broad enough to make the release useful. For a top-tier ML conference with high expectations on methodological rigor, I would be more hesitant in its current form. The dataset is promising, but the paper needs stronger validation of annotation decisions, better control of confounds in the transfer experiments, and clearer documentation of sampling bias and evaluation methodology.

The most important improvements I would suggest are the following. First, add a deeper dataset analysis focused on sampling bias: compare weighted versus unweighted sampling, report user/account-type statistics if possible, and discuss how popularity weighting affects topic prevalence. Second, analyze annotation aggregation more rigorously by comparing at least one stricter threshold and, ideally, by releasing soft-label variants. Third, control for training size in the cross-lingual and multilingual comparisons so that claims about transfer are not conflated with data volume. Fourth, document the LLM prompting and output parsing pipeline in enough detail to make the comparison reproducible and fair. Fifth, expand the error analysis to include label confusions and multi-label-specific failure modes. Sixth, improve the writing and formatting substantially.

In summary, this is a useful and relevant dataset paper with practical value and a reasonably comprehensive benchmark suite. Its strongest contribution is the release of multilingual labeled social media data for topic classification, an area where resources are indeed scarce. However, the current manuscript overclaims in some places and underdocuments several important methodological choices. With stronger analysis of annotation and sampling, better-controlled comparisons, and improved presentation, the paper could become a solid contribution. As submitted, I view it as promising but not yet fully convincing at the level of rigor expected for a top-tier conference.
