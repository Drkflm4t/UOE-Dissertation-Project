# Review ID: MV_029

**Validation Track:** `RQ2`

---

This paper tackles an important and underexplored problem: translating natural language into first-order logic, with an emphasis on practical adaptation of open models. The paper’s central claims are that a 7B LLaMA model fine-tuned on a GPT-4-generated silver dataset can outperform GPT-3.5 on direct NL-to-FOL translation, and that a correction pipeline using supervised perturbation training plus RLHF can bring GPT-3.5 outputs close to GPT-4 quality at much lower cost. The work is ambitious and timely, and it combines dataset creation, model adaptation, and a task-specific reward design. Overall, I find the paper interesting and potentially impactful, but there are also several methodological and evaluation issues that currently limit how strongly I would trust the conclusions.

The strongest aspect of the paper is that it addresses a real bottleneck in neuro-symbolic NLP: the lack of scalable, sentence-level NL-FOL supervision. The proposed Malls dataset is a meaningful contribution in itself. The authors are transparent that it is a silver dataset generated by GPT-4 rather than manually curated gold annotations, and they make a reasonable case that such a resource is useful for training local models when cost, privacy, or deployment constraints make GPT-4 impractical. The prompting pipeline for diversity control is thoughtful, especially the use of negative n-gram constraints and explicit prompting for operator diversity and predicate decomposition. The paper also does a good job motivating why direct autoregressive fine-tuning may be insufficient and why correction, especially iterative correction, is a natural formulation for this task.

A second notable strength is the attempt to define a task-aware evaluation and reward signal. Exact string match is clearly inappropriate for logic translation, and the paper correctly emphasizes that logically equivalent formulas may differ substantially at the surface level. The proposed logical equivalence score is therefore directionally well motivated. Likewise, the correction framing is sensible: if a stronger model already captures much of the structure, a smaller specialized model may indeed be able to repair systematic errors efficiently. This is a practically appealing idea.

That said, the paper’s main empirical claims rest on several assumptions that are not yet validated rigorously enough. The biggest concern is data quality and possible benchmark contamination. Malls is generated by GPT-4 using prompts seeded initially with FOLIO examples and later with GPT-4-generated examples. The authors state that they “checked” that no FOLIO examples or close variations leaked into Malls, but this is not described in enough detail to be convincing. Since the evaluation benchmarks are small, especially FOLIO, even modest semantic overlap or templatic similarity could materially inflate results. More broadly, because both the training data and one of the strongest baselines come from the same model family, it is difficult to disentangle whether LogicLLaMA is learning robust NL-FOL translation or distilling GPT-4’s annotation style and predicate naming conventions in a way that happens to align with the test sets.

Relatedly, the paper acknowledges that Malls lacks rigorous NL-FOL alignment verification beyond syntax checking and a simplistic term-frequency heuristic. This is a serious limitation. In semantic parsing to logic, syntactic validity is far from sufficient; subtle scope, quantifier, negation, and implication errors are exactly what matter. If the silver labels contain systematic semantic mistakes, the model may learn them. The fact that evaluation on FOLIO and LogicNLI is reasonably strong is encouraging, but it does not substitute for a direct audit of Malls quality. A manual analysis of a random sample, with error categories and estimated annotation precision, is essential for supporting the dataset contribution.

The evaluation methodology also raises concerns. The logical equivalence metric appears to reduce formulas to truth tables over extracted literals treated as Boolean variables, with greedy binding between literals and dummy padding when the predicate sets differ. This is not really first-order logical equivalence in the standard sense; it is closer to propositionalized structural similarity under heuristic alignment. That may still be useful as a practical metric, but the paper overstates it by calling it logical equivalence. In particular, quantifiers, variable binding structure, arity, and predicate semantics are not faithfully captured by truth-table comparison over literals alone. Two formulas can differ critically in quantification while looking similar under such a reduction. Conversely, formulas with renamed predicates or different decomposition choices may be penalized or rewarded in unintuitive ways. Since this metric is also used as the RL reward, any mismatch between the metric and true semantic correctness directly affects training. The paper needs a much more careful discussion of what the metric does and does not measure, along with validation against human judgments or theorem-prover-based equivalence checks on a representative subset.

The use of BLEU as part of the reward is also questionable. I understand the motivation of encouraging correct predicate and entity naming, but BLEU is a brittle surface metric and may bias the model toward lexical imitation of the silver labels rather than semantically faithful translation. This is especially problematic when the paper’s own examples emphasize that multiple logically equivalent decompositions are possible. A stronger alternative would be to separate semantic correctness from canonicalization, or to use a normalization procedure before comparison. At minimum, the paper should include an ablation on the reward mixing coefficient and show whether BLEU materially helps beyond the heuristic equivalence score.

The RLHF component is interesting but under-justified experimentally. The paper presents the SFT+RLHF framework as a novel contribution, yet it is not clear how much of the gain comes from synthetic perturbation SFT, how much from iterative correction at inference time, and how much from PPO fine-tuning. The current presentation suggests that RLHF improves over naive correction, but I did not see sufficiently detailed ablations isolating T1 vs T2 vs T3 vs T4 under matched settings. For a paper whose novelty centers on the training framework, this is a major omission. In particular, I would want to see: direct translation trained on Malls only; naive correction trained on GPT-3.5 outputs; CoT correction with synthetic perturbation SFT only; and then the full RLHF model. Without this decomposition, it is hard to assess whether PPO is necessary or whether most gains come from simply exposing the model to perturbation-repair trajectories.

There is also a conceptual issue with calling the method RLHF. No human feedback appears to be used in the RL stage; the reward is computed automatically from the reference FOL using a verifier-like metric. This is closer to reinforcement learning from automated feedback than RLHF in the usual sense. That does not diminish the technical idea, but the terminology is misleading and should be corrected.

The experimental scope is somewhat narrow. The paper evaluates on FOLIO and LogicNLI, which are reasonable choices, but LogicNLI is acknowledged to be templatic and nearly saturated by most methods. Thus, the main evidence effectively comes from FOLIO, a 2K-example benchmark. Given the central claim of broad NL-FOL translation capability, I would like to see stronger generalization tests: out-of-domain examples, harder compositional splits, or at least a manually curated challenge set emphasizing quantifier scope, negation, disjunction, biconditionals, and exclusive-or. The dataset statistics show operator diversity in Malls, but there is no corresponding breakdown of model performance by operator or construction type. Such an analysis would be very informative.

Another weakness is the lack of comparison to non-LLM or prior neural semantic parsing baselines. The paper compares mainly against GPT-3.5 and GPT-4, which is useful from a practical standpoint, but insufficient scientifically. Since the task is NL-to-FOL translation, prior semantic parsing systems or recent neural parsers should be included where feasible, even if adapted imperfectly. Otherwise, it is difficult to place the contribution relative to the broader literature rather than only relative to proprietary general-purpose LLMs.

The cost-efficiency claim is plausible but not fully substantiated. The paper cites API prices and argues that GPT-3.5 plus LogicLLaMA approaches GPT-4 at lower cost, but a fair accounting should include the cost of generating Malls with GPT-4, generating GPT-3.5 outputs for correction training, and RL fine-tuning. If the intended claim is inference-time cost only, that should be stated clearly. If the claim is end-to-end system cost, then training-time expenses matter.

I also found some aspects of the presentation imprecise. The paper repeatedly refers to “ground truth” FOL in Malls despite acknowledging that these are silver labels from GPT-4. This is understandable for notation, but it risks overstating confidence in the labels. The claim that LogicLLaMA is “the first specialised LM for the NL-FOL translation task” also seems too strong without a more careful survey of prior task-specific neural parsers. The paper would benefit from more restrained wording throughout.

Despite these concerns, I think the paper has real merit. The idea of distilling or correcting proprietary LLM outputs into a local symbolic translator is compelling. The dataset creation pipeline is practical and likely useful to others. The correction formulation is more interesting than straightforward fine-tuning, and the reported gains on FOLIO suggest that the approach is not merely trivial. If the authors can shore up the evaluation and provide stronger evidence about data quality and the necessity of each training component, this could become a solid contribution.

For improvement, my main recommendations are as follows. First, add a manual audit of Malls, ideally with at least a few hundred randomly sampled examples, reporting syntax validity, NL-FOL semantic alignment, and common error types. Second, provide a rigorous leakage/overlap analysis between Malls and FOLIO/LogicNLI, including lexical and semantic similarity statistics. Third, expand the ablation study to isolate the contributions of direct training, naive correction, perturbation-based CoT SFT, iterative inference, and PPO fine-tuning. Fourth, validate the proposed LE metric against either theorem-prover judgments or expert human ratings on a subset, and clarify that it is a heuristic approximation rather than full first-order equivalence. Fifth, include performance breakdowns by logical operator and construction type. Sixth, revise the terminology around RLHF and moderate claims about “ground truth” and “GPT-4-level” performance.

My overall assessment is cautiously positive on the idea and practical contribution, but mixed on the current level of empirical rigor. I would lean borderline accept for a venue that values practical systems and dataset contributions, but borderline reject for a top-tier conference unless the concerns above are addressed convincingly. The paper is promising, but the current evidence does not yet fully support its strongest claims.
