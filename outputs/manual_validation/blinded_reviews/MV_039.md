# Review ID: MV_039

**Validation Track:** `RQ2`

---

This paper introduces X-Topic, a multilingual multi-label topic classification dataset for X/Twitter covering English, Spanish, Japanese, and Greek, with 1,000 annotated tweets per language and a 19-topic taxonomy inherited from prior English-only work. The paper also benchmarks a range of fine-tuned multilingual encoders and prompted LLMs under zero-shot, few-shot, monolingual, cross-lingual, and multilingual settings. Overall, I find the paper timely and potentially useful to the community. Multilingual social media topic classification is under-resourced, and a carefully curated benchmark in this space could have meaningful impact. The experimental section is broad and the paper is generally easy to follow. At the same time, I have several concerns about dataset construction choices, evaluation design, and some of the claims drawn from the results. My overall assessment is moderately positive on the resource contribution, but weaker on methodological rigor and on the strength of some conclusions.

The main strengths are clear. First, the paper addresses a real gap: multilingual labeled social media topic datasets are indeed scarce, especially with a reasonably broad taxonomy and multi-label annotations. Second, the inclusion of typologically diverse languages is valuable, particularly Japanese and Greek, which are less commonly represented together in multilingual benchmarks. Third, the paper does more than release data; it provides a fairly extensive empirical study across multiple transfer settings, which helps position the benchmark and gives practitioners useful baselines. Fourth, the comparison between generic multilingual encoders, social-media-specialized encoders, and prompted LLMs is interesting and practically relevant. Finally, the authors appear to have made the dataset and models publicly available, which increases reproducibility and utility.

That said, the dataset itself is quite small for the scope of the claims. With only 1,000 tweets per language and 19 labels in a multi-label setting, many labels must be very sparse, and this sparsity is visible in the instability of some results. The paper acknowledges imbalance, but I do not think it fully grapples with the implications for benchmark reliability. A 5-fold CV with 720/80/200 train/val/test per fold means each monolingual training split is extremely small for a 19-label multi-label problem. This makes it difficult to disentangle whether observed gains are due to multilingual transfer, larger effective sample size, temporal/domain alignment, or simply variance from low-resource fine-tuning. The paper often interprets differences as substantive modeling insights when some may primarily reflect data scarcity.

A central concern is the sampling and preprocessing pipeline, which may substantially distort the target distribution while the paper simultaneously claims realism. The authors state that they avoid keyword filtering to obtain a distribution “closer to the real distribution of content shared in X,” but then remove tweets with URLs, tweets with more than four emojis or mentions, incomplete sentences, “abusing words,” and near-duplicates after normalization and lemmatization, and finally sample with weights based on retweet count and follower count. These choices likely bias the dataset toward cleaner, more mainstream, more popular, and less interaction-heavy content. That may be defensible for annotation quality, but it weakens the claim that the benchmark reflects realistic platform distributions. In particular, removing URLs is consequential because many topical tweets, especially news, politics, business, and technology, contain links. Weighting by popularity may also overrepresent institutional or celebrity discourse relative to ordinary users. I would strongly encourage the authors to frame the dataset as a curated benchmark rather than a realistic sample, unless they provide evidence that these filters preserve topical distributions.

Relatedly, several preprocessing decisions need more justification and transparency. “Abusing words” is vague and potentially problematic; it is unclear whether this refers to profanity, slurs, repeated characters, spam-like text, or something else. Such filtering can introduce sociolinguistic bias and disproportionately affect certain communities or topics. The near-duplicate filtering procedure also deserves more detail, especially because lemmatization quality differs substantially across the four languages, and because aggressive normalization can collapse distinct tweets. Since the paper references appendix tables for dropped counts, it would still benefit from a concise summary in the main text of how much data each filter removes and whether topic or language distributions shift after filtering.

The annotation protocol is reasonable in broad outline, but there are unresolved issues. Using five annotators per tweet is good, and releasing all annotations would be valuable. However, the label aggregation rule of assigning a topic if at least two annotators selected it is permissive and can inflate label cardinality and noise. The authors justify this by citing prior multi-label work and a desire for realism, but the consequences should be analyzed more carefully. For example, how often do labels arise from exactly two annotators versus three or more? What is model performance if one uses stricter aggregation, such as 3/5 agreement? Since the paper’s conclusions partly depend on benchmark difficulty, it would be important to know how much of that difficulty comes from genuine ambiguity versus label noise induced by aggregation. The reported average of 1.8 topics per tweet is higher than the original TweetTopic’s 1.6, which may partly reflect this rule.

The agreement analysis is somewhat underdeveloped. Krippendorff’s alpha values around 0.21–0.26 are low, even if not unusual for subjective multi-label social media tasks. The paper argues these are comparable to emotion datasets, but topic classification is not necessarily equally subjective, and the comparison does not fully resolve concerns. Percent agreement values around 0.87–0.89 seem high, but in multi-label settings with many absent labels, such numbers can be misleading. The “overlap” metric is intuitive but nonstandard. I would have liked per-label agreement statistics, or at least some discussion of which topics are most ambiguous for annotators. That would also help interpret downstream model errors. As written, the paper acknowledges lower agreement than TweetTopic but then moves on too quickly.

The experimental coverage is broad, but some aspects of the setup make interpretation difficult. The cross-lingual setting trains on the full English TweetTopic dataset of 11,267 examples and tests on non-English X-Topic, while the monolingual target setting trains on only 720 examples per fold. The paper then emphasizes that cross-lingual models outperform monolingual ones and suggests this is encouraging for unseen languages. But this comparison is confounded by training set size by more than an order of magnitude, and also by possible differences in label distribution and annotation style between TweetTopic and X-Topic. The authors do note the size effect, but the discussion still overstates the cross-lingual conclusion. A fairer comparison would subsample TweetTopic to match target-language training size, or plot performance as a function of training size for both settings. Without that, it is hard to know whether the result demonstrates cross-lingual transfer or simply more supervision.

Similarly, the multilingual versus monolingual comparison is also largely a comparison of more data versus less data. That is not uninteresting, but the paper sometimes frames it as evidence of multilingual learning benefits per se. To support that claim, one would want controlled experiments with matched total training size, for example comparing 720 target-language examples against 720 mixed-language examples, or adding languages incrementally while holding total examples fixed. The current results do show that pooling multilingual data helps in practice, but they do not isolate why.

The model comparison section contains an apparent inconsistency. The text states that “models trained on X data consistently underperform the generic XLMR models,” but the table does not support such a blanket statement. In fact, xlmt_large often outperforms xlmr_large, and bernice is competitive in some settings. The paragraph later says xlmt_large surpasses xlmr_large by an average of 3 macro-F1 points, which contradicts the opening sentence. I suspect the intended claim is that social-media-specialized models do not uniformly dominate generic models, especially in low-resource settings, but the wording should be corrected and the analysis tightened.

The zero-shot and few-shot LLM evaluation is interesting but not fully rigorous. Prompt-based evaluation is highly sensitive to prompt wording, output parsing, label verbalization, and decoding settings. The paper says prompts are similar to those used for BLOOMz and MT0 training and refers to an appendix, but the main text does not discuss whether prompts were translated or language-specific, whether label names were localized, how invalid outputs were handled, whether temperature was fixed at zero, or whether multiple prompt variants were tried. Since the paper draws conclusions about LLM underperformance relative to fine-tuned encoders, stronger prompt methodology would be important. In particular, comparing proprietary GPT-4o to open fine-tuned encoders is useful pragmatically, but the fairness of the comparison depends heavily on prompt optimization effort. Also, because API models evolve over time, reproducibility is limited unless exact model versions and inference settings are documented.

The statistical significance reporting is also too thin. Table 3 marks some differences as statistically significant relative to the second-best score, but the paper does not explain in the main text what test was used, over what units, and whether multiple comparisons were corrected. With 5-fold CV and many models/settings/languages, significance claims should be handled carefully. Reporting mean and standard deviation across folds for each score would already improve interpretability substantially.

I also think the evaluation could be stronger in several ways. Macro-F1 and micro-F1 are appropriate, but for multi-label classification I would also expect exact match, subset accuracy, Hamming loss, label ranking metrics, or at least macro precision/recall consistently for all models and settings. Since thresholding strongly affects F1 in multi-label tasks, the paper should specify how thresholds were chosen for fine-tuned models and whether they were tuned per label on validation data. This is especially important because some of the observed precision-recall tradeoffs may simply reflect threshold choices rather than model capability. For prompted LLMs, the equivalent issue is output calibration and label count bias; the paper partially discusses average predicted labels per tweet, which is good, but more systematic calibration analysis would help.

The error analysis is a useful start but remains fairly shallow. Identifying topics with high false negative rates is informative, yet it would be more compelling to analyze confusion patterns between specific labels, especially semantically adjacent ones such as Arts & Culture versus Other Hobbies, or Business & Entrepreneurs versus Science & Technology. Since the dataset is multilingual, it would also be valuable to examine whether certain topics are culturally or linguistically harder because of lexical ambiguity, code-switching, named entities, or annotation guideline mismatch. The paper hints at such issues but does not investigate them deeply.

There are also some concerns about the benchmark’s long-term utility and comparability. The taxonomy is inherited from prior work and said to be designed by social media experts, but the paper does not revisit whether the 19 topics transfer equally well across languages and cultures. Some categories, especially “Other Hobbies,” seem residual and poorly defined, which likely contributes to low performance and low agreement. A multilingual benchmark would benefit from evidence that the taxonomy is semantically coherent and culturally portable. Even a small qualitative study of problematic categories across languages would strengthen the resource contribution.

On ethics and licensing, the paper is generally responsible, but there are a few caveats. Masking non-verified user mentions is a limited privacy measure; tweet text itself can often be searchable and re-identifiable, especially for popular tweets, and weighting by popularity may exacerbate this. The authors should discuss whether they release raw text, tweet IDs, or processed text, and how this aligns with platform terms and privacy norms. Also, the stated annotator compensation of £8/hour may or may not meet fair-pay expectations depending on local minimum wage standards and unpaid overhead; more precise reporting would be preferable.

In terms of writing quality, the paper is mostly clear, but there are a number of grammatical issues and typos that should be fixed in revision. Examples include “surprasses,” “bee seen,” “tha arise,” “it’s predecessor,” and some awkward or contradictory phrasing in the analysis section. Table formatting in the provided version is also difficult to parse, though that may be an artifact of extraction. The Greek example in Table 1 appears corrupted in rendering, which should be corrected because it undermines confidence in multilingual presentation quality.

My recommendation would depend on venue emphasis. If the venue values dataset/resource papers and practical multilingual benchmarks, I think this paper is a plausible accept, but not a strong one. The resource is useful and the baseline suite is broad. However, for a top-tier ML conference with high expectations on methodological rigor, I find the empirical claims somewhat overstated relative to the evidence, and the dataset design choices need more careful justification. I would lean borderline accept for a resource-oriented track, and borderline reject for a main research track unless the authors substantially strengthen the analysis.

The most important improvements I would request are the following. First, provide a clearer and more critical characterization of the dataset as curated rather than “realistic,” and quantify the effect of each preprocessing and sampling step on language/topic distributions. Second, analyze annotation aggregation sensitivity, especially comparing 2-of-5 versus 3-of-5 label assignment and reporting how many labels come from minimal agreement. Third, make the cross-lingual and multilingual comparisons fairer by controlling for training set size, or at least by adding learning curves and matched-size ablations. Fourth, document thresholding, prompt details, decoding settings, and significance testing much more carefully. Fifth, deepen the error analysis with per-label agreement, confusion patterns, and multilingual qualitative examples. Sixth, revisit and soften claims about social-media-specific pretraining and cross-lingual superiority where the evidence is mixed or confounded.

In summary, this is a useful benchmark paper with a meaningful contribution in multilingual social media topic classification, but it currently falls short of the strongest standard of empirical rigor. The dataset release and baseline coverage are the main reasons to view it positively; the main weaknesses are confounded comparisons, limited annotation analysis, and insufficient scrutiny of sampling bias and label noise. With a stronger revision along the lines above, the paper could become a solid and impactful resource.
