# Review ID: MV_027

**Validation Track:** `RQ1`

---

This paper tackles natural-language-to-first-order-logic translation and proposes three main contributions: a 34K GPT-4-generated “silver” dataset of NL-FOL pairs called MALLS, a 7B LLaMA-based model fine-tuned with LoRA for direct translation and correction (LOGICLLAMA), and a training framework for iterative chain-of-thought-style correction that combines supervised fine-tuning on synthetically perturbed FOL with RLHF using a logical-equivalence-based reward. The paper reports that LOGICLLAMA outperforms GPT-3.5 on direct translation and that, when used to correct GPT-3.5 outputs, it approaches GPT-4 performance on FOLIO at much lower cost.

Overall, I found the paper interesting and timely. The problem is important: robust NL-to-formal-logic translation would be valuable for reasoning, theorem proving, semantic parsing, and logic-grounded NLP systems. The paper is also ambitious in scope, spanning dataset creation, model training, reward design, and empirical evaluation. The practical angle is appealing as well: the authors emphasize single-GPU fine-tuning and local deployment, which is relevant for privacy- and cost-sensitive settings. The empirical results on FOLIO are promising, especially the reported improvement from GPT-3.5 few-shot performance to GPT-3.5 plus LOGICLLAMA correction. The paper is generally readable, and the figures illustrating direct translation versus naive correction versus iterative CoT correction are helpful, especially Figure 2 on page 5 and Figure 3 on page 6.

That said, I have substantial reservations about the rigor of the evaluation and the strength of some of the claims. The central issue is that the paper’s strongest contribution may actually be the dataset and the practical recipe, but the evidence for dataset quality, reward validity, and generalization is not yet strong enough to fully support the broader conclusions. In particular, the paper relies heavily on GPT-4-generated silver supervision, a custom logical equivalence metric that is only an approximation, and relatively limited evaluation on two benchmarks that are themselves imperfect and small. As a result, while the work is promising, I do not think the current version fully establishes that the proposed method reaches “GPT-4-level” NL-FOL translation in a robust sense.

The dataset contribution is potentially valuable. MALLS is much larger than prior sentence-level NL-FOL resources, and Table 1 on page 3 gives useful descriptive statistics showing larger vocabulary and more literals per rule than FOLIO and LogicNLI. The prompting pipeline in Section 3.1 is thoughtfully designed: the negative n-gram control, randomization over logical operators and variable counts, and the “break-down prompt” to discourage overly fused predicates are all sensible heuristics. The verifier based on a CFG parser is also a reasonable first-pass filter for syntactic validity. The visualizations on page 3 and in Appendix Figure 7 suggest some diversity in term co-occurrence structure, and Figures 8 and 9 in the appendix provide additional frequency statistics.

However, the evidence for semantic correctness of MALLS is weak. The authors explicitly acknowledge in Section 3.2 that they do not conduct rigorous alignment checking between NL and FOL beyond a simplistic term-frequency threshold. This is a major limitation because semantic alignment is exactly the core challenge in NL-FOL translation. A dataset can be syntactically valid and lexically diverse while still containing many subtle semantic errors: quantifier scope mistakes, incorrect polarity, missing existential commitments, wrong argument structure, or over-compressed predicates. The paper’s main defense is downstream performance on FOLIO and LogicNLI, but that is only indirect evidence. Since the entire training pipeline depends on MALLS, I would have expected at least a manual audit of a random sample with expert judgments on semantic faithfulness, perhaps broken down by operator type and complexity. Without such an audit, it is difficult to know whether the model is learning correct translation or merely inheriting GPT-4’s biases and shortcuts.

Relatedly, there is a conceptual tension in the paper’s framing. The authors motivate the work partly by saying GPT-4 is costly and not always deployable, which is fair. But the proposed system depends on GPT-4 for dataset creation and on GPT-3.5 at inference time in the correction setting. So the strongest reported results are not from a standalone local model, but from a pipeline that still requires a proprietary upstream model. The direct translation results for LOGICLLAMA are still good and perhaps the more important practical result, but the paper sometimes blurs the distinction between “local model competitive with GPT-4” and “local model plus GPT-3.5 correction pipeline approaching GPT-4.” These are materially different deployment scenarios and should be separated more clearly in the claims.

The modeling ideas are creative. The distinction among direct translation, one-shot correction, and iterative CoT correction is well motivated, and Figure 2 on page 5 makes the setup concrete. The synthetic perturbation strategy in Section 4.2.1 is also a clever way to create supervision for correction steps when real correction traces are unavailable. The perturbation taxonomy in Table 2 on page 6 covers several plausible edit types, including label changes, insertions, and deletions. I also appreciated the inclusion of negative examples with zero perturbations to discourage over-correction.

Still, I am not fully convinced that the synthetic perturbation process matches the error distribution of GPT-3.5 outputs closely enough to justify the subsequent RLHF stage. The paper states that GPT-3.5 rarely generates syntactically invalid rules and therefore restricting perturbations to valid rules is acceptable, but syntactic validity is not the main issue. The more important question is whether the semantic and structural errors induced by random AST perturbations resemble the kinds of mistakes GPT-3.5 actually makes. For example, LLM outputs may exhibit systematic paraphrastic predicate renaming, scope ambiguity, omitted premises, or semantically plausible but globally wrong decompositions that are not well captured by local perturbations. The paper would be much stronger with an analysis comparing real GPT-3.5 errors against the synthetic perturbation distribution, or at least an ablation showing which perturbation types matter most for transfer to real correction.

The RLHF component is interesting but under-validated. The reward model is not learned from human preferences despite the terminology; instead it is a hand-designed mixture of a custom logical equivalence score and BLEU. This is not necessarily a problem, but calling it RLHF is somewhat misleading. It is closer to reinforcement learning from programmatic feedback or verifier-based rewards. More importantly, the logical equivalence metric itself appears approximate in ways that may materially affect both training and evaluation. In Section 4.2.3 and Appendix C, the authors compute equivalence by extracting literals, aligning them greedily using edit distance, padding with dummy literals when needed, and comparing truth tables under the best binding found. This is a heuristic similarity measure, not a sound or complete test of first-order logical equivalence. It ignores quantifier semantics beyond the propositionalized literal structure, depends on surface-form literal extraction, and may reward semantically incorrect but structurally similar formulas. The example in Figure 4 on page 6 is intuitive, but it also reveals the simplification: the method effectively reduces formulas to Boolean circuits over extracted literals. That may be useful as a proxy, but then the paper should be much more cautious in interpreting LE as logical equivalence.

This concern matters because LE is used both as an evaluation metric and as the main RL reward. If the metric is imperfect, the model may overfit to the proxy rather than true semantic correctness. The addition of BLEU partly compensates for lexical fidelity, but BLEU is also a weak semantic metric here. I would have liked to see validation of LE against human judgments or against a stronger theorem-proving-based equivalence checker on a subset. Even a small manually curated set of formula pairs with known equivalence/non-equivalence would help calibrate whether LE is meaningful. As it stands, the reported gains may partly reflect optimization toward the proxy metric.

The experimental results are encouraging but not fully comprehensive. Table 3 on page 8 shows that direct translation with LOGICLLAMA beats GPT-3.5 on both LogicNLI and FOLIO, and that RLHF CoT correction reaches 0.849 LE on FOLIO versus 0.855 for 5-shot GPT-4. This is the headline result, and it is impressive if robust. The comparison against both zero-shot and five-shot GPT baselines is useful. Table 4 and Figure 5 provide some analysis of iterative correction depth and performance by GPT-3.5 difficulty bins, which helps support the claim that correction is especially useful on harder examples. Figure 6 on page 9 also gives qualitative examples where CoT correction improves over naive correction.

However, several aspects of the evaluation weaken confidence. First, the paper does not report variance across runs, confidence intervals, or significance tests. Given the small size of FOLIO and the complexity of the pipeline, it is important to know whether the observed differences, especially 0.849 versus 0.855 LE, are statistically meaningful. Second, the paper includes 1K LogicNLI training examples in addition to MALLS because LogicNLI has a different rule distribution. This is understandable, but it complicates the claim that the model is trained solely on silver data and then evaluated on gold benchmarks. At minimum, the paper should report results without those 1K examples and clarify whether any benchmark-specific adaptation is necessary. Third, the evaluation benchmarks are limited. LogicNLI is acknowledged to be templatic and easy, and FOLIO is only around 2K examples. A broader evaluation, or at least a more detailed breakdown by operator type, quantifier complexity, and sentence phenomena, would be important.

I also found the baseline selection somewhat incomplete. Since the paper is fundamentally about fine-tuning an open model on GPT-generated data, stronger open-model baselines would be relevant. For example, how does plain supervised fine-tuning on MALLS compare across different base models or sizes? Is the gain due mainly to the dataset, the correction framing, or the RL stage? Table 3 includes direct translation, naive correction, SFT CoT correction, and RLHF CoT correction, which is good, but more ablations are needed. In particular, I would want to see: direct translation trained on MALLS only versus MALLS plus LogicNLI; correction without synthetic perturbation pretraining; RL with LE only versus BLEU only versus the mixture; different mixing weights; and perhaps a comparison to self-consistency or multi-sample decoding from GPT-3.5/GPT-4. The current ablations are not enough to isolate which ingredients are essential.

There are also some methodological ambiguities and possible issues. The paper says GPT-3.5 outputs are first corrected by naive correction before being fed to the RLHF CoT model in the appendix on page 16. This is an important detail that is easy to miss and should be stated clearly in the main paper, because it means the final correction pipeline is not simply GPT-3.5 followed by the RLHF model, but GPT-3.5 followed by naive correction followed by RLHF CoT correction. That makes attribution of gains less clear. Similarly, the term “iterative correction” could mean either multiple CoT steps within one generation or multiple rounds of generation; both appear in the paper and should be disentangled more carefully.

The paper’s qualitative examples are helpful but also expose some lingering issues. In Figure 6 on page 9, the corrected outputs still appear imperfect in places. For the “Pappy’s” example, the RLHF CoT correction seems to improve structure but may still not fully match the gold formula, and the formatting suggests possible truncation or missing parentheses. For the “taller man can block the other’s shooting” example, the final corrected formula still contains a free variable z unless the quantifier is restored correctly; the displayed output seems not fully well-formed semantically. These examples do not invalidate the approach, but they suggest that the system may still struggle with argument structure and quantification even when the metric improves. More careful qualitative error analysis would strengthen the paper.

The writing is mostly clear, but there are some overstatements and terminology issues. “First specialized LM for the NL-FOL translation task” seems too strong without a more careful survey of prior neural semantic parsing and logic translation systems. “RLHF” should be renamed or qualified, since no human preference data appears to be used in the reward model. “GPT-4 level performance” should be softened to “approaches GPT-4 on FOLIO under the proposed LE metric” or similar. There are also a number of minor grammatical issues and formatting inconsistencies throughout the paper and appendix, though these are fixable.

In terms of strengths, I want to emphasize that the paper does several things well. It identifies a practically meaningful niche where a small local model can complement larger proprietary models. It contributes a sizable dataset that, even if silver, may be useful to the community. It proposes a plausible correction-centric training paradigm rather than only direct translation. And it attempts to incorporate formal verification signals into training, which is a worthwhile direction. The single-GPU fine-tuning setup also lowers the barrier to reproduction. If the released code, weights, and data are complete and usable, that would further increase the paper’s practical value.

My main recommendations for improvement are as follows. First, add a manual quality audit of MALLS with expert annotation of semantic correctness on a representative sample, ideally stratified by complexity and operator type. Second, validate the LE metric against stronger semantic judgments, either human or theorem-prover-based, and clarify its limitations. Third, expand the ablation study to isolate the contributions of MALLS, correction framing, synthetic perturbations, iterative generation, and RL reward components. Fourth, report statistical uncertainty and, if possible, multiple random seeds. Fifth, clarify the exact inference pipeline and separate claims about standalone LOGICLLAMA from claims about GPT-3.5 plus LOGICLLAMA correction. Sixth, include a more systematic error analysis, especially for quantifiers, negation, disjunction, biconditionals, and predicate decomposition. Finally, consider renaming RLHF to something more precise such as verifier-guided RL or RL with programmatic feedback.

My overall assessment is positive but cautious. I think the paper contains a promising idea and a potentially useful resource, and it points toward an interesting paradigm for formal-language translation with small models. But the current empirical and methodological support is not yet strong enough for the strongest claims, especially around semantic correctness and GPT-4-level performance. With stronger validation of the dataset and reward metric, clearer ablations, and more careful framing, this could become a solid contribution. In its current form, I would view it as borderline: interesting and practically relevant, but not yet fully convincing by top-tier conference standards.
