Review ID: MV_049
Validation Track: RQ2
============================================================

This paper studies reward overoptimization in offline RLHF and proposes a theoretically motivated objective that adds an imitation/SFT term to preference optimization. The central claim is appealing and timely: the SFT loss can be interpreted as an implicit adversarial regularizer that mitigates overoptimization under distribution shift and reward uncertainty. The paper combines a maximin formulation, an equivalence argument leading to a practical objective called RPO, finite-sample guarantees under partial coverage, and empirical results on 7B-scale LPO/DPO-style tuning. Overall, I found the paper interesting and potentially impactful, especially because it connects a widely used practical heuristic—mixing SFT with preference optimization—to a principled pessimistic/adversarial view. At the same time, there are several technical and empirical issues that currently limit my confidence in the strength of the claims.

The main strengths are as follows. First, the problem is important. Overoptimization in RLHF is a central concern, and a theory-to-practice account of why adding SFT-like regularization helps would be valuable. Second, the paper’s conceptual bridge from a pessimistic reward-learning objective to a simple DPO+SFT objective is elegant and easy to understand at a high level. The message that “SFT changes gradient direction, not just scale” is intuitive and useful. Third, the theoretical development is more ambitious than many practical alignment papers: the authors work in an offline contextual bandit formulation with general function approximation, derive a finite-sample bound under a partial coverage coefficient, and then argue equivalence between the theoretical maximin objective and the practical minimax/RPO objective. Fourth, the practical method is simple and low-overhead, which increases the chance of adoption if the claims hold. Finally, the empirical results are directionally positive across two model families and several benchmarks.

That said, my overall assessment is mixed because the paper overstates some of its theoretical and empirical conclusions relative to what is actually established. The biggest issue is the gap between the theoretical object and the implemented algorithm. The theory proves guarantees for an exact optimizer of a maximin objective over a policy class and reward class, and then, under regularity assumptions, for the corresponding minimax solution. The practical algorithm, however, is a parameterized neural policy trained by SGD on a surrogate objective. There is no optimization error analysis, no approximation error analysis for the policy parameterization, and no argument that the implemented RPO training procedure reaches the minimax solution or even an approximate stationary point with meaningful guarantees. As a result, the statement that “incorporating SFT loss in RLHF gives you a regularizer that provably mitigates overoptimization” is too strong if interpreted as a guarantee for the actual LLM training procedure. What is proved is a guarantee for an idealized optimizer of a related objective under substantial assumptions.

Relatedly, the equivalence argument in Section 5.2 relies on assumptions that seem stronger and less benign than the paper suggests. Assumption 5.5 requires compactness of the reward class and a convex-like property tailored to the objective. In practice, the induced reward class corresponding to neural policies or the reparameterized policy family is not obviously compact or convex-like in the required sense. The text mentions convex classes such as linear or Lipschitz-continuous model classes as examples, but this does not really cover the neural-network setting used in experiments. Since the practical significance of the theory hinges on this equivalence, the paper should be much more explicit about the mismatch between assumptions and implementation. As written, the narrative risks implying that the theorem directly justifies the neural RPO training setup, which is not established.

I also found some notational and formal inconsistencies that make the theory harder to trust than it should be. In Section 5.2, equation (5.4) defines phi using a baseline action sampled from pi_ref, whereas earlier objectives use pi_base. This appears to be a substantive mismatch, not just notation, because the baseline policy is central to the method. There are several similar slips in the appendix, including places where pi_ref and pi_base are interchanged in decompositions. If these are typographical, they still matter because the proofs depend on the exact form of the objective. There is also a likely typo in the definition of \hat r_theta in Section 4: it should be a log ratio, but the text writes a quotient of logarithms. These issues are fixable, but in a theory-heavy paper they reduce confidence.

The sample complexity result itself is interesting but should be interpreted carefully. The guarantee is comparator-dependent and scales with a partial coverage coefficient and a KL term to the reference policy. This is reasonable, but it means the result does not show global robustness to overoptimization; it shows that under sufficient coverage of the comparator policy and bounded divergence from the reference, the learned policy competes with that comparator. That is a meaningful but narrower statement than the paper’s broader rhetoric about mitigating overoptimization in RLHF. Moreover, the choice of beta = 1/sqrt(N) in theory is quite different from the fixed practical beta values used in experiments, and eta is also selected by validation rather than theory. This is not inherently problematic, but it further weakens the “provably” framing for the practical algorithm.

I also think the role of the baseline policy deserves more scrutiny. The theory requires a baseline distribution that is covered by the data and enters both the adversarial objective and the practical imitation term. In experiments, the baseline is effectively the chosen responses in the preference dataset. This is a natural choice, but it makes RPO very close in spirit to existing methods that combine preference optimization with positive-example likelihood training, including ORPO-like and DPO-positive/Smaug-style objectives. The paper acknowledges some related work, but the novelty claim would benefit from a sharper delineation. Is the main novelty the exact adversarial derivation? The specific weighting? The equivalence proof? Or the empirical recipe? As written, the practical algorithm itself feels incremental, while the theoretical interpretation is the more novel contribution.

On the empirical side, the results are promising but not yet fully convincing. The in-distribution evaluation uses only 150 filtered examples judged by GPT-4. This is a small sample, and the filtering procedure for “undesired annotations” could introduce bias. The paper should report how many examples were discarded, why, and whether conclusions are robust without filtering or under alternative parsing rules. Confidence intervals or bootstrap uncertainty estimates are also needed, especially since some gains over DPO are modest. For example, the benchmark improvements in Table 2 are consistent but small in several cases; without variance estimates across seeds or evaluation noise, it is hard to know whether they are statistically meaningful.

The reliance on LLM-as-a-judge throughout is another limitation. Since the paper’s central claim concerns alignment with human preference and mitigation of reward misgeneralization, stronger human evaluation would be highly desirable, even on a modest subset. At minimum, the authors should discuss the limitations of GPT-4 judging more directly and perhaps include agreement checks with human annotators on a sample. This is especially important because the paper critiques proxy reward overoptimization, yet the evaluation itself relies heavily on another proxy.

The evidence for “overoptimization mitigation” is also somewhat indirect. The main diagnostic is higher log-probability on chosen responses during training and better pairwise judged outputs. While this is suggestive, it does not directly demonstrate reduced exploitation of reward model errors or improved robustness under distribution shift. Since the paper’s theory is framed around uncertainty and OOD actions, I would have liked to see more targeted experiments: for example, varying the strength of the SFT term and measuring tradeoffs; evaluating on deliberately shifted prompts; comparing against stronger regularized baselines such as IPO, ORPO, SimPO, DPO+NLL, or Smaug/DPO-positive; or constructing a synthetic/semi-synthetic setup where reward misspecification can be controlled and overoptimization measured directly. As it stands, the empirical section mostly shows that adding a small chosen-response NLL term helps relative to vanilla DPO in two training recipes, which is useful but not enough to fully validate the paper’s broader claims.

Another concern is the fairness and breadth of baselines. The paper compares primarily to DPO and the released Zephyr models. But because the proposed objective is essentially DPO plus an imitation term, the most relevant baselines are methods that already add such regularization or otherwise address overoptimization. The related work mentions ORPO and Smaug-like approaches, but they are not included experimentally. Without these comparisons, it is difficult to assess whether the theoretical framing yields a practically superior method or mainly rederives an already competitive heuristic. Even a simple ablation against “DPO + chosen-response NLL” with matched weighting and implementation details would help isolate whether RPO is more than a renaming of that recipe.

The PCA-based discussion of prompt distribution coverage is not very persuasive as evidence for the assumptions behind Corollary 5.8. Two-dimensional PCA overlap of embeddings from a proprietary embedding model is at best a rough visualization and does not meaningfully establish bounded density ratios between prompt distributions. I would recommend toning down the claim that the corollary explains benchmark generalization based on these plots. At most, the plots provide a qualitative sanity check that the benchmark prompts are not obviously disjoint from training prompts.

There are also some presentation issues. The paper is generally readable, but there are many typos and grammatical errors, and some equations are inconsistently referenced or described. In a mathematically dense paper, these issues accumulate. I strongly encourage a careful proofreading pass, especially around the definitions of phi, the baseline/reference policies, and the appendix proofs. The paper would also benefit from a clearer separation between what is proved for the idealized algorithm and what is hypothesized or observed for the neural implementation.

To strengthen the paper, I would suggest the following concrete improvements. First, clarify the exact scope of the theoretical claims and soften the practical “provably mitigates” language unless additional optimization/approximation analysis is added. Second, fix the notation inconsistencies and proof typos, especially the pi_base versus pi_ref mismatch. Third, add stronger empirical baselines, particularly methods that combine preference optimization with SFT/NLL regularization. Fourth, include ablations over eta, baseline choice, and perhaps beta, along with multiple random seeds and uncertainty estimates. Fifth, provide more direct evidence of overoptimization mitigation, ideally in a controlled setup where reward misspecification or OOD behavior can be measured. Sixth, if possible, add a small-scale human evaluation or at least judge-human agreement analysis. Seventh, discuss more explicitly how the assumptions in Assumption 5.5 relate—or fail to relate—to neural LLM training.

In summary, I think the paper contains a genuinely interesting idea: a pessimistic/adversarial interpretation of SFT regularization in offline RLHF, leading to a simple practical objective. The theory is nontrivial and the empirical results are encouraging. However, the current version overconnects the theory to the practical algorithm, lacks the most relevant empirical comparisons, and contains enough formal inconsistencies that I am not yet fully convinced. With a tighter statement of contributions, corrected theory presentation, and stronger experiments, this could become a strong paper. In its current form, I view it as promising but not yet fully mature for acceptance at a top-tier venue.

My recommendation is borderline leaning negative. The conceptual contribution is strong, but the evidence and rigor around the theory-to-practice bridge need improvement.
