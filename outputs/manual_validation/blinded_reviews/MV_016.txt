Review ID: MV_016
Validation Track: RQ2
============================================================

This paper tackles an important and timely problem: standard NLI benchmarks contain substantial annotation artifacts and spurious correlations, so raw test accuracy can overstate actual reasoning ability. The paper proposes to characterize existing test examples into easy/ambiguous/hard subsets using training-dynamics features, with the goal of automatically deriving a more challenging and less artifact-driven evaluation set without manually constructing adversarial examples. The same machinery is also applied to training data selection. Overall, I found the motivation strong and the empirical patterns interesting, but I also have significant methodological concerns that limit how strongly I can trust the central claims.

The main idea is intuitive and potentially useful. The authors extend dataset cartography by combining confidence, variability, correctness, and AUM, computed both for a standard premise+hypothesis model and a hypothesis-only model, then clustering examples with a GMM into three difficulty levels. The resulting hard subsets of SNLI and MultiNLI are indeed much more difficult for RoBERTa and less exploitable by hypothesis-only baselines. The paper also attempts to validate that these subsets exhibit reduced heuristic artifacts such as overlap, antonyms, and negation cues. This is a sensible direction, and one appealing aspect is that the method uses naturally occurring examples rather than synthetic stress tests.

That said, the most serious issue is the decision to train models directly on the test set in order to compute training dynamics for test-set characterization. Even though the authors state that this is “only to gather training dynamics and not directly use it as a classifier,” this is still a form of test-set fitting. The proposed hard/easy partition is defined using labels and optimization trajectories on the very evaluation set being characterized. This makes the resulting benchmark less of an independently constructed test set and more of a post hoc partition based on model learnability under supervised access to test labels. In other words, the method does not create a challenge set from unlabeled held-out data; it uses the labels of the held-out data to decide which examples are hard. That may still be acceptable as an analysis tool, but it substantially weakens the framing as an automated construction of a new test set for fair evaluation. At minimum, the paper should discuss this much more explicitly and position the method as benchmark diagnosis rather than benchmark creation.

Relatedly, because the partition is induced by a particular model family and training setup, there is a risk that “hardness” mostly reflects idiosyncratic optimization behavior rather than intrinsic linguistic difficulty or artifact-freeness. The cross-model transfer experiment with DeBERTa is helpful, but not fully convincing. Table 4 suggests some transfer, yet there are also large discrepancies, especially for ambiguous and hard subsets in MultiNLI. This does not invalidate the approach, but it suggests that the claim of model independence is overstated. More diverse architectures, seeds, and training regimes would be needed before concluding that the splits are robust and model-agnostic.

A second major concern is that the paper conflates several notions: difficulty, ambiguity, artifact prevalence, and data quality. These are related but not equivalent. An example can be hard because it is genuinely reasoning-intensive, because it is mislabeled, because it is out-of-domain, because it is lexically unusual, or because the model is poorly calibrated. The paper acknowledges calibration issues briefly, but the empirical analysis does not disentangle these possibilities. In particular, the hard subsets contain many more neutral examples, and neutral is already known to be the most difficult class in SNLI/MultiNLI. Therefore, some of the observed performance drop may simply reflect label-distribution shift rather than successful removal of spurious cues. The authors do analyze heuristic measures by class, which is useful, but I would have liked stronger controls: for example, compare against class-balanced random subsets of equal size, or construct class-balanced hard/easy splits and re-evaluate. Without such controls, it is difficult to know how much of the effect comes from reduced artifacts versus changed class composition.

The statistical analysis around spurious correlations is directionally encouraging but still somewhat limited. The paper reports Mann-Whitney tests over heuristic measures across classes and difficulty levels, concluding that the hard split shows little evidence of spurious correlations. However, the heuristics used are fairly shallow and incomplete proxies for annotation artifacts. Absence of significant differences on these heuristics does not imply absence of artifacts. Moreover, significance is sensitive to sample size, and the hard subsets are relatively small. I would have preferred effect sizes, confidence intervals, and perhaps predictive analyses showing that these heuristics are less label-informative in the hard split than in the easy split. For example, train a simple heuristic classifier on each split and compare its accuracy. That would directly support the claim that the hard split is less shortcut-driven.

There are also some technical and presentation issues. The variability formula in Equation (2) appears incorrect as written: it seems to omit squaring the deviation term inside the sum. This may be a typographical error, but it should be fixed. More broadly, the paper does not provide enough detail about the GMM setup: covariance type, initialization, number of restarts, sensitivity to random seed, and whether three clusters were fixed a priori or validated empirically. Since the clustering step is central, robustness analyses are important. It would also help to compare against simpler alternatives, such as thresholding on confidence or hypothesis-only confidence alone, to show that the full 8-feature GMM is necessary.

The training-set characterization results are interesting but also somewhat hard to interpret. The paper claims that training on selected subsets can match or exceed full-data performance on Naik et al.’s stress tests while using less data. This is potentially valuable, but the evaluation is narrow: only one downstream stress benchmark is used, and in some cases the selected subsets underperform on overall accuracy or on certain categories. Since the method is supposed to improve data quality, it would be important to report standard in-domain validation/test performance alongside stress-test performance for each training subset, and ideally calibration or robustness metrics as well. Otherwise, it is unclear whether the method improves generalization broadly or merely tunes toward a particular benchmark. Also, the comparisons to Data Maps and AUM seem somewhat ad hoc because the thresholds for those baselines are chosen heuristically and may not be optimized fairly.

I did appreciate the inclusion of FEVER as a dataset with weaker hypothesis-only bias. This helps show that the method is not only detecting classic SNLI/MultiNLI artifacts. Still, the FEVER setup raises its own questions because the premise/hypothesis roles are nonstandard and the task structure differs from canonical NLI. The dramatic drop on the hard FEVER split could indicate genuine difficulty, but it could also reflect annotation noise or evidence-selection peculiarities. More qualitative analysis would strengthen this part.

The paper’s strengths are clear. The problem is important, the approach is conceptually simple, and the empirical findings are plausible and potentially useful for benchmark diagnosis. The use of both standard and hypothesis-only training dynamics is a nice idea. The paper also makes a reasonable effort to connect the induced splits to known heuristics and to test transfer across encoders. If reframed more modestly as a method for analyzing and partitioning benchmark examples by model-dependent learnability and artifact susceptibility, I would find the contribution more convincing.

However, in its current form, I think the paper overclaims on benchmark construction, model independence, and artifact removal. The central methodological choice of using supervised training on the test set to define the test partition is the biggest obstacle for me. This does not make the analysis invalid, but it changes what kind of contribution this is. I would encourage the authors to either redesign the method so that test characterization does not require fitting on test labels, or substantially revise the framing and claims.

For improvement, I would strongly recommend the following. First, add controls for label distribution by evaluating class-balanced hard/easy subsets or matched random subsets. Second, quantify artifact reduction more directly, for example via heuristic-only classifiers or mutual information between heuristics and labels across splits. Third, provide robustness analyses for the clustering procedure across seeds, epochs, and model families. Fourth, compare against simpler scoring functions to justify the full feature set and GMM. Fifth, clarify the exact status of the proposed “test set construction” given that it uses test labels during optimization. Finally, expand the qualitative analysis of hard examples, including estimates of annotation error rates.

My overall assessment is that this is a promising and interesting empirical study with a useful diagnostic idea, but the current methodology and framing are not yet strong enough for a top-tier conference acceptance. I would lean borderline reject in its present form, while noting that a revised version with tighter claims and stronger controls could become a solid contribution.
