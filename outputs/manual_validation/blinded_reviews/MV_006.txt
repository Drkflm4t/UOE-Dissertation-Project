Review ID: MV_006
Validation Track: RQ2
============================================================

This paper tackles an important and timely problem: standard NLI benchmarks such as SNLI and MultiNLI are known to contain annotation artifacts and spurious correlations, which make reported test accuracy an unreliable proxy for genuine inference ability. The paper proposes an automated way to partition an existing test set into difficulty levels using training dynamics, with the goal of isolating a harder subset that is less contaminated by shortcuts. The method extends dataset cartography by combining eight features derived from training dynamics under both premise+hypothesis and hypothesis-only training, then clustering examples with a Gaussian mixture model into easy/ambiguous/hard groups. The paper further applies the same characterization to training data and argues that training on selected subsets can match or exceed full-data performance on stress tests while using less data.

Overall, I found the paper interesting and potentially useful, especially because it aims to produce harder evaluation subsets without manually constructing synthetic adversarial examples. The central intuition is sensible: examples that are easy to fit even in a hypothesis-only setting are likely to reflect artifacts, while examples that remain difficult across training dynamics may better reflect genuine inference. The empirical trends are also broadly plausible: the hard subsets are much smaller, substantially lower-performing for strong encoders, and much closer to chance for hypothesis-only models. This is a meaningful result if robustly established. The paper is also commendably broad in scope, covering SNLI, MultiNLI, and FEVER, and comparing RoBERTa and DeBERTa.

That said, I have substantial concerns about the methodological framing and the strength of the claims. The biggest issue is that the proposed “test set characterization” is obtained by training directly on the test set itself. The authors note that this is done only to gather training dynamics and not to use the resulting model as a classifier, but this does not fully resolve the conceptual problem. Difficulty is being defined in terms of memorization/fit dynamics on the very examples later used for evaluation. This makes the resulting hard split partly endogenous to the model and optimization procedure used to define it. In other words, the paper is not constructing an independently challenging test set; it is constructing a subset that is hard for a particular family of models under a particular training protocol. This is not necessarily invalid, but it is a much narrower claim than “automated construction of a challenging test set” or “more accurate measure of model performance in all contexts.” The paper should be much more explicit that the method identifies examples that are hard-to-fit under chosen models and training dynamics, rather than universally hard or artifact-free examples.

Relatedly, the claim of model-agnosticism is overstated. The cross-model comparison with RoBERTa and DeBERTa is useful, but it is not sufficient to establish model independence. Both are closely related pretrained transformer encoders with similar fine-tuning behavior. Table 4 actually suggests notable instability in some splits, especially for ambiguous and hard subsets in MultiNLI and FEVER, where accuracies differ substantially depending on which model produced the characterization. This does support some transferability of the coarse easy/hard distinction, but not strong model-agnosticism. A more accurate claim would be that the method shows partial robustness across two modern encoder families.

A second major concern is the lack of sufficiently strong baselines and ablations for the proposed characterization method. The paper combines four training-dynamics features from P+H and four from H-only, then clusters with a 3-component GMM. However, it is unclear how much each design choice matters. For example: how much improvement comes simply from using hypothesis-only confidence or correctness? How much comes from adding AUM? Is GMM actually better than k-means, hierarchical clustering, or even simple thresholding on one or two features? Since the paper’s novelty lies in the combination of features and clustering strategy, ablations are essential. Without them, it is difficult to know whether the gains over Data Maps/AUM arise from the richer feature set, from using H-only dynamics, from unequal cluster sizes, or simply from tuning a more flexible partitioning procedure.

The statistical analysis around spurious correlations is suggestive but not fully convincing. The paper uses a small set of automatically computed heuristics—word overlap, antonyms, length mismatch, misspellings, negation—and shows that differences across labels diminish in the hard split. This is directionally encouraging, but these heuristics are only a narrow proxy for annotation artifacts. The absence of significant differences on these measures does not imply the absence of spurious correlations more broadly. In fact, many known NLI artifacts are lexical, syntactic, discourse-level, or annotation-process-specific in ways not captured by these heuristics. The paper should therefore moderate claims such as “minimizes spurious correlations” or “providing a more accurate measure of model performance in all contexts.” At best, the results show reduction in a selected set of measurable heuristics and reduced hypothesis-only exploitability.

I also have concerns about the interpretation of the hard split. The paper often equates hardness with reduced artifacts, but these are not the same thing. Examples can be hard because they are genuinely inference-demanding, because they are mislabeled, because they are ambiguous even for humans, because they are out-of-domain, or because they are linguistically noisy. The authors acknowledge that the method is not suitable for identifying mislabeled examples, but this limitation is more central than presented. Especially since the method trains on the test set and uses fit dynamics, mislabeled or annotation-inconsistent examples may naturally migrate into the hard cluster. The manual inspection anecdotes are helpful, but a more systematic human analysis of the hard split would be needed to support the claim that it contains “more realistic and diverse linguistic phenomena” rather than simply more problematic examples.

There are also some technical and presentation issues. The definition of variability in Equation (2) appears incorrect as written: the squared deviation term seems to be missing inside the summation. If this is only a typesetting error, it should be corrected; if implemented as written, the feature would be invalid. More generally, the paper should specify whether training dynamics are collected per epoch on the full dataset, whether predictions are taken before or after each epoch’s updates, and how randomness is handled. Since clustering may be sensitive to initialization and fine-tuning noise, reporting variance across random seeds would be important. At present, the paper reports single numbers throughout, which makes it hard to assess robustness.

The training-set characterization results are interesting but also somewhat difficult to interpret. The paper compares against Data Maps and AUM on Naik et al.’s stress tests and shows that some subset combinations outperform full-data training. However, the comparisons are not entirely apples-to-apples. Your method produces variable subset sizes, while the baselines are instantiated with percentile thresholds that may not be optimal for them. Also, the best-performing subset combinations often use much more than one-third of the data, and in some cases the gains over full-data training are modest. This section would be stronger with validation-based model selection for all methods, confidence intervals, and perhaps evaluation on the original in-domain test sets as well as stress tests, to quantify the tradeoff between robustness and standard accuracy.

I did appreciate several aspects of the empirical study. The hypothesis-only dynamics are a clever addition and well motivated for NLI. The FEVER experiment is useful because it tests whether the method merely detects hypothesis artifacts or can also identify generally difficult examples in a dataset with weaker hypothesis-only bias. The monotonic degradation in accuracy from easy to hard across datasets is a strong sanity check. The paper is also generally well motivated and connected to prior work on NLI stress testing and training dynamics.

To strengthen the paper, I would strongly recommend the following. First, reframe the contribution more carefully: this is a model- and training-procedure-dependent characterization of benchmark instances, not an objective reconstruction of a clean test set. Second, add ablations isolating the contribution of P+H features, H-only features, AUM, and GMM clustering. Third, report stability across seeds and clustering runs. Fourth, include stronger validation of the hard split through human annotation or targeted linguistic analysis, ideally distinguishing genuinely reasoning-intensive examples from mislabeled or ambiguous ones. Fifth, compare against simpler alternatives, such as ranking by hypothesis-only confidence, by P+H minus H confidence gap, or by standard cartography features alone. If those simpler methods perform similarly, the paper’s main novelty would need to be reconsidered; if not, the current method would be much better justified.

In terms of significance, I think the paper addresses a real problem and offers a promising direction. However, the current evidence does not fully support some of the stronger claims, and the methodology needs more careful validation to establish that the resulting hard subsets are genuinely better evaluation sets rather than merely harder-for-these-models subsets. My overall assessment is therefore mixed-positive on the idea, but not yet fully convinced by the execution.

If I were reviewing this for a top-tier ML/NLP venue, I would lean borderline reject in its current form, mainly due to the conceptual issue of characterizing the test set by training on it, the insufficient ablation/robustness analysis, and overclaiming around model-agnosticism and spurious-correlation removal. With a stronger reframing and additional experiments, this could become a solid and useful paper.

The main strengths are the importance of the problem, the intuitive use of training dynamics plus hypothesis-only signals, the broad empirical coverage, and the potentially practical utility for benchmark diagnosis. The main weaknesses are the endogenous definition of test hardness, limited validation that hard examples are truly better evaluation instances, insufficient ablations, and claims that currently go beyond the evidence.
