# Review ID: MV_024

**Validation Track:** `RQ2`

---

This paper tackles an interesting and important problem: translating natural language into first-order logic, with the practical goal of obtaining a local, relatively small model that approaches the performance of frontier proprietary LLMs. The paper contributes three main pieces: a GPT-4-generated silver dataset of 34K NL-FOL pairs (Malls), a LoRA-finetuned LLaMA-7B model for direct translation and correction (LogicLLaMA), and a correction-oriented training pipeline combining synthetic perturbation-based supervised fine-tuning with PPO-style RLHF using a logic-based reward. The overall framing is timely, the task is meaningful for neuro-symbolic NLP, and the empirical claim that a 7B local model can approach GPT-4-level performance on FOLIO is potentially impactful.

The strongest aspect of the paper is its practical ambition. The authors identify a real deployment gap: GPT-4 can often perform NL-FOL translation reasonably well, but cost, privacy, and lack of customization motivate distillation or specialization into a local model. The correction setup is also intuitively appealing. Rather than forcing a small model to solve the entire task from scratch, the paper leverages GPT-3.5 as a first-pass translator and trains LogicLLaMA to repair its outputs. This “heavy lifting plus correction” paradigm is plausible and, if robust, could generalize beyond NL-FOL. The dataset construction pipeline is also thoughtfully engineered, with explicit attempts to diversify prompts and enforce syntactic validity.

That said, I have substantial concerns about the paper’s methodological rigor and the strength of its central claims. My overall assessment is that the paper is promising but not yet fully convincing as a top-tier conference submission, primarily because the evaluation and reward design appear to simplify first-order logic in ways that may undermine the validity of the reported gains.

The most serious issue is the definition of “logical equivalence” used both for evaluation and as the RL reward. The paper proposes to compare FOL formulas by extracting literals, treating them as Boolean variables, constructing truth tables, and then greedily binding literals between formulas. This is not, in general, a sound notion of equivalence for first-order logic. It effectively collapses formulas toward propositional structure over extracted atoms, ignoring or severely simplifying quantification, variable binding, predicate arity interactions, and domain semantics. For example, formulas that differ crucially in quantifier scope or variable dependencies may still look similar under this abstraction, while formulas that are semantically equivalent under renaming or standard logical transformations may be mishandled by the greedy literal matching. Since this approximate LE metric is used not only for reporting results but also as the reward model in RLHF, the entire optimization target may be misaligned with true semantic correctness. This is a foundational concern, not a minor implementation detail.

Relatedly, the paper repeatedly refers to “FOL verifier” and “logical equivalence solver,” but what is actually implemented seems to be a syntactic CFG parser plus an approximate propositionalized overlap metric. That distinction matters. A syntax checker is not a semantic verifier, and an approximate truth-table comparison over extracted literals is not a general FOL equivalence solver. The paper should be much more precise in its terminology and much more cautious in its claims.

A second major concern is dataset validity. Malls is generated by GPT-4, filtered for syntax, and only weakly checked for NL-FOL alignment via term-frequency heuristics. The authors are commendably transparent that they did not conduct rigorous alignment verification and that the dataset should be treated as silver. However, the paper’s strongest claims depend on Malls being semantically reliable enough to train a high-quality translator. Without a careful manual audit, it is difficult to know whether LogicLLaMA is learning genuine NL-FOL mapping or merely inheriting GPT-4’s stylistic conventions and systematic errors. This concern is amplified by the fact that the evaluation benchmarks are relatively small and may share representational biases with the prompting scheme used to generate Malls.

The paper would be much stronger with a human evaluation of a representative sample from Malls, ideally reporting semantic correctness, syntactic validity, and adequacy of predicate decomposition. Even a few hundred manually checked examples would substantially improve confidence. As written, the paper asks the reader to accept that GPT-4-generated silver labels are “high-quality and diverse” largely on the basis of vocabulary statistics, operator counts, and downstream performance. Those are useful but insufficient proxies for semantic fidelity.

A third concern is possible contamination or benchmark alignment effects. The paper states that few-shot examples for GPT-4 data generation were initially sampled from FOLIO and later from generated pairs, and that the authors “checked to ensure none of the FOLIO examples, or close variations are leaked.” This is reassuring in spirit but too vague in practice. What exact deduplication or similarity filtering was used? Was it lexical, embedding-based, or manual? Given that FOLIO is small, even modest overlap in semantics or templatic structure could inflate downstream results. More broadly, because both Malls and the baselines rely on GPT-family models, there is a risk that the learned representation style is unusually compatible with GPT-generated or GPT-inspired annotations. A stronger evaluation on independently annotated datasets, or at least a more detailed contamination analysis, would help.

The empirical section is encouraging but incomplete. The headline result on FOLIO, where GPT-3.5 plus LogicLLaMA approaches GPT-4, is interesting. However, the paper does not provide enough statistical or experimental detail to judge robustness. I would like to see variance across random seeds, especially because LoRA finetuning and PPO-style RLHF can be unstable. I would also like to see exact table values for all systems, significance testing where appropriate, and a clearer breakdown of syntactic validity versus semantic correctness. Since the proposed method includes several stages—direct translation, naive correction, synthetic CoT SFT, and RLHF—an ablation isolating the contribution of each component is essential. The paper compares T2 and T4, but the role of T3 pretraining, the reward mixture weight, the perturbation distribution, and the iterative correction budget all deserve more systematic study.

The RLHF component is novel in spirit, but I am not fully convinced it is necessary or well justified in its current form. The reward is based on an approximate metric, and the “human feedback” terminology is misleading because no human preference data appears to be used. This is closer to reinforcement learning from automated feedback than RLHF in the standard sense. Moreover, because the reward includes BLEU, the model may be incentivized toward surface-form similarity rather than semantic correctness. The paper should clarify whether PPO actually improves over simply continuing supervised training on pseudo-targets or self-training on corrected outputs. A simpler alternative baseline—such as rejection sampling, best-of-n decoding under the reward, or direct preference optimization with synthetic preferences—would help establish whether PPO is the right tool here.

I also have concerns about the chain-of-thought framing. The paper says synthetic perturbations are used to “encourage chain-of-thought reasoning,” but the generated intermediate steps are essentially edit traces reversing perturbations. That is not obviously the same as natural reasoning about semantic mismatches between NL and FOL. It may still be useful as a scaffold for iterative correction, but the paper should avoid overstating this as genuine reasoning supervision. More importantly, because the model is trained to emit intermediate correction steps, there is a risk that performance gains come from structured decomposition or longer effective decoding rather than from CoT per se. A comparison against iterative correction without textual rationales would be informative.

There are also several technical ambiguities. The paper says GPT-3.5 outputs are first corrected by naive correction before being fed to T4 when syntactically invalid, but it is unclear how often this occurs and how much it affects results. The perturbation process is described at a high level, but the actual distribution over perturbation types, operator changes, and AST locations is not reported. Since the synthetic data is central to T3, these details matter. Likewise, the literal-binding procedure in the LE metric uses greedy edit-distance matching with a search-depth cap of 1000; this heuristic could materially affect both evaluation and reward, yet there is no sensitivity analysis.

On the positive side, the paper is fairly well motivated and generally readable. The examples help convey the correction setting, and the dataset creation section is more transparent than many papers using LLM-generated supervision. The single-GPU finetuning story is also practically valuable. If the claims hold under stronger evaluation, this could be a useful contribution to the neuro-symbolic and semantic parsing communities.

To strengthen the paper, I would strongly recommend the following. First, replace or at least supplement the current LE metric with a more semantically faithful evaluation. If full FOL equivalence checking is intractable in general, the authors could restrict to the benchmark grammar and use theorem provers or model checkers under bounded domains, or report exact-match modulo alpha-renaming and standard normalizations alongside the current metric. At minimum, they should validate the approximate LE metric against human judgments or a stronger symbolic checker on a manually curated subset. Second, conduct a manual audit of Malls and of model outputs on FOLIO/LogicNLI, reporting semantic correctness categories and common failure modes. Third, provide stronger ablations: T1 vs T2 vs T3-only vs T4, with and without BLEU in the reward, with and without iterative decoding, and with alternative correction targets. Fourth, clarify contamination controls and provide more details on prompt generation, filtering, and deduplication. Fifth, tone down the use of “RLHF” unless actual human feedback is involved.

I also think the paper should discuss limitations more explicitly. The FOL formalism used here excludes equality and appears constrained to a relatively narrow grammar. The dataset is sentence-level rather than discourse-level, so phenomena like anaphora, scope ambiguity, and context dependence are largely out of scope. The prompting pipeline may bias predicate naming conventions and decomposition style. These are not fatal limitations, but they should be acknowledged as boundaries on the contribution.

In summary, I find the problem important and the overall direction promising. The idea of training a compact local model to correct or emulate stronger LLMs for NL-FOL translation is compelling, and the dataset plus training pipeline could become useful resources. However, the current paper overclaims semantic rigor relative to what is actually validated. The approximate equivalence metric and weakly verified silver data leave too much uncertainty about whether the reported improvements reflect true gains in first-order semantic parsing. With stronger evaluation, clearer terminology, and more careful ablations, this work could become significantly more convincing.

My recommendation is borderline to weak reject in its current form, based mainly on concerns about evaluation validity and semantic correctness. The paper has clear potential, and I would encourage the authors to revise and resubmit with a more rigorous semantic assessment.
