# Review ID: MV_050

**Validation Track:** `RQ1`

---

This paper tackles natural-language-to-first-order-logic translation, an important and difficult problem at the intersection of semantic parsing, reasoning, and neuro-symbolic NLP. The paper proposes three main contributions: a 7B LLaMA-based model, LOGICLLAMA, for direct translation and correction of FOL outputs; a new 34K-example GPT-4-generated dataset, MALLS, for sentence-level NL-FOL supervision; and a training framework that combines supervised fine-tuning on synthetically perturbed FOLs with RLHF using a logical-equivalence-based reward. Overall, I found the paper interesting, ambitious, and practically motivated. The work is notable for trying to distill expensive proprietary model behavior into a smaller local model while also introducing explicit symbolic verification into both data creation and training. The empirical results are promising, especially on FOLIO, where the proposed correction pipeline narrows the gap between GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 at much lower cost.

The paper’s strongest aspect is that it addresses a genuinely underexplored but important task with a fairly complete end-to-end story: data creation, model design, training methodology, evaluation, and practical deployment motivation. The motivation is clearly articulated in the introduction: current LLMs can often produce nearly-correct formalizations, but the remaining errors matter a lot in logic, and relying on GPT-4 directly is costly and may be unsuitable for privacy-sensitive settings. The proposed “small corrector over large model outputs” framing is compelling. In particular, the distinction between direct translation and iterative correction is well motivated and empirically supported. The examples in Figure 2 on page 5 and Figure 6 on page 9 make the setup concrete and help explain why chain-of-thought-style correction may be easier than one-shot generation.

The MALLS dataset is a meaningful contribution in its own right. The paper does more than simply prompt GPT-4 and dump outputs: it describes a prompt pipeline with diversity control via n-gram frequency suppression, randomized FOL-form prompts, and a breakdown prompt to discourage overly fused predicates. The verifier-based filtering step is also important. Table 1 on page 3 gives useful comparative statistics against FOLIO and LogicNLI, showing that MALLS is substantially larger and appears more diverse in vocabulary and logical structure. The visualizations on page 3 and page 12, especially the frequent term-pair Sankey-style plots and the literal-frequency histogram, support the claim that the dataset is not narrowly templatic. I also appreciated the authors’ candor in explicitly calling MALLS a “silver” dataset and acknowledging that alignment between NL and FOL is only partially checked automatically. That honesty improves the credibility of the paper.

Methodologically, the SFT+RLHF pipeline is creative. The synthetic perturbation idea is sensible: if real correction traces are unavailable, generate controlled perturbations of gold FOL and reverse them into correction steps. Table 2 on page 6 clearly enumerates the perturbation operators, and the iterative correction formulation on page 7 is intuitive. The RLHF stage is also interesting because it replaces a learned reward model with a symbolic logical-equivalence solver plus BLEU mixture. This is a nice example of using task structure rather than generic preference modeling. The logical equivalence metric itself is one of the more novel technical pieces of the paper. The truth-table-based comparison with greedy literal alignment and dummy variables is not perfect, but it is a thoughtful attempt to evaluate semantic similarity beyond exact string match. Given how brittle text overlap is for logic forms, this is a valuable direction.

The empirical results are encouraging. Table 3 on page 8 is the key result: direct translation with LOGICLLAMA outperforms GPT-3.5 on both LogicNLI and FOLIO, and RLHF CoT correction reaches 0.849 LE on FOLIO versus 0.855 for 5-shot GPT-4. That is a strong headline result, especially considering the model size and hardware budget. The comparison between direct translation, naive correction, SFT CoT correction, and RLHF CoT correction is also informative. It shows that the full pipeline matters, and that SFT alone on synthetic perturbations is insufficient without RLHF adaptation to real GPT-3.5 outputs. Table 4 and Figure 5 provide useful analysis of iterative correction depth and performance as a function of GPT-3.5 difficulty. These analyses strengthen the paper by going beyond a single benchmark table.

That said, there are several limitations that should be addressed more carefully before publication.

The biggest concern is evaluation validity. The proposed logical equivalence score is clever, but it is only an approximation to true FOL equivalence. The paper’s method appears to reduce formulas to truth tables over extracted literals, with greedy literal matching based partly on edit distance and dummy padding when literal sets differ. This may work for many sentence-level formulas, but it is not a sound or complete equivalence test for first-order logic in general, especially in the presence of quantifiers, variable binding structure, arity differences, and renamed but semantically distinct predicates. The running example in Figure 4 on page 6 is simple and propositionalized. The paper should more explicitly discuss the scope and failure modes of this metric. For example, how often does LE disagree with human judgment or theorem-prover-based equivalence on a manually checked subset? Since LE is used both as an evaluation metric and as the RL reward, any mismatch here could materially affect the conclusions.

Relatedly, the use of BLEU as part of the reward is understandable but somewhat ad hoc. The paper argues that BLEU helps recover correct predicate/entity names, but this also risks rewarding superficial lexical similarity rather than semantic correctness. It would help to include an ablation over the reward mixing coefficient omega, or compare pure LE reward versus LE+BLEU. Right now, the choice of 0.7 seems under-justified.

A second major concern is possible contamination or circularity stemming from GPT-4-generated training data and GPT-family baselines. The paper evaluates on FOLIO and LogicNLI, which are external benchmarks, so this is not direct train-test leakage. However, because MALLS is generated by GPT-4 and the baselines are GPT-3.5/GPT-4, there is a risk that the student model is inheriting stylistic or representational conventions specific to GPT-4 rather than learning a broadly grounded NL-FOL mapping. This is not fatal, but the paper should discuss it more explicitly. In particular, it would be useful to know whether the gains persist under alternative annotation styles or on a small manually curated out-of-distribution set. The claim that MALLS is “high-quality” is plausible, but stronger validation would help.

A third concern is that the experimental setup somewhat favors the correction setting by using GPT-3.5 outputs as inputs and, according to page 16, first passing syntactically invalid GPT-3.5 outputs through naive correction before feeding them to the RLHF corrector. This is a reasonable engineering choice, but it means the reported RLHF CoT correction numbers are for a two-stage pipeline rather than a single model directly correcting arbitrary GPT-3.5 outputs. The paper should make this more prominent in the main text and perhaps report the exact fraction of examples requiring this preprocessing. More generally, the correction setting is practically useful, but it would be good to compare against stronger non-proposed correction baselines, such as self-refinement with GPT-3.5, majority voting, or theorem-prover-guided repair heuristics.

The baseline section is also somewhat limited. The paper compares mainly against GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 in zero/few-shot settings, which is useful, but there are prior neural semantic parsing approaches and logic translation systems cited in related work that are not empirically included. Even if many are not directly comparable, some stronger fine-tuned encoder-decoder or seq2seq baselines on FOLIO/LogicNLI would help contextualize the gains. Since the paper’s central claim is not just “better than prompting GPT-3.5” but also “a strong specialized model for NL-FOL translation,” broader comparisons would strengthen that claim.

Another issue is that LogicNLI seems close to saturation for most methods, as the authors themselves note. This makes FOLIO the more meaningful benchmark, but FOLIO is only around 2K examples. Given the importance of the task, I would have liked more evaluation slices by logical phenomenon: quantifiers, negation, disjunction, biconditionals, xor, number of literals, or predicate decomposition difficulty. Table 1 suggests MALLS contains richer operator usage than prior datasets, but the evaluation does not show where the model succeeds or fails across these dimensions. A fine-grained error analysis would be especially valuable because the paper’s main methodological novelty is about correction steps.

I also found some aspects of the paper under-specified or potentially brittle. The prompt templates in Table 5 on page 14 include a few examples that appear noisy or inconsistent; for instance, one few-shot example’s NL and FOL do not obviously align. If these are transcription issues, they should be corrected. More broadly, because the dataset is generated through prompting, small prompt artifacts may shape the learned formalization style. The paper would benefit from a short manual audit of MALLS quality, perhaps reporting annotation accuracy on a random sample. The current “simplistic verifier” for NL-FOL alignment described on page 4 seems too weak to support strong claims about semantic correctness.

There are also some technical questions about the RLHF stage. The paper says PPO is used with the LE/BLEU reward, but details about stability, reward variance, KL control, and sample efficiency are sparse in the main text. Since the model generates multi-round corrections and receives rewards on intermediate corrected FOLs, it would be useful to know whether reward is assigned only to final outputs or to each round, and how sensitive results are to the maximum number of generations. Table 4 partially addresses the latter, but more training details or ablations would improve reproducibility.

Despite these concerns, I think the paper has substantial merit. The problem is important, the proposed system is thoughtfully designed, and the results are strong enough to make the work noteworthy. I especially like the practical framing of using a small local model to repair or emulate stronger proprietary systems for formal reasoning tasks. The paper also contributes useful infrastructure: a dataset, a verifier, a correction framework, and a semantic evaluation attempt. Even where some components are imperfect, they are directionally valuable and likely to stimulate follow-up work.

For improvement, I would strongly encourage the authors to add a more rigorous validation of the LE metric, ideally against human judgments or theorem-prover-based equivalence on a sampled subset; include ablations for reward design, especially LE-only versus LE+BLEU and different omega values; provide a manual quality audit of MALLS with estimated NL-FOL alignment accuracy; clarify the exact inference pipeline for RLHF CoT correction, including the role of naive correction preprocessing; and expand error analysis on FOLIO by logical operator and formula complexity. If space is limited, some of this could go to the appendix, but at least the metric validation should be in the main paper.

In summary, this is a strong and timely paper on an important task. Its main contributions are meaningful, the empirical results are compelling, and the work is likely to be useful to both the semantic parsing and reasoning communities. My reservations are mostly about evaluation rigor and dataset validation rather than the core idea. With stronger evidence around the semantic correctness of both the data and the LE metric, this would be an even more convincing paper. As it stands, I view it as a solid submission with clear novelty and practical significance.
