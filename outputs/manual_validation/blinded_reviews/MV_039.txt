Review ID: MV_039
Validation Track: RQ2
============================================================

This paper tackles an important and underexplored problem: moving spoken dialogue systems beyond turn-based, half-duplex interaction toward genuinely synchronous, full-duplex behavior with overlaps, backchannels, and latency-aware turn-taking. The central idea is to adapt a pretrained autoregressive LLM into a wall-clock-synchronous dialogue model by interleaving two speaker streams, inserting periodic synchronization tokens, and training the model to predict deduplicated speech-unit chunks for both sides of the conversation. The paper also proposes a practical three-stage training recipe that relies heavily on synthetic speech generated from text dialogue corpora, followed by relatively modest amounts of real dual-channel spoken dialogue data. Empirically, the authors report substantial gains over dGSLM in semantic meaningfulness while maintaining comparable naturalness, and they further simulate interaction under one-chunk latency to argue robustness to realistic network delays.

Overall, I found the paper ambitious, timely, and technically interesting. The problem formulation is compelling, and the paper makes a credible case that “full-duplex” spoken interaction requires explicit temporal synchronization rather than merely better turn-end detection. The proposed synchronization-token mechanism is simple and elegant, and the latency-tolerant interaction procedure based on predicting the user’s current chunk before generating the agent’s next chunk is intuitively well motivated. The empirical results, especially the large human-rated gains in meaningfulness over dGSLM, suggest that leveraging a strong text-pretrained LLM is indeed a powerful advantage for spoken dialogue generation. At the same time, several aspects of the work would benefit from sharper experimental isolation, stronger baselines, and more rigorous evaluation of the claimed full-duplex capabilities. My overall assessment is positive, but not without reservations.

The strongest aspect of the paper is the conceptual reframing of spoken dialogue modeling as synchronous sequence modeling with an explicit shared clock. This is a meaningful contribution beyond simply adding speech tokens to an LLM. The periodic speaker tags serving as synchronization markers are a neat mechanism for injecting coarse timing into an autoregressive model that otherwise lacks any notion of wall-clock time. The deduplication strategy is also sensible: repeated HuBERT tokens are known to dilute semantic density, and the paper’s argument that timing can be preserved coarsely through synchronization tokens while modeling only novel units is plausible. The interpolation step at synthesis time is heuristic, but the overall design is coherent and practically implementable.

A second major strength is the training recipe. The three-stage progression from text/speech alignment, to synthetic full-duplex-without-overlap formatting, to real spoken dialogue fine-tuning is well thought out. In particular, the use of large-scale synthetic spoken dialogue to compensate for the scarcity of real dual-channel conversational speech is pragmatic and likely necessary. The sentence-level text/speech interleaving in stage 1 is also an interesting curriculum-like stabilization strategy for adapting a text-only LLM to speech tokens. This part of the paper feels like a valuable engineering contribution even beyond the specific SyncLLM architecture.

The human evaluation results are also notable. The reported meaningfulness gap versus dGSLM is large, and the fact that naturalness remains roughly comparable is encouraging. If these results are robust, they support the paper’s main claim that text-pretrained LLMs can substantially improve the semantic coherence of spoken dialogue generation without sacrificing turn-taking quality. The out-of-distribution evaluation on CANDOR is another positive aspect; many dialogue papers evaluate only in-domain, so the inclusion of OOD testing is appreciated.

That said, the paper has several weaknesses that limit how strongly I can endorse the claims.

The first concern is about the baseline set. The paper compares primarily against dGSLM, which is indeed a relevant prior full-duplex spoken dialogue model, but it is also a relatively specialized and older speech-only architecture. Since the paper’s main empirical advantage appears to come from leveraging text-pretrained LLM knowledge, the comparison risks conflating “full-duplex synchronization modeling” with “using a much stronger pretrained language backbone plus synthetic text-derived data.” A stronger evaluation would include at least one LLM-based spoken dialogue baseline that is adapted to speech but remains turn-based, and ideally a latency-aware cascaded baseline with ASR + text LLM + TTS plus explicit turn-taking heuristics or overlap/backchannel policies. Without such baselines, it is difficult to isolate how much of the gain comes from the synchronous formulation itself versus simply inheriting Llama3’s semantic competence.

Relatedly, the ablation evidence is too limited for a paper making several intertwined contributions. The method combines at least four major ingredients: synchronization tokens, deduplication, future user-chunk estimation for latency tolerance, and the three-stage synthetic-to-real training pipeline. The paper reports chunk-size comparisons, but I did not see sufficiently targeted ablations answering questions such as: what happens without synchronization tokens but with the same interleaved format; what happens without deduplication; what happens if the model conditions only on observed user chunks and incurs a one-chunk delay; what is the contribution of each training stage; how much real-data fine-tuning is actually needed; and how sensitive are results to the TTS system used for synthetic data generation? These are central to understanding whether the proposed design choices are necessary or merely convenient.

A second major concern is the evaluation methodology for semantics. The paper evaluates semantic quality by ASR-transcribing generated spoken dialogues, discarding overlaps, converting them into turn-based text dialogues, and then computing perplexity under a text dialogue model. This is at best an indirect proxy. It may reward lexical fluency and topical plausibility while missing important dialogue-level failures, especially those specific to full-duplex interaction such as inappropriate interruptions, mistimed backchannels, or semantically incoherent overlap behavior. Moreover, because overlaps are ignored in transcription, the evaluation may systematically erase exactly the phenomena the paper claims to model. The human meaningfulness ratings partly address this, but the automatic semantic metric remains weakly aligned with the stated task. At minimum, the limitations of this metric should be discussed more explicitly.

The naturalness evaluation is also somewhat narrow. Correlating average durations of IPUs, pauses, and FTOs with ground-truth continuations given the same prompt is an interesting diagnostic, but it does not fully establish natural conversational behavior. High correlation in aggregate timing statistics does not necessarily imply appropriate local turn-taking decisions. For example, a model could match average overlap durations while placing overlaps in pragmatically odd places. The human naturalness MOS is therefore important, but the reported differences are small and sometimes slightly favor the baseline. This weakens the claim that the model truly achieves superior full-duplex naturalness; the evidence more strongly supports “better semantics at similar perceived naturalness” than “better full-duplex dialogue overall.”

A third concern is that the “interaction” setting is still simulated model-model interaction rather than real human-agent interaction. I understand why this is done, but the paper’s framing emphasizes deployment-relevant full-duplex dialogue with internet-scale latency. The current experiments show that two SyncLLM instances can converse under one-chunk delayed access to each other’s outputs, but this is not the same as demonstrating robust interaction with real users, real ASR/TTS imperfections, variable network jitter, and spontaneous human interruptions. In particular, the user-side speech in the simulation is generated by another model that shares the same tokenization and training assumptions, which may make the task easier. The paper should be more careful not to overstate this as evidence of real-world full-duplex usability.

There are also some technical details that need clarification. The interpolation heuristic for reconstructing periodic HuBERT tokens from deduplicated chunks seems potentially lossy, especially for larger chunk sizes and rapidly changing acoustics. The paper states that the effect is imperceptible, but this is asserted rather than quantified. Some objective or subjective speech-quality analysis would help. Similarly, the choice of chunk sizes 160/200/240 ms is motivated by latency, but the tradeoff between chunk size, semantic quality, overlap fidelity, and synthesis artifacts is not deeply analyzed. Since chunk size is central to the method, a more systematic discussion would strengthen the paper.

The paper also leaves open questions about computational efficiency and streaming feasibility. Because the model predicts both speakers’ streams autoregressively and continuously, one wonders about inference cost, KV-cache growth, and whether real-time operation is actually achieved with Llama3-8B under realistic deployment constraints. The paper motivates internet latency, but not end-to-end system latency including tokenization, model inference, and vocoding. A full-duplex interface is only compelling if the system can run in real time; some throughput or latency measurements would be valuable.

Another issue is the dependence on synthetic data quality. Stage 1 and stage 2 rely on Bark TTS with only 10 speaker presets. This may introduce artifacts or unnatural prosodic regularities that shape the model’s learned timing and speaking style. The paper argues that stage 3 with real dialogue data corrects this, but it would be useful to know how much the model inherits from synthetic speech and whether different TTS engines materially change results. Given that the synthetic corpus is enormous relative to the real corpus, this matters.

On presentation, the paper is generally understandable, but there are places where the exposition could be tightened. Some claims are repeated multiple times, and there are several grammatical issues and typos. More importantly, some experimental details are missing or buried. For example, the exact text dialogue datasets used in stages 1 and 2 are not clearly enumerated in the main text, nor are training hyperparameters, context lengths, decoding settings, or the speech synthesis pipeline described in enough detail for reproducibility. Figure 7 is referenced but the image appears missing in the provided text, which makes it impossible to inspect that result. The paper would benefit from a clearer separation between continuation-mode claims and interaction-mode claims, since the evidence for the latter is weaker.

Despite these concerns, I do think the paper has substantial merit. The core idea of synchronizing an LLM to wall-clock dialogue via periodic tokens and interleaved chunk prediction is novel and likely influential. The empirical gains in meaningfulness are large enough that they are hard to dismiss, and the work opens an interesting direction for spoken dialogue systems that goes beyond conventional turn-based interfaces. My main hesitation is that the current experimental package does not fully disentangle the source of improvements or conclusively validate the strongest full-duplex claims.

For improvement, I would strongly encourage the authors to add targeted ablations: remove synchronization tokens, remove deduplication, remove future user-chunk estimation, and vary the amount of stage-3 real dialogue data. I would also encourage adding a stronger LLM-based baseline, ideally a cascaded or turn-based spoken dialogue system built on the same or similar text backbone, so that the benefit of synchronous modeling can be isolated from the benefit of text pretraining. If possible, even a small real-user study with live interaction would significantly strengthen the paper. Short of that, more realistic simulations with ASR errors, jitter, and heterogeneous user speech would help. Finally, reporting real-time factors and end-to-end latency would make the deployment claims much more convincing.

In summary, this is a creative and important paper with a strong central idea and promising results. It advances the conversation on spoken dialogue from turn-based response generation toward genuinely synchronous interaction, and it does so with a method that is conceptually simple enough to be broadly useful. However, the current evidence more convincingly demonstrates improved semantic spoken dialogue generation with comparable turn-taking naturalness than it does fully validated real-world full-duplex dialogue capability. I am positive overall, but I would want the final version to sharpen the claims, strengthen the ablations, and better isolate the contribution of synchrony modeling from the contribution of large-scale text-pretrained language modeling.

My recommendation is a weak accept. The novelty and potential impact are high, and the results are compelling enough to merit discussion by the community, but the paper would benefit from stronger experimental controls and more cautious interpretation of the interaction claims.
