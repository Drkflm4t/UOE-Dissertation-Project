# Review ID: MV_044

**Validation Track:** `RQ2`

---

This paper tackles an important and underexplored problem: translating natural language into first-order logic, with the dual goals of building a practical local model and reducing dependence on expensive proprietary APIs. The paper’s main contributions are a GPT-4-generated silver dataset of 34K NL-FOL pairs (Malls), a LoRA-tuned LLaMA-7B model for direct translation and correction (LogicLLaMA), and a correction-oriented training pipeline combining synthetic perturbation-based SFT with RLHF using a logic-based reward. The topic is timely, the problem is meaningful for neuro-symbolic NLP, and the paper is ambitious in trying to combine dataset creation, model adaptation, and reward design in one framework.

At a high level, I found the paper promising and practically motivated, but also methodologically uneven. The strongest aspect is the attempt to operationalize NL-FOL translation with a relatively small open model and a released dataset. The weakest aspects are the limited rigor around data quality, the somewhat ad hoc logical equivalence metric and reward design, and insufficient experimental detail and ablation to fully justify several central claims. My overall assessment is that the paper has clear merit and likely practical value, but it falls short of the level of empirical and methodological validation I would expect for a top-tier venue unless strengthened substantially.

The problem formulation is well motivated. The authors correctly note that even strong LLMs often produce imperfect formalizations, and that a local specialized model could be useful for privacy, cost, and controllability. The distinction between direct translation and correction is also sensible. In particular, the idea that a smaller model can specialize in “repairing” outputs from a stronger general model is interesting and potentially broadly useful beyond NL-FOL. This framing is one of the paper’s conceptual strengths.

The Malls dataset is potentially valuable, but the paper does not establish its quality convincingly enough. The authors are commendably transparent that the dataset is silver rather than gold, and they describe several mechanisms to encourage diversity and syntactic validity. However, the core issue in NL-FOL translation is semantic faithfulness, not merely syntactic well-formedness or lexical overlap. The paper explicitly admits that rigorous alignment checking was not performed, and the described alignment verifier is extremely weak: term-frequency overlap between FOL terms and the NL sentence is not a reliable proxy for semantic correctness. In fact, it may bias the dataset toward shallow lexicalized mappings and against legitimate abstraction or paraphrase. Since the entire training pipeline depends on Malls, the lack of a careful manual audit is a serious limitation. Even a modest human evaluation on a few hundred examples, with error categories such as quantifier mistakes, scope errors, predicate decomposition errors, and hallucinated entities, would have substantially strengthened the paper.

Relatedly, the use of GPT-4 both as a data generator and as a performance reference raises concerns about circularity. The paper argues that LogicLLaMA approaches GPT-4 performance on gold benchmarks, but because the training data is generated by GPT-4, the model may simply be distilling GPT-4’s representational conventions and biases. That is not inherently invalid, but it should be discussed more explicitly. The paper would benefit from analysis of whether LogicLLaMA generalizes beyond GPT-4-style formalization choices, especially since FOL often admits multiple equally valid predicate vocabularies and decompositions.

The prompting pipeline for dataset creation is interesting, especially the dynamic negative n-gram constraints and prompts encouraging predicate decomposition. These are practical engineering ideas. Still, the evidence for their effectiveness is mostly anecdotal. The diversity analysis based on vocabulary size, term frequencies, and co-occurrence graphs is suggestive but not sufficient. High lexical diversity does not imply semantic diversity, and term-pair connectivity is at best an indirect signal. I would have liked to see stronger analyses: distribution over logical phenomena, quantifier nesting depth, operator combinations, arity distributions, proportion of grounded versus quantified formulas, and manual comparisons against FOLIO on semantic complexity rather than surface statistics.

The model training setup is reasonably clear for the direct translation and naive correction tasks. The correction framing is intuitive and likely useful in practice. However, the paper’s central novelty claim is the SFT+RLHF chain-of-thought correction framework, and here the methodology is less convincing than the presentation suggests. The synthetic perturbation idea is sensible: perturb a gold FOL, then train the model to reverse the perturbations. But the perturbation space appears hand-designed and may not resemble the actual error distribution of GPT-3.5 outputs. The paper claims this synthetic stage equips the model with corrective reasoning, yet there is no quantitative comparison between synthetic perturbation types and real GPT-3.5 error types. Without such evidence, it is unclear whether the gains come from learning meaningful correction behavior or simply from additional supervised exposure to FOL editing patterns.

The RLHF component is also somewhat overstated. Strictly speaking, this is not “human feedback” in the usual sense; the reward is computed automatically from a logical equivalence score mixed with BLEU. This is closer to reinforcement learning from automated feedback than RLHF. That distinction matters because the paper invokes InstructGPT-style framing, but the reward source is entirely different. I would encourage the authors to rename this component more precisely. More importantly, the paper does not provide enough detail about the PPO setup, reward normalization, KL control, rollout lengths, stability, or sample efficiency. RL fine-tuning of language models is notoriously sensitive, and the absence of these details makes the results hard to assess or reproduce.

The logical equivalence metric is one of the most concerning parts of the paper. The authors propose comparing formulas via truth-table overlap after greedily binding literals and padding with dummy inputs. This may work as a heuristic for small propositionalized formulas, but it is not a principled treatment of first-order logic equivalence. FOL formulas with quantifiers, variable renaming, differing predicate vocabularies, and structural transformations cannot in general be reduced to a simple truth-table comparison over extracted literals without strong assumptions. The paper seems to collapse formulas into Boolean circuits over literals, which ignores the semantics of quantification and variable binding in a fundamental way. The greedy literal matching procedure is also potentially brittle and may reward semantically incorrect but structurally similar formulas. Since this metric is used both for evaluation and as the RL reward, any mismatch between the metric and true semantic equivalence directly affects the validity of the results. This is a major methodological weakness.

The addition of BLEU to the reward partly acknowledges that logical equivalence alone is insufficient, but BLEU is also a poor proxy for correct predicate naming or semantic alignment. In fact, mixing a weak semantic heuristic with a surface-form metric may encourage models to imitate reference syntax rather than produce genuinely correct formalizations. The paper would be much stronger if it used a more principled theorem-proving or model-checking based equivalence criterion where feasible, or at least validated the proposed LE metric against human judgments on a manually annotated subset.

The experimental results are encouraging but not fully persuasive. The headline claim is that LogicLLaMA direct translation outperforms GPT-3.5 and that correction mode approaches GPT-4 on FOLIO. If robust, this would be notable. However, the presentation lacks enough granularity to judge significance. The paper does not report variance across runs, confidence intervals, or statistical tests. Given the modest absolute differences on FOLIO, especially between some variants, it is hard to know how stable these gains are. This is particularly important because the training data is silver and the evaluation metric is nonstandard.

The choice of benchmarks is reasonable: LogicNLI and FOLIO are among the few available datasets with FOL annotations. But LogicNLI appears too easy and template-driven to be very informative, which the authors themselves acknowledge. That makes FOLIO the main meaningful benchmark, yet it is small. This further increases the importance of careful significance analysis and qualitative error analysis. I would also have liked to see cross-domain robustness tests, for example on held-out manually authored examples with phenomena underrepresented in Malls, or on adversarial paraphrases designed to stress quantifier scope and negation.

The comparison setup with GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 is useful, but there are some fairness questions. The paper compares a fine-tuned specialized model against prompted general-purpose models. That is a valid practical comparison, but it should be framed carefully. Also, because Malls is generated by GPT-4, the comparison to GPT-4 is not entirely clean: the specialized model may inherit GPT-4’s annotation style. It would help to compare against prior supervised semantic parsing or NL-to-logic baselines where possible, even if older or smaller-scale, to situate the gains relative to the literature rather than only proprietary LLMs.

One positive aspect is that the paper does attempt some analysis beyond aggregate scores. The comparison between direct translation, naive correction, and CoT correction is useful, and the observation that correction helps most on harder examples is plausible. Still, the analysis remains somewhat shallow. For example, the paper should break down errors by logical phenomenon: universal vs existential quantification, negation scope, implication direction, conjunction/disjunction confusion, predicate decomposition, constants vs variables, and biconditional/xor handling. Such analysis would reveal whether the model is truly learning formal semantics or mostly improving surface alignment.

I also have concerns about the chain-of-thought aspect. The paper claims that synthetic perturbations “encourage chain-of-thought reasoning,” but what is actually supervised seems to be textual correction steps, not necessarily faithful reasoning traces. There is no evidence that these intermediate steps are causally useful rather than just another output format. An ablation comparing hidden-step training, explicit-step training, and direct iterative editing without natural-language CoT would clarify whether the gains come from reasoning supervision or simply from decomposing the task into smaller edits. Given current concerns in the field about the interpretability and faithfulness of chain-of-thought, stronger evidence is needed before making substantive claims here.

The paper is generally readable and the motivation is clear, but some claims are too strong relative to the evidence. For example, calling LogicLLaMA “the first specialized LM for the NL-FOL translation task” may be difficult to defend given prior neural semantic parsing work and recent logic-focused fine-tuned models. Similarly, “GPT-4 level performance” feels overstated when the reported FOLIO score still trails GPT-4 by a nontrivial margin in the numbers quoted in the introduction. The cost argument is reasonable, but a more complete accounting should include the cost of generating Malls with GPT-4 and of RL training, not only inference-time API prices.

The reproducibility story is mixed. Releasing code, weights, and data is a strong positive. But the paper still omits several details needed to understand the RL stage and the exact prompting/evaluation setup. Since the method depends heavily on prompt engineering for both data generation and baseline evaluation, those prompts should be fully documented in the main paper or appendix. Likewise, the exact grammar, parser behavior, failure handling, and reward computation details are central rather than peripheral.

In terms of strengths, I would highlight four points. First, the paper addresses a genuinely important task at the intersection of NLP and formal reasoning. Second, the dataset and model release could be useful to the community. Third, the correction-oriented framing is practically appealing and may generalize to other structured generation tasks. Fourth, the empirical results, while not fully airtight, do suggest that a small open model can become competitive on this task through targeted fine-tuning.

In terms of weaknesses, the most important are the uncertain semantic quality of the silver dataset, the weakly justified logical equivalence metric, the limited rigor of the RLHF formulation and reporting, and the lack of deeper ablations and human evaluation. These are not minor issues; they affect the validity of the central claims.

To strengthen the paper, I would strongly recommend the following. First, add a manual evaluation of Malls on a representative subset, with inter-annotator agreement and fine-grained error categories. Second, validate the LE metric against human judgments or a stronger symbolic equivalence checker on a curated set of formula pairs. Third, provide ablations isolating the contributions of Malls, synthetic perturbation SFT, iterative correction, explicit CoT text, RL fine-tuning, and the BLEU component of the reward. Fourth, analyze real GPT-3.5 errors and compare them to the synthetic perturbation distribution. Fifth, report variance across seeds and statistical significance on FOLIO. Sixth, include more detailed qualitative analysis of failure cases, especially quantifier and scope errors. Seventh, clarify terminology around RLHF and perhaps rename it to avoid implying human preference data where none exists.

My overall recommendation is borderline. I see clear novelty and practical value, and I appreciate the ambition of building an open, specialized NL-FOL translator. However, for a top-tier conference, I would expect substantially stronger validation of the dataset quality and the semantic evaluation methodology before fully trusting the reported gains. If the authors can shore up those aspects, this could become a strong contribution; in its current form, it is promising but not yet fully convincing.
