# Review ID: MV_010

**Validation Track:** `RQ2`

---

This paper tackles an important and underexplored problem: helping users manage privacy risks in voluntary online self-disclosure. The paper’s central idea is compelling and timely. Rather than focusing only on classic PII detection or on post-level profiling, the authors aim to identify fine-grained self-disclosure spans and then generate less specific rewrites that preserve communicative utility. This is a meaningful shift in framing, and the combination of dataset creation, span-level modeling, user study, and generation experiments gives the work breadth. I found the paper interesting and potentially impactful, especially because it tries to bridge NLP and HCI rather than stopping at benchmark performance.

The strongest contribution is the problem formulation. The distinction between self-disclosure and standard PII is well motivated, and the paper makes a persuasive case that redaction is often too blunt for social media contexts where disclosure is intentional and socially functional. The proposed abstraction task is novel and intuitively useful. The paper also benefits from grounding in user feedback: the abstraction task is not introduced arbitrarily, but as a response to participants asking for help rewriting risky content. This iterative, human-centered framing is a real strength.

The dataset contribution is also valuable. A span-level corpus with 19 categories of self-disclosure is a useful resource, and the taxonomy is broader than much prior work. The examples in Table 1 make the task concrete, and the distinction between attributes and experiences is sensible. The annotation process appears thoughtful in several respects: in-house annotators, training, batch monitoring, and adjudicated recent batches for testing. The decision to annotate contextualized spans rather than minimal spans is also defensible for downstream usability, though it has implications for evaluation that should be discussed more explicitly.

On the modeling side, the identification results are promising but should be interpreted carefully. Achieving 75+ token F1 sounds strong, but the span-level F1 is much lower, around 49 for multi-class and 54 for binary in the best sentence-level setting. Since the practical utility of a highlighting tool depends heavily on accurate boundaries, the span-level numbers are arguably the more relevant metric. The paper does report them, which is good, but the narrative sometimes emphasizes token F1 more than is warranted. The per-class breakdown also reveals substantial unevenness: some categories such as Age and Location are reasonably strong, while Sexual Orientation and Relationship Status are much weaker. This matters because the most privacy-sensitive categories may also be among the hardest. I would encourage the authors to foreground this tradeoff more honestly.

A related concern is the lack of annotation agreement statistics. For a new 19-class span-labeling taxonomy, inter-annotator agreement is essential. The paper says two annotators first screened 10K posts and only posts both agreed were disclosing were retained, but it does not report agreement for the screening stage or for span/category annotation. Without agreement numbers, it is difficult to assess whether the task is well defined, whether some categories are inherently ambiguous, or whether the reported model ceiling is constrained by annotation inconsistency. This is one of the most important missing pieces in the paper.

The data construction pipeline also raises some questions. The authors sample 10K posts from a filtered Reddit dump, then reconstruct posts with comments via the API. However, the paper is not fully clear about what exactly is annotated: only the original post text, or also comments/replies authored by the same user, or all comments in the thread? Some wording suggests “posts (plus associated comments),” but the annotation instructions say spans that reveal information “about the authors themselves within each post,” which is ambiguous when comments from multiple users are present. Since self-disclosure is author-specific, this needs to be clarified carefully. If comments from other users are included, there is a risk of conflating disclosures by different people. If only the original poster’s text is annotated, that should be stated explicitly.

The taxonomy is broad, but some category design choices seem debatable. Splitting Husband/BF and Wife/GF while also having Relationship Status may encode gendered assumptions and create avoidable sparsity. Similarly, Age/Gender as a separate class from Age and Gender may be practical for annotation but complicates modeling and interpretation. It would help to justify why these categories are preferable to compositional labels or multi-label span annotations. More generally, the paper would benefit from a discussion of whether the taxonomy is intended as psychologically grounded, operational for modeling convenience, or optimized for intervention design.

The user study is a welcome addition and differentiates the paper from purely benchmark-driven work. It is useful to see that many participants reacted positively and that the study surfaced concerns around oversensitivity, subreddit norms, personalization, and explainability. These are exactly the kinds of issues that matter for deployment. That said, the study should be interpreted as formative rather than validating strong real-world effectiveness claims. The sample size is small, participants are all U.S.-based adult Reddit users, and the outputs shown to participants were manually prepared images rather than an interactive system. Also, the paper reports percentages such as 82% positive outlook, but with 21 participants these percentages can sound more precise than they are. I would recommend toning down claims of “real-world applicability” and framing the study more explicitly as exploratory evidence of perceived usefulness and design requirements.

The abstraction section is intriguing but currently less convincing than the identification part. The task itself is novel and worthwhile, but the experimental setup has several limitations. Most importantly, the training and evaluation targets are generated by ChatGPT/GPT-4 rather than written by humans. This means the models are effectively being trained to imitate another model’s notion of abstraction, and automatic metrics like BLEU/ROUGE are then measuring similarity to LLM-generated references. The paper does include human evaluation, which helps, but the dependence on synthetic references substantially weakens claims about true task performance. At minimum, the paper should discuss this more candidly and ideally include a small human-authored reference set or direct comparison against prompting strong LLMs at inference time.

The abstraction dataset is also quite small: 159 posts for training, 25 for validation, and 50 for testing from the most recent 10% of the corpus. It is not clear how many span instances this corresponds to, nor how they are distributed across categories. Since the task is span-level and one post may contain multiple disclosures, reporting the number of training examples is essential. The paper should also clarify whether all categories are represented and whether performance differs by category or disclosure type. As written, it is hard to know whether the generation results are robust or driven by a narrow subset of easier cases.

The evaluation methodology for abstraction has additional weaknesses. BLEU and ROUGE are poor proxies for privacy reduction and utility preservation in this setting, and the paper itself seems aware of this, since the main conclusions rely on human judgments. However, the human evaluation is limited: one author conducts the evaluation on 60 sampled instances, and later another round compares only the top two models. Single-rater evaluation is a serious concern, especially for subjective dimensions like privacy reduction and utility. Inter-rater reliability is not reported because there appears to be only one rater. This makes the reported differences between methods hard to trust. Also, the “coherence” metric is binary and reported as 93.7% for both top models, which suggests limited discriminative value.

A more substantive conceptual issue is how “privacy reduction” is defined. The paper uses Likert judgments of detail removal, but privacy risk is contextual and adversarial. Replacing “32” with “early 30s” may reduce specificity, but whether it meaningfully reduces re-identification risk depends on the rest of the post and external knowledge. Likewise, some abstractions may preserve enough semantic content to remain highly identifying in context. The paper should be more careful not to equate abstraction with privacy protection in a strong sense. What is demonstrated is perceived reduction in specificity, not formal privacy guarantees or even measured reduction in inference risk. This distinction is crucial.

The ethics discussion is thoughtful in some respects, but one statement is too strong: “We identified no potential harms that would disproportionately impact marginalized or otherwise vulnerable populations.” Given that the paper itself motivates the work partly through risks to marginalized groups, and given known disparities in NLP performance across dialects and identity-related disclosures, this claim seems insufficiently supported. There are plausible harms: false negatives for sensitive disclosures, over-flagging identity terms in support communities, or abstraction suggestions that erase identity-relevant nuance. The paper should acknowledge these possibilities rather than dismiss them.

I also have concerns about reproducibility and openness. The authors state they will not release the dataset and models publicly, only to researchers who adhere to ethical guidelines. I understand the sensitivity, but for a conference paper this limits reproducibility substantially. At minimum, the paper should specify what artifacts will be released, under what access process, and whether evaluation scripts, annotation guidelines, prompts, and model checkpoints can be shared in some controlled form. Since the work’s main contribution is partly a dataset, restricted access reduces community value unless the access mechanism is concrete.

There are several technical clarifications and ablations that would strengthen the paper. First, the identification model uses a hybrid approach for rare classes like name and contact, combining LUKE/Presidio with a sentence classifier. It is not clear how these outputs are integrated into the overall evaluation in Tables 2 and 3, especially since Name and Contact do not appear in the per-class table. Are these classes excluded from the main multi-class model evaluation? If so, the headline “19 categories” overstates what the learned model handles directly. Second, the paper should compare against simpler baselines, such as CRF/BiLSTM-CRF, dictionary/regex baselines for some categories, or prompting a modern instruction-tuned LLM for span extraction. Third, error analysis would be very helpful: where do boundary errors come from, which categories are confused, and how often does the model over-highlight due to contextualized span annotation?

The paper is generally well motivated, but the writing could be tightened. There are many grammatical issues and typos throughout (“broad the scope,” “fine-graiend,” “perform better or equally well,” “the loss are ignored,” etc.). Some tables are also hard to interpret due to formatting artifacts. The abstraction section in particular is more difficult to follow than necessary, partly because of many experimental variants and partly because the distinction between one-span and three-span settings is not always crisply explained. A careful proofreading pass would improve readability.

Overall, I view this as a promising and original paper with meaningful practical motivation, but also with several methodological gaps that prevent me from fully endorsing the strength of its claims in its current form. The identification dataset and task framing are the most solid parts. The user study adds valuable perspective, though it should be framed more modestly. The abstraction task is exciting, but the current evidence is preliminary due to synthetic references, small data, and limited human evaluation rigor.

My main suggestions for improvement are: report inter-annotator agreement for screening and span/category annotation; clarify exactly what text is annotated and how author identity is handled in threads; justify the taxonomy design and discuss alternative label structures; provide stronger baselines and clearer treatment of rare classes; include deeper error analysis for identification; substantially strengthen the abstraction evaluation with multiple human raters and, ideally, some human-authored references; temper claims about privacy protection to “specificity reduction” unless actual privacy-risk reduction is measured; and revise the ethics section to acknowledge potential disparate harms more realistically.

If the authors can address these issues, I think this line of work could become an important contribution at the intersection of NLP, privacy, and HCI. As it stands, the paper is interesting and potentially impactful, but some of its empirical and evaluative foundations are not yet strong enough for the broad claims it makes.
