Review ID: MV_010
Validation Track: RQ2
============================================================

This paper studies whether training dynamics can be used to automatically characterize NLI examples by difficulty and, in particular, to carve out a harder and less artifact-prone evaluation subset from existing benchmarks. The core idea is to compute per-example dynamics from two models trained on the target split itself, one using premise+hypothesis and one using hypothesis only, extract eight features (confidence, variability, correctness, and AUM in each setting), and cluster examples with a 3-component GMM into easy/ambiguous/hard groups. The paper argues that the hard split has lower hypothesis-only performance, weaker heuristic artifact signals, and substantially lower accuracy for standard NLI models. The same machinery is also applied to training-set filtering, where subsets selected by the proposed method are compared against Data Maps and AUM on Naik et al.’s stress tests.

I think the paper addresses an important and timely problem. NLI benchmark contamination by annotation artifacts and shallow heuristics is well established, and there is clear value in methods that can produce more realistic, challenging evaluations without expensive manual adversarial collection. The paper is also appealing in that it tries to avoid synthetic stress tests and instead reuses naturally occurring benchmark examples. The empirical trends are intuitive: the “hard” subsets are much smaller, much lower-performing, and much less solvable by hypothesis-only models. The training-set filtering results are also potentially useful.

That said, I have substantial concerns about the methodology and the strength of the claims. The biggest issue is conceptual: the proposed “test set characterization” requires training directly on the test set labels in order to obtain training dynamics. Even if the authors emphasize that this is only for characterization and not for final classifier evaluation, this is still a form of label access to the evaluation set. In a benchmark setting, this is a serious concern. The resulting hard split is not an independently defined test set; it is a partition induced by supervised fitting on the very labels later used to assess difficulty. This makes the procedure unsuitable as a standard evaluation protocol unless the split is fixed once and released publicly, and even then one should be careful about overfitting the benchmark construction process itself. More importantly, because the clustering features include correctness/confidence with respect to gold labels, the method is almost tautologically selecting examples that are hard for the chosen training procedure and model family. This weakens the claim that it measures intrinsic dataset difficulty or real-world robustness.

Relatedly, the paper repeatedly claims model independence or model agnosticism, but the evidence is not fully convincing. The characterization is derived from fine-tuned transformer classifiers and from their calibration-sensitive outputs. The cross-model transfer experiment with RoBERTa and DeBERTa is useful, but it is limited to two closely related pretrained encoder families with similar training paradigms. Moreover, Table 4 actually shows fairly large differences in accuracies across source characterizations, especially for ambiguous and hard MultiNLI, suggesting that the exact partition is not entirely stable. This does not invalidate the approach, but it does mean the “model-agnostic” claim should be softened to something like “partially transferable across similar encoder architectures.”

A second major concern is that the paper does not adequately disentangle “hard because artifact-reduced” from “hard because mislabeled, noisy, or distributionally odd.” The authors acknowledge that the method is not suitable for identifying mislabeled examples, yet several qualitative examples in SNLI easy appear mislabeled, and some hard examples may also simply be annotation-noisy or semantically underspecified. Since the clustering is driven by learnability signals, hard examples could include a mixture of genuinely reasoning-intensive cases, ambiguous cases, and annotation errors. The paper would be much stronger with a human validation study on a sample from each split, asking annotators to assess label correctness, ambiguity, and linguistic phenomena. Without that, the claim that the hard split is “more realistic” and provides a “more accurate measure of real-world performance” feels overstated.

The statistical analysis around spurious correlations is also weaker than the framing suggests. The heuristics used—word overlap, antonyms, length mismatch, misspellings, negation—are reasonable but quite shallow and incomplete proxies for annotation artifacts. Showing that these heuristics are less differentiated across labels in the hard split is suggestive, but not sufficient to conclude that spurious correlations have been minimized. In particular, many known NLI artifacts are lexical-semantic or discourse-level and would not be captured by these measures. The paper would benefit from stronger baselines such as artifact classifiers from prior work, partial-input baselines beyond hypothesis-only, or probing whether known annotation-artifact predictors lose power on the hard split.

There are also some methodological details that need clarification or improvement. First, the variability formula in Equation (2) appears incorrect as written: it seems to omit squaring the deviations from the mean. This may be a typesetting issue, but it should be fixed. Second, the choice of exactly three GMM clusters seems inherited from Data Maps rather than justified empirically. Why three? Did the authors compare BIC/AIC across different numbers of components? Since the resulting cluster sizes are highly imbalanced, it would be useful to know whether the data naturally support three modes or whether this is imposed. Third, the ranking of clusters by average P+H confidence is somewhat circular, since confidence is one of the clustering features and also the criterion used to define difficulty labels. Fourth, because the models are trained on the same split being characterized, the number of epochs, optimization settings, and random seeds may materially affect the partition. I did not see sufficient analysis of stability across seeds. A method like this should report cluster consistency and downstream metrics over multiple runs.

The training-set characterization section is interesting, but it also raises questions. The comparisons to Data Maps and AUM do not seem fully controlled. The proposed method uses richer features, including hypothesis-only dynamics, while the baselines are implemented with fairly simple percentile rules. That is a reasonable comparison to canonical usage, but it is not apples-to-apples in terms of information available. Also, the evaluation is only on Naik et al.’s stress tests, which the authors themselves describe as unrealistic and artifact-amplifying. If the claim is improved data quality and robustness, I would expect evaluation on standard validation/test sets, ANLI, HANS-like challenge sets, or cross-dataset transfer. The reported gains over full-data training are modest and somewhat inconsistent across subsets, so stronger evidence would help.

I also found the FEVER inclusion somewhat underdeveloped. It is presented as a fact-checking dataset repurposed for NLI, and the paper argues that because hypothesis-only performance is near chance, the hard split there reflects genuine difficulty rather than artifacts. That is plausible, but FEVER has a very different structure from SNLI/MultiNLI, including evidence retrieval assumptions and much longer evidence text. The paper notes this in passing when discussing length mismatch, but the broader implications for the method are not explored. It would help to explain whether the same notion of “difficulty” is really comparable across these datasets.

On the positive side, the empirical patterns are clear and the paper is generally well motivated. The drop from standard test accuracy to hard-split accuracy is substantial and likely meaningful. The use of both P+H and H-only dynamics is a nice idea, and intuitively well aligned with the goal of isolating artifact-driven examples. The qualitative examples are helpful, especially in illustrating that easy SNLI examples can include dubious contradiction labels and that easy MultiNLI examples often reduce to simple negation/paraphrase cues. The paper is also commendably transparent that the method is not for mislabeled-data detection and that calibration may matter.

For improvement, I would strongly encourage the authors to reframe the contribution more carefully. Rather than “constructing a challenging test set” in a benchmark sense, this is better described as a post hoc supervised characterization of an existing labeled evaluation set into subsets that are easier or harder for a given model family, with some evidence that the hardest subset is less driven by simple artifacts. That is still a useful contribution, but it is narrower and more defensible. I would also recommend adding the following analyses: a seed-stability study for the clustering; ablations showing the contribution of each feature group, especially the hypothesis-only features; comparison against simpler alternatives such as ranking by hypothesis-only confidence alone or by P+H confidence/AUM alone; model transfer beyond RoBERTa/DeBERTa if possible; and human annotation on a sample from each split to assess label quality and ambiguity. If the authors want to claim reduced spurious correlations, they should also compare against stronger artifact detectors from prior NLI bias work.

In terms of presentation, the paper is mostly readable, but there are several places where the claims are stronger than the evidence. Phrases like “more accurate measure of model performance in the real world,” “minimizes spurious correlations,” and “model-independent” should be toned down. Some tables are also difficult to interpret because the subset naming in the training-set experiments is not always intuitive, and the rationale for why, for example, Easy+Ambiguous or Ambiguous+Hard should be preferred is not deeply discussed. Finally, the fact that the method trains on the test set should be foregrounded much earlier and discussed more candidly as a limitation.

Overall, I see promise in the idea of using combined P+H and hypothesis-only training dynamics to characterize NLI examples, and I think the paper contains useful observations. However, in its current form, the central evaluation methodology is problematic for a benchmark paper, and several claims about realism, artifact removal, and model agnosticism are not yet sufficiently supported. With a more careful framing and stronger validation, this could become a solid contribution.

My recommendation is weak reject. The problem is important and the empirical idea is interesting, but the current paper overclaims relative to what the methodology can justify, and the reliance on supervised training over the test set labels is a significant conceptual limitation.

The main strengths are the relevance of the problem, the intuitive use of hypothesis-only dynamics, the clear empirical separation of easy versus hard subsets, and the potentially useful training-set filtering results. The main weaknesses are the use of test labels to define the evaluation split, limited evidence for model independence and true artifact reduction, insufficient validation against annotation noise/ambiguity, and a need for stronger baselines and stability analyses.
