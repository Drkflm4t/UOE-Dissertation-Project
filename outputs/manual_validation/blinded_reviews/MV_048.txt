Review ID: MV_048
Validation Track: RQ2
============================================================

This paper presents a broad empirical study of task-specific knowledge distillation for natural language generation under a practically motivated setting: medium-resource labeled data, abundant unlabeled task-specific inputs, off-the-shelf pretrained models, and a strong emphasis on inference efficiency. The central contribution is a systematic comparison of architectural and distillation choices, culminating in a pseudo-target training recipe that combines token-level KD with multiple pseudo-targets generated from labeled and unlabeled inputs, plus a proposed Joint-Teaching variant that mixes teacher- and student-generated pseudo-targets. The paper also includes an exploratory “extreme setup” using GPT-4 as teacher with no task-specific labeled data for teacher fine-tuning.

Overall, I found the paper interesting, practically relevant, and stronger on empirical breadth than on methodological novelty. Its main value is not a single sharply defined algorithmic breakthrough, but rather a careful distillation recipe backed by a reasonably extensive set of experiments across several NLG tasks and efficiency metrics. The paper addresses a real gap between much of the KD-for-NLG literature and realistic deployment settings, especially the use of unlabeled task-specific data and the explicit reporting of latency and throughput. I also appreciate that the authors do not oversell the GPT-4 setting and clearly position it as exploratory.

The strongest aspect of the paper is the practical framing and the systematic nature of the study. Many distillation papers evaluate one task, one architecture family, and one metric; this paper instead compares encoder-decoder versus decoder-only models, pruning strategies, several KD objectives, sequence-level augmentation variants, and a new Joint-Teaching idea across four tasks. The inclusion of both task metrics and deployment-oriented metrics such as latency and throughput is valuable and likely useful to practitioners. The finding that unlabeled data plus pseudo-targets substantially improves task-specific NLG distillation is important, even if intuitively plausible. Likewise, the observation that multiple sampled pseudo-targets outperform the standard single beam pseudo-target is a useful empirical contribution.

I also think the paper’s emphasis on exposure bias as a motivation for pseudo-target training is conceptually appealing. The argument that teacher-generated pseudo-targets expose the student to non-gold prefixes, and that student-generated prefixes combined with teacher token supervision may further reduce train-test mismatch, is sensible. Joint-Teaching is a natural extension of this idea and appears empirically beneficial in the reported setup.

That said, I have several concerns about the rigor of the experimental methodology and the strength of some of the claims.

First, the paper repeatedly uses language suggesting general conclusions or “rules of thumb,” but the experimental basis is still somewhat narrow. The study covers four English-to-English tasks with relatively short outputs, two encoder-decoder families, and only two teacher-student pairs in the main KD stages. This is enough to support a useful empirical recipe for similar settings, but not enough to justify some of the broader claims, especially around architecture choice and exposure bias. For example, the claim that encoder-decoder models outperform decoder-only models for task-specific NLG may well be true in these settings, but the comparison is not fully controlled. The ED and DO models differ not only in architecture but also in pretraining objectives, tokenization, data, and implementation details. The paper acknowledges some of this in the appendix, but the main text still presents the result rather strongly. I would encourage the authors to soften the claim or more explicitly frame it as an empirical observation for the chosen model families and tasks.

Second, the sequential stage-wise design is practical but introduces a substantial risk of path dependence. The paper chooses one option at each stage based on average dev performance and then proceeds, rather than evaluating a fuller factorial combination. This is understandable computationally, but it means later conclusions are conditional on earlier choices. For instance, the benefit of sampling-based pseudo-targets may depend on using Logits KD, on the chosen student-teacher pairs, or on the amount of unlabeled data. Similarly, Joint-Teaching is only compared after several earlier design decisions have already been fixed. As a result, the paper is best interpreted as proposing a good recipe discovered by greedy search, rather than isolating independent causal effects of each component. I think the paper would be stronger if it emphasized this limitation more clearly and, ideally, included a small number of interaction checks to show that the main conclusions are robust.

Third, the main summary metric in Table 3—the fraction of the student-teacher gap closed—is intuitive but can be misleading. Gap-closure percentages can exaggerate improvements when the teacher-student gap is small, and they are hard to compare across tasks and metrics with different scales and variances. The paper does provide raw metrics elsewhere, which helps, but the main narrative leans heavily on gap closure and “wins.” I would have preferred more emphasis on absolute improvements over the student baseline, confidence intervals or variance across runs, and perhaps normalized comparisons that are less sensitive to denominator effects. In particular, reporting only wins out of 32 setups without significance testing makes it difficult to assess robustness.

Relatedly, I did not see evidence of multiple random seeds or statistical significance analysis. Given that many of the reported differences in dev score are small, especially between KD objectives and decoding variants, variance matters. This is particularly important because the paper makes selection decisions stage by stage based on average dev performance. Without seed variance, it is hard to know whether some of these choices are stable. At minimum, the final recipe and a few key ablations should be repeated across several seeds.

Fourth, the novelty of some methodological components is moderate. Using pseudo-targets from unlabeled data, multiple pseudo-targets, and sampling-based pseudo-target generation are all natural extensions of sequence-level KD. The paper’s contribution is mainly to combine and systematically validate them in a realistic task-specific NLG setting. That is still worthwhile, but the paper should be careful not to overstate novelty. The most novel component is Joint-Teaching, which is interesting, but even here the mechanism is not fully dissected. Is the gain due to exposure-bias mitigation specifically, or simply due to additional data augmentation and harder-prefix training? A stronger analysis would compare against simpler alternatives such as scheduled mixing of teacher-forced and student-forced prefixes without full Joint-Teaching, or teacher KD on corrupted prefixes sampled from other distributions.

Fifth, the exposure bias argument is suggestive but not fully established. Figure 2 supports the claim that the teacher can better continue student prefixes than the student itself in one setup, but this is only a limited diagnostic. The paper repeatedly states that this is the “first exposure bias perspective for KD” and uses exposure bias as a central motivation, yet the experiments do not directly measure whether the gains correlate with reduced error accumulation or improved robustness to off-gold prefixes. It would strengthen the paper considerably to include targeted evaluations: for example, prefix perturbation tests, performance as a function of forced prefix corruption, or analyses of compounding errors during generation. As it stands, exposure bias is a plausible interpretation, but not conclusively demonstrated as the operative mechanism.

Sixth, the computational story is incomplete. The paper does a good job reporting inference-time efficiency, which is commendable, but the proposed recipe relies heavily on generating many pseudo-targets, including for unlabeled data, and in the Joint-Teaching case also student-generated pseudo-targets during training. The limitations section acknowledges that training cost is not considered, but this omission is important because one of the paper’s main practical claims is that the recipe is attractive in realistic settings. For many practitioners, teacher inference over 5x data with multiple pseudo-targets is a major cost. Even if the authors assume one-time training cost is secondary to deployment cost, reporting approximate wall-clock training time and teacher-generation cost for each stage would materially improve the paper’s practical value. This is especially true because some methods may offer similar final quality with much lower distillation cost.

Seventh, some dataset choices raise questions. The newly constructed Shake7 dataset may be useful, but because it is introduced by the authors, more scrutiny is needed regarding data construction, split quality, leakage prevention, and evaluation suitability. Since one of the headline findings is that the distilled model can outperform the teacher on Shake7, it is important to ensure this is not an artifact of dataset construction or metric mismatch. More generally, the paper relies heavily on lexical overlap metrics plus BERTScore and perplexity. For tasks like abductive reasoning and style transfer, these metrics are imperfect proxies for quality. The human evaluation on ART10 is a good step, but limited. More human evaluation, or at least task-specific learned metrics, would strengthen the conclusions.

Eighth, the GPT-4 “extreme setup” section is interesting but underdeveloped. It is understandable that cost limits the scope, but the section currently feels more like an appendix-level exploratory note than a fully integrated contribution. The setup differs from the main study in several confounding ways at once: no teacher fine-tuning, different architecture families, different tokenizers, limited data, and API constraints. As a result, the mixed findings are hard to interpret. I would not count this section heavily in favor of the paper, though I do appreciate the honesty in reporting that the conclusions only partially transfer.

There are also a few presentation issues. The writing is generally understandable, but there are many grammatical problems and awkward passive constructions throughout the paper. Some claims are phrased too strongly relative to the evidence. The paper would benefit from tighter editing, especially in the results section where the narrative sometimes overinterprets modest differences. I also found the notation and stage numbering somewhat cumbersome; the paper is readable, but the many stages and table subsections require effort to track.

Despite these concerns, I think the paper has meaningful strengths. The empirical results are substantial enough to support the practical takeaway that, in medium-resource task-specific NLG distillation, one should strongly consider encoder-decoder students, shallow-decoder pruning, combining token-level KD with pseudo-target training, leveraging unlabeled inputs, generating multiple pseudo-targets via sampling rather than only beam search, and possibly using Joint-Teaching. Even if some individual ingredients are not highly novel, the integrated study is useful and likely to influence practice.

To strengthen the paper, I would recommend the following. First, add variance estimates over multiple seeds for the main comparisons, especially the final recipe and the key stage transitions. Second, report absolute metric improvements more prominently alongside gap-closure percentages. Third, include at least a limited interaction analysis to test whether the main conclusions hold across both student-teacher pairs and perhaps one additional setting. Fourth, temper the broader claims about architecture superiority and exposure bias, or provide more direct evidence. Fifth, add training-cost statistics for pseudo-target generation and distillation. Sixth, provide more detail and validation for the Shake7 dataset and, if possible, expand human evaluation beyond ART10.

My overall assessment is positive but not unequivocal. I view this as a solid empirical systems-and-methods paper with practical relevance and a useful distillation recipe, but with moderate novelty and some methodological weaknesses around statistical rigor and claim strength. If accepted, it would likely be valuable to practitioners and researchers working on efficient NLG, especially those interested in realistic semi-supervised distillation settings.
