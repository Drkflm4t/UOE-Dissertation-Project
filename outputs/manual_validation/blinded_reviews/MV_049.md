# Review ID: MV_049

**Validation Track:** `RQ1`

---

This paper proposes CoT-Influx, a plug-and-play prompting module for improving mathematical reasoning in LLMs by pruning few-shot chain-of-thought context at two levels: first selecting useful CoT exemplars (“shot pruning”), then removing unimportant tokens within retained exemplars (“token pruning”). The method is trained with reinforcement learning using a custom reward that combines LLM loss, answer correctness, and a token-budget term, and it relies on a newly constructed GPT-4-based dataset, MRD3, containing math CoT examples with diverse difficulty levels and reasoning depths. Across LLaMA2-7B/13B/70B and five math benchmarks, the paper reports consistent gains over standard few-shot CoT, retrieval baselines, and prompt compression methods, with the strongest headline result being that LLaMA2-70B with CoT-Influx reaches 59.6 EM on GSM8K, surpassing several larger prior models listed in Table 4 on page 7.

Overall, I found the paper interesting, well motivated, and empirically fairly strong. The central intuition is compelling: context windows are not merely too short, but also poorly utilized because few-shot CoT prompts contain redundancy both at the example level and token level. The pilot study on page 3 does a good job motivating this claim. In particular, Figure 1 shows that increasing the number of retrieved CoT examples can help until context constraints interfere, while Table 1 shows that random addition of examples can hurt. The qualitative example in Figure 2 on page 3 is also useful in illustrating why generic prompt compression methods may fail for math reasoning: they often remove numerals, formatting markers, or structural cues that are disproportionately important in this domain. This is a sensible and practically relevant problem formulation.

The paper’s main strengths are threefold. First, the proposed coarse-to-fine design is intuitive and reasonably well aligned with the problem. Separating shot selection from token pruning is a natural decomposition, and the ablation in Table 7 on page 8 supports that both stages matter. Second, the empirical evaluation is broad enough to be convincing at a first pass: multiple LLaMA2 scales, five datasets, comparisons to prompting, retrieval, and compression baselines, plus compatibility with self-consistency and self-verification (Table 5, page 8). Third, the paper is unusually careful in discussing training challenges and stabilization tricks for RL, including difficulty-aware filtering, repeated sampling, EMA smoothing, and appending manual 8-shot CoTs during training. These details make the method feel more engineered and less purely conceptual.

That said, I also have several substantive concerns, mostly around methodological clarity, fairness of comparison, and the degree to which the gains should be attributed to the pruning method itself versus the surrounding pipeline.

My biggest concern is that the contribution is somewhat entangled with the newly created MRD3 dataset and with GPT-4-generated/evolved CoTs. The paper presents CoT-Influx as a pruning method, but in practice the full system includes a substantial data-generation component: GPT-4 reformats and evolves examples, assigns difficulty scores, and provides the basis for both training and inference retrieval pools. Table 6 on page 8 shows that MRD3 materially improves results over using the GSM8K training set or MRD3 without evolution. This is important, but it also means the reported gains are not cleanly attributable to pruning alone. In other words, the method is not just “better context selection/compression”; it is “better context selection/compression given a curated synthetic CoT bank.” That is still valuable, but the paper should be more explicit about this dependency and should better isolate the marginal contribution of each component. For example, I would have liked to see stronger baselines that use the same MRD3 pool but without RL pruning, or with simpler learned selectors, to quantify how much of the gain comes from the data versus the policy.

Relatedly, the retrieval baselines may not be fully competitive. The paper compares against Random, TopK, and BM25, which are reasonable but relatively basic. Since the paper argues that existing retrieval is suboptimal because it is model-agnostic and difficulty-insensitive, a stronger test would compare against more advanced supervised or iterative retrieval methods, or at least against retrieval augmented with difficulty matching using the same GPT-4 difficulty annotations. As written, the comparison risks overstating the novelty of the pruning policy because the baselines do not exploit some of the same side information that CoT-Influx uses. The appendix’s Table 15 on page 21 positions CoT-Influx favorably against many prior prompting strategies, but this is more of a conceptual comparison than an empirical one.

A second major concern is the RL formulation and its reproducibility. The paper states on page 5 that the shot and token pruners are two-layer MLPs operating over BERT embeddings, and that REINFORCE is used because pruning decisions are discrete. However, several details remain underspecified or somewhat confusing. The state definitions on page 5 are not entirely clear: the notation suggests states in R^{k x N}, while the hidden representations are in R^{k x N x D}; it is not obvious what exactly is fed into the MLP at each stage, especially for shot-level pruning where one might expect pooled sentence embeddings rather than tokenwise embeddings. The action sampling description also mixes sigmoid and categorical/softmax language in a way that is technically imprecise. Since each decision is binary, Bernoulli parameterization would be the natural description. These are not fatal issues, but they make the method harder to reproduce and evaluate rigorously.

The reward design also raises questions. Equation (5) combines inverse loss, exact-match correctness, and a token-budget factor. This is plausible, but somewhat ad hoc. In particular, the token term [t/T]^w is unusual, and the text says w<0 penalizes prompts that are too short while w>0 penalizes exceeding the limit. But if t>T and w>0, the reward factor becomes greater than 1, which seems opposite to a penalty unless there is some clipping or other handling not described. Perhaps the intended behavior is different, but as written the formulation is confusing. Since the reward is central to the method, this should be clarified carefully. The appendix ablation on page 13 suggests all reward components matter, especially the token-limit term, but the mathematical behavior still needs a cleaner explanation.

A third concern is fairness and consistency in the evaluation setup. The paper repeatedly emphasizes “no additional inference cost” because the final prompt fits within the original context window. But this is only partly true. At deployment, CoT-Influx still requires running BERT-Large and the pruner to process candidate examples before constructing the final prompt. Table 8 on page 8 does report this overhead and shows it is small, which is good, but the claim in the abstract and introduction could be phrased more carefully as “no additional target-LLM context extension cost” rather than “no additional inference overhead.” There is overhead, just not much.

Also, the comparisons to prompt compression baselines appear somewhat unfavorable to those baselines in setup. For example, Table 2 on page 7 shows dramatic collapses for Selective Context and LLMLingua, but the appendix case study on pages 14–15 suggests that CoT-Influx benefits from rule-based prompt reconstruction of structural tokens such as “Q:”, “A:”, “Let’s think step by step”, and “The answer is” (page 20), whereas it is unclear whether analogous reconstruction or prompt-format preservation was applied to the compression baselines. If not, then part of the advantage may come from this post-processing rather than from the learned pruning itself. Since preserving prompt structure is known to matter in ICL, the baselines should ideally be given the same reconstruction heuristic or a structure-preserving variant.

There is also a subtle issue in the training/inference pipeline: Algorithm 1 on page 5 indicates that the final input includes both the pruned CoTs and manual few-shot CoTs x_few-shot. This means CoT-Influx is not replacing the standard 8-shot prompt but augmenting it. The paper does mention appending manually designed 8-shot CoTs during training for stability (page 6), but it is less clear whether this is always present at inference across all experiments. If yes, then the effective comparison is not simply “CoT-Influx versus few-shot CoT,” but “few-shot CoT plus pruned retrieved/evolved CoTs versus few-shot CoT alone.” That is still a valid setting, but it should be stated prominently because it affects interpretation of the gains and the token accounting.

On the empirical side, the gains are real but modest in several places, especially on GSM8K for larger models. For example, Table 2 shows 54.59 to 59.59 on LLaMA2-70B, which is meaningful but not transformative. The strongest relative gains are on smaller models and against weaker baselines. This is not a criticism per se, but the paper’s rhetoric occasionally overstates the extent to which it “pushes the boundary” of few-shot reasoning. A more measured framing would help. In addition, the paper would benefit from statistical reporting: multiple seeds, confidence intervals, or at least variance estimates for the RL-trained pruner. Given the stochasticity of both RL and generation, single-number EM results are less convincing than they could be.

I also have some concerns about possible contamination or benchmark leakage, though the paper does not provide enough information to assess this fully. MRD3 is built partly from GSM8K training data and other math datasets, then evolved with GPT-4. Evaluation includes GSM8K test and several other benchmarks. This is probably acceptable, but because the retrieval pool is synthetic and derived from benchmark-adjacent sources, the paper should discuss whether evolved examples may become near paraphrases of evaluation items or encode benchmark-specific patterns. The appendix evolution examples on pages 16–18 show that some mutations are fairly shallow. A more careful deduplication or similarity analysis against test sets would strengthen confidence.

Another limitation is generality. The method is evaluated only on math word problems. The paper argues that numerical and formatting tokens are especially important in math, which is exactly why generic compression fails. But this also means it is unclear whether CoT-Influx is a broadly useful prompting method or a domain-specific engineering solution for arithmetic reasoning. The title and framing are somewhat broad (“Boosting LLM Reasoning”), while the evidence is entirely mathematical reasoning. I would encourage the authors either to narrow the claims or to add at least one non-math reasoning domain.

Despite these concerns, I think the paper has several notable positive aspects beyond the main results. The appendix is informative and adds useful analyses. The comparison with context-window extension on page 12 is interesting, even if limited to one setup; it suggests that simply extending context may not preserve reasoning quality. The sensitivity analysis on page 13 and training dynamics on page 19 are helpful. The qualitative compression examples on pages 14–15 are particularly effective in showing why structure-aware pruning matters. I also appreciated the observation on pages 16–17 that different model sizes prefer examples of different difficulty levels; this is a plausible and potentially important insight for future work, though here it remains more observational than deeply exploited.

For improvement, I would strongly recommend the following. First, disentangle the contributions more cleanly. Add experiments that compare: (a) MRD3 + simple TopK under the same token budget; (b) MRD3 + shot-only learned selector; (c) MRD3 + structure-preserving heuristic token pruning; and ideally (d) a supervised selector trained without RL. Second, clarify the exact inference prompt composition and whether manual 8-shot CoTs are always appended. Third, revise the reward equation and explain the token-budget term unambiguously. Fourth, strengthen baseline fairness by applying the same prompt reconstruction heuristic to compression baselines, or by comparing against structure-aware variants. Fifth, report variance across runs or seeds. Sixth, discuss possible train/test overlap or synthetic-data leakage more explicitly. Finally, tone down the broad claims unless additional non-math evaluations are added.

In summary, this is a promising and practically relevant paper with a good core idea, solid motivation, and encouraging empirical results. Its main novelty lies in combining example selection and token pruning for few-shot CoT under a learned RL policy, supported by a synthetic difficulty-diverse CoT bank. However, the current presentation does not fully isolate the source of gains, some methodological details are unclear, and several comparisons could be made fairer and stronger. I view it as a worthwhile contribution, but one that would benefit from tighter experimental controls and clearer exposition before being fully convincing at top-tier conference standards.
