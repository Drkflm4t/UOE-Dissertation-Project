Review ID: MV_047
Validation Track: RQ2
============================================================

This paper studies reward overoptimization in offline RLHF and proposes a theoretically motivated objective whose practical instantiation is simply DPO plus an SFT term, termed RPO. The central claim is appealing: the added SFT loss can be interpreted as an implicit adversarial regularizer arising from a maximin formulation over reward models, and this regularization provably mitigates overoptimization under partial coverage assumptions. The paper combines a nontrivial theoretical development with a practical algorithm and empirical results on 7B-scale chat models. Overall, I found the paper interesting and potentially impactful, especially because it connects a widely used heuristic in practice—mixing preference optimization with supervised imitation—to a principled pessimistic/adversarial view of offline RLHF. At the same time, there are several important issues in clarity, rigor of the theory-to-practice bridge, and empirical validation that prevent me from viewing the current version as fully convincing.

The main strengths are threefold. First, the paper addresses an important and timely problem. Overoptimization against imperfect preference models is a central concern in RLHF, and understanding why adding SFT-like regularization helps is valuable. Second, the theoretical framing is novel and conceptually elegant. The maximin objective in which the adversary minimizes an MLE loss plus a reward penalty is a clean way to encode pessimism under uncertainty, and the resulting interpretation of SFT as arising from the baseline term is insightful. Third, the practical objective is simple and useful. If the derivation is sound, then the message “DPO + SFT is not just a heuristic but an adversarially justified regularizer” is likely to resonate with both theory and practice communities.

That said, my overall assessment is mixed because the paper overstates some claims relative to what is actually proved and empirically demonstrated. The theory is for an offline contextual bandit with general reward function classes under Bradley-Terry preferences and exact optimization over policy/reward classes. The practical algorithm is gradient-based fine-tuning of large neural language models with a specific choice of baseline distribution and approximate optimization. The paper presents this bridge as quite direct, but in reality it depends on several assumptions and approximations that deserve much more explicit discussion.

On the theoretical side, the maximin objective in Section 3 is interesting, and Theorem 5.3 gives a finite-sample suboptimality bound under a partial coverage coefficient. This is a meaningful result. However, the exact novelty relative to prior pessimistic offline preference learning work is somewhat blurred. The paper emphasizes “general function approximation,” but the complexity measure is still a covering number of the reward class, and the assumptions needed for the minimax equivalence later are fairly strong. In particular, Assumption 5.5 requires compactness and a convex-like property of the reward class with respect to the objective. This is mathematically reasonable in abstract function spaces, but it is far from obvious for the neural parameterizations implicitly used in practice. Since the practical contribution hinges on Theorem 5.6 and the equivalence between maximin and minimax, the paper should be much more careful in stating that the practical RPO objective is theoretically justified only insofar as the policy class can be viewed as induced by a reward class satisfying these regularity conditions. As written, the paper risks giving the impression that the theorem directly covers neural LLM fine-tuning, which it does not.

Relatedly, the reparameterization argument from reward to policy is standard in DPO-style derivations, but here it is used in a more delicate minimax setting. The paper claims that solving the minimax objective via the policy parameterization yields a policy that also solves the original maximin target. This is plausible under the stated assumptions, but the practical optimization in Equation (4.8) is over an arbitrary parameterized policy class, not necessarily the exact image of the reward class under the Gibbs map induced by the reference policy. This representational mismatch is important. If the policy class used in practice is larger or different from the image of the admissible reward class, then Equation (4.8) is no longer obviously equivalent to the theoretical minimax problem. The paper should either formalize this point or clearly state that RPO is inspired by, rather than exactly equivalent to, the theoretical objective in realistic neural settings.

I also found some notational and conceptual inconsistencies that make the theory harder to trust than it should be. In Section 3 the baseline policy is denoted by pi^base, but in Section 5.2 the definition of phi in Equation (5.4) appears to use pi^ref in the a^0 expectation rather than pi^base. If this is intentional, it needs explanation; if not, it is a serious typo because the baseline distribution is central to the interpretation of the SFT term. There are several similar presentation issues: some equations appear malformed, there are typographical errors in parameter definitions, and the prose occasionally confuses “reference” and “baseline” roles. For a theory-heavy paper, these issues matter. I would strongly encourage the authors to carefully proofread the mathematical statements and ensure all symbols are consistent.

The interpretation of DPO’s beta parameter in Section 4 also seems somewhat overstated. The paper says beta in DPO “only changes the scale of the gradient instead of the direction.” Per-example this is true for the multiplicative weight in the displayed gradient, but globally beta also changes the reward-to-policy mapping and therefore the optimization landscape and relative weighting across examples. So the contrast between “DPO only rescales” and “RPO changes direction” is directionally useful but too simplistic as a conceptual claim. I would suggest softening this discussion.

The sample complexity result itself is potentially useful, but its practical implications are limited by the dependence on the unknown coverage coefficient and the KL term to the reference policy in the bound. The theorem competes with any policy pi satisfying partial coverage, which is standard and reasonable, but the paper sometimes phrases the result more broadly as “provably mitigating overoptimization” without emphasizing that the guarantee is relative to covered comparator policies. This distinction is important: the method does not solve the fundamental extrapolation problem outside covered regions; it regularizes toward a baseline so that the learned policy stays in better-covered regions. That is a good contribution, but the claims should reflect it more precisely.

On the empirical side, the results are promising but not yet strong enough for the breadth of the claims. The practical algorithm is essentially DPO plus an SFT loss on chosen responses, with eta tuned on a held-out split. This is a sensible baseline, but the empirical section needs stronger comparisons and ablations. In particular, prior work such as ORPO and related methods already combine preference optimization with supervised or odds-ratio style regularization. The paper acknowledges similar algorithmic components in prior work, but the experiments do not compare against these methods. Since the practical novelty is modest absent the theory, comparisons to strong existing “DPO + auxiliary regularization” baselines are essential.

More importantly, the paper should disentangle whether the gains come from the specific adversarial interpretation or simply from adding extra supervised signal. At minimum, I would want to see ablations over the baseline distribution pi^base, the weight eta, and perhaps alternative regularizers such as KL-to-reference, chosen-only SFT, rejected-response penalties, or random-response imitation. The theory suggests that the choice of baseline matters through coverage; the experiments currently fix it to chosen responses and do not test this claim. Without such ablations, the empirical evidence supports “adding chosen-response SFT helps a bit” more than it supports the stronger theoretical narrative.

The evaluation methodology also has limitations. The in-distribution evaluation uses only 150 GPT-4-annotated examples after filtering malformed judgments. This is a small sample, and no confidence intervals or significance tests are reported. The benchmark gains on MT-Bench and AlpacaEval 2.0 are positive but modest in some cases, especially relative to DPO. Given the variance of LLM-as-a-judge evaluations, statistical uncertainty should be reported. It would also help to include human evaluation or at least a second judge model, especially because the paper is about alignment and overoptimization, where automatic judges can be brittle and biased. The paper cites concerns about LLM evaluators in the references but does not really address them in the experimental design.

I was also not convinced by the PCA-based argument for prompt-distribution coverage in Section 5.3. A 2D PCA visualization of prompt embeddings is at best a loose qualitative sanity check; it does not meaningfully validate the bounded density ratio assumption in Corollary 5.8. Presenting it as evidence that the assumption “approximately holds” is too strong. This section should be reframed as intuition only, not validation of the theorem’s assumptions.

Another empirical concern is that the paper’s main narrative about overoptimization is tied to the decrease in log-probability of chosen responses during DPO training. This phenomenon has been observed before, but it is not by itself a definitive measure of harmful overoptimization. A model could reduce chosen-response likelihood while still improving pairwise preference performance if it reallocates probability mass appropriately. Since the paper’s theory is about true reward under coverage, it would be better to include more direct diagnostics: reward-model overoptimization curves, calibration of preference margins, performance as a function of training steps, or robustness under noisy/out-of-distribution preference pairs. Right now the evidence for “mitigating overoptimization” is somewhat indirect.

Despite these criticisms, I do think the paper contains a valuable idea. The theoretical derivation gives a principled lens on why supervised imitation can stabilize offline preference optimization, and the resulting objective is simple enough to matter in practice. If the authors tighten the claims, fix the presentation issues, and strengthen the empirical section, this could become a strong paper.

I have several concrete suggestions for improvement. First, clarify the exact scope of the theory-to-practice connection. Explicitly distinguish the abstract reward/policy classes in the theorems from neural parameterizations used in experiments, and discuss representational mismatch in Equation (4.8). Second, fix notation and consistency issues, especially the role of pi^base versus pi^ref in Section 5.2 and any malformed equations. Third, add stronger empirical baselines, especially methods that already mix preference optimization with supervised regularization. Fourth, include ablations on eta and on the baseline distribution used for the SFT term; this is central to the paper’s theoretical story. Fifth, report uncertainty estimates for all empirical comparisons and, if possible, add human evaluation or at least multiple judge models. Sixth, tone down the PCA coverage discussion and present it as qualitative intuition rather than evidence for the theorem’s assumptions.

A few smaller comments. The paper would benefit from a clearer discussion of how eta should scale with data size in practice, since the theorem suggests eta on the order of 1/sqrt(N) while experiments use a fixed tuned value. It would also help to explain whether the SFT term is token-level teacher forcing on chosen responses or sequence-level log-probability, and whether length normalization is used. Finally, the writing has many grammatical and typographical errors; while not fatal, they do hurt readability and confidence in the formal statements.

In summary, I view this as a good paper with an interesting core idea, but one that currently falls short of fully substantiating its strongest claims. The theoretical contribution is the main asset; the practical algorithm is simple and plausible, but the empirical evidence is not yet comprehensive enough to establish that the proposed adversarial interpretation is the right explanation for the gains. My recommendation would depend on venue standards, but for a top-tier ML conference I would lean borderline positive if the theory is judged the primary contribution, and borderline negative if strong empirical validation is expected. The paper is certainly worth serious consideration, but it needs revision to reach its full potential.
