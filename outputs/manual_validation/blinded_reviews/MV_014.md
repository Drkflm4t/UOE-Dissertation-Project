# Review ID: MV_014

**Validation Track:** `RQ1`

---

This paper studies reward overoptimization in RLHF and proposes a theoretically motivated objective that leads to a practical algorithm, Regularized Preference Optimization (RPO), which augments DPO with an imitation/SFT term. The central claim is appealing and important: the SFT loss can be interpreted as an implicit adversarial regularizer that mitigates overoptimization caused by reward uncertainty and distribution shift. The paper combines a maximin formulation, an equivalence argument to a minimax/practical objective, finite-sample guarantees under a partial coverage condition, and empirical results on LLM alignment benchmarks. Overall, I found the paper interesting, timely, and potentially impactful. The main conceptual message—that a simple SFT term can be justified as a principled regularizer against overoptimization—is valuable. At the same time, there are several places where the theory-to-practice bridge is not yet fully convincing, some assumptions and derivations need clarification, and the empirical evidence, while promising, is still somewhat limited relative to the strength of the claims.

The strongest aspect of the paper is the unifying perspective it offers. The formulation in Sections 3 and 4 is elegant: starting from a pessimistic/adversarial reward estimation objective and arriving at a practical loss that is simply “DPO + SFT” is a neat result. The intuition is well conveyed, especially through Figure 1 on page 3, which illustrates how DPO can assign probability mass to undesirable but weakly constrained responses, while RPO’s extra log-likelihood term pushes mass back toward desirable/chosen responses. This is a compelling story, and the paper does a good job motivating why standard KL regularization to the reference model may be insufficient: as argued in Section 4, the DPO temperature/scaling parameter mainly rescales gradients per example, whereas the added imitation term can alter the gradient direction. That distinction is useful and, if made fully rigorous, could be an important contribution.

The theoretical contribution is also nontrivial. The paper proposes a maximin objective over policies and reward models in a general function approximation setting, rather than restricting to linear rewards. The finite-sample guarantee in Theorem 5.3 gives a suboptimality bound scaling roughly as a coverage-dependent term times 1/sqrt(N), plus a KL-to-reference term. This is a meaningful result in the offline RLHF setting, where partial coverage is the right lens. I also appreciate that the paper explicitly discusses the role of the baseline policy and ties it to identifiability under Bradley-Terry preferences. The equivalence result in Section 5.2, connecting the maximin objective to the minimax objective and then to the practical RPO loss, is the key theoretical bridge that makes the paper more than just “another DPO variant with SFT.”

That said, I have several concerns about the theory. First, the practical algorithm optimizes over a parameterized policy class, but the formal guarantees are stated for the idealized optimization problems over policy/reward classes, not for the actual nonconvex neural optimization used in experiments. This gap is common, but here it matters because the paper’s main selling point is the theory-to-practice equivalence. Theorem 5.6 shows that if one solves the minimax problem exactly, then the induced policy solves the maximin problem. But the practical RPO implementation in Algorithm 2 does not solve the minimax problem over reward models; it directly optimizes a parameterized policy objective. The paper informally motivates this via the reward-policy reparameterization, but the exact conditions under which the parameterized policy class corresponds to the image of the reward class are not spelled out. In practice, the optimization landscape and parameterization constraints may break the equivalence. The paper should be much more explicit about what is actually guaranteed for the implemented algorithm versus the idealized objective.

Second, Assumption 5.5 is doing substantial work and feels under-discussed. The “convex-like” condition on the reward class is fairly abstract, and while the paper notes that convex reward classes satisfy it, the practical relevance to neural reward classes or implicit policy parameterizations is unclear. Since the paper emphasizes general function approximation, it would help to characterize concrete nontrivial classes where the assumption holds and where the equivalence theorem is meaningful. As written, the result is mathematically valid but may be narrower in applicability than the presentation suggests.

Third, there is some notational and conceptual slippage around the baseline policy. In Section 3, the baseline is introduced as necessary because Bradley-Terry only identifies reward differences. In theory, this baseline should be chosen to satisfy coverage properties. In practice, the paper uses chosen responses from the preference dataset, which is reasonable and supported by Remark 5.4 and Appendix A. However, the practical objective in Equation (4.5) becomes an expectation over prompts from d0 and actions from pi_base. In implementation, this seems to reduce to standard SFT on chosen responses from the dataset, but the exact correspondence between the theoretical expectation and the empirical loss is not fully spelled out. Since this baseline choice is central to the method, the paper should clarify whether the practical estimator is unbiased for the theoretical term, and how prompt-distribution mismatch is handled.

Fourth, the generalization argument in Section 5.3 is fairly weak relative to the empirical claims. Corollary 5.8 assumes bounded density ratio between the new prompt distribution and the training prompt distribution. The paper then uses PCA plots of prompt embeddings on page 12 to argue that this “approximately holds” for MT-Bench and AlpacaEval. This is not a convincing validation of a density-ratio assumption. PCA overlap in two dimensions is at best a rough visualization and does not establish coverage or bounded density ratio in the original space. I would strongly recommend toning down the theoretical justification for benchmark generalization, or replacing it with a more careful empirical analysis of prompt-distribution similarity.

On the empirical side, the results are encouraging but not yet fully persuasive. The paper evaluates on two 7B model families, beta and gemma, using training setups modeled after Zephyr variants. The reported gains over DPO are consistent but modest on standard benchmarks. For example, Table 2 on page 14 shows MT-Bench improvements from 7.278 to 7.381 for beta and from 7.688 to 7.916 for gemma, and AlpacaEval 2.0 gains that are similarly positive but not dramatic. These are good signs, but given the variability of LLM evaluation pipelines, especially with GPT-4-based judging, it would be important to report variance across seeds or repeated runs. The paper uses a single seed (42) according to Table 3 on page 29. Without multiple runs, it is hard to know whether the gains are robust.

Relatedly, the in-distribution evaluation on 150 GPT-4-annotated examples is too small to support strong conclusions. Table 1 on page 14 reports pairwise win rates, but no confidence intervals or significance tests are provided. Since the margins over DPO are not huge, statistical uncertainty matters. The same issue applies to the benchmark comparisons. I would like to see either bootstrap confidence intervals over prompts or repeated training/evaluation runs.

A more substantive empirical concern is that the paper does not sufficiently disentangle whether the gains come from the specific adversarial-regularization interpretation or simply from adding extra supervised signal. In other words, is RPO better because the chosen-response SFT term is theoretically special, or because any auxiliary likelihood regularizer would help stabilize DPO? The paper argues for the former, but the experiments do not test it. Stronger ablations are needed: varying the baseline distribution, comparing chosen-response SFT versus reference-model imitation versus rejected-response penalties, and matching total optimization budget/token exposure. Since the core claim is that “your SFT loss is implicitly an adversarial regularizer,” the paper should empirically validate that the particular SFT term predicted by the theory matters.

The comparison set is also somewhat narrow. The related work section acknowledges methods such as ORPO, DPO-positive/Smaug, and other preference optimization variants that incorporate SFT-like or monolithic regularization. Yet the experiments compare primarily against vanilla DPO and the released Zephyr models. Since some prior methods already combine preference optimization with supervised terms, the novelty claim would be much stronger if the paper compared directly against those baselines. Otherwise, it is difficult to assess whether the contribution is mainly theoretical reinterpretation or whether RPO is empirically superior to the closest alternatives.

There are also a few technical presentation issues that should be fixed. In Section 5.2, Equation (5.2) appears to define phi using pi_ref in the expectation over a0, whereas earlier sections use pi_base; this looks like either a typo or an inconsistency and should be corrected. On page 9, the notation for r_theta is written as beta · log(pi_theta(a|x))/log(pi_ref(a|x)), which seems dimensionally wrong; presumably it should be beta times the log-ratio, i.e., beta (log pi_theta(a|x) - log pi_ref(a|x)). There are several minor typographical issues throughout, but this one affects the mathematical meaning. I also noticed some inconsistencies in table labels in Appendix E.3 on page 30, where the headers for gemma tables still say “RPO (beta)” / “Ref. (beta)” / “DPO (beta).” These are small but should be cleaned up.

Another point that deserves more discussion is the choice of hyperparameter eta. In practice, eta is selected on a held-out test split of the training dataset according to Section 6 and Appendix E.1. Since eta controls the strength of the imitation regularizer, the method’s effectiveness may depend heavily on tuning. The paper should provide a sensitivity analysis over eta and beta, ideally showing whether RPO is robust across a reasonable range. This is especially important because one of the claimed advantages is practical simplicity. If the gains require careful tuning of eta per setup, that should be stated clearly.

I did appreciate the additional experiments in Appendix F on GSM8K, ARC, and MBPP. These results suggest that the regularizer does not obviously hurt reasoning/coding performance and may even help slightly. Still, these experiments use a modified variant of RPO implemented in a different codebase with a token-normalized SFT term and different beta, which makes them less directly comparable to the main results. They are useful as supplementary evidence but should not be overinterpreted.

In terms of significance, I think the paper has real promise. The idea that a simple SFT term can emerge from a pessimistic/adversarial treatment of reward uncertainty is conceptually strong and likely to interest both the RLHF theory and practice communities. The paper is also commendable for trying to connect offline RLHF theory to implementation-friendly objectives, which many theoretical works fail to do. If the authors can tighten the theory/practice correspondence, strengthen the empirical validation with better ablations and stronger baselines, and clean up the presentation, this could become a strong contribution.

My main suggestions for improvement are as follows. First, clearly separate the guarantees for the idealized maximin/minimax objectives from what is actually optimized in neural RPO, and state the additional assumptions needed for the practical parameterized policy version. Second, expand the discussion of Assumption 5.5 and give concrete examples of reward classes where it holds in a meaningful way. Third, add ablations on the baseline distribution and the form of the SFT regularizer to test the central theoretical claim. Fourth, compare against the closest practical baselines that also mix preference optimization with supervised terms. Fifth, report uncertainty estimates and, ideally, multiple seeds. Sixth, fix the notation/typos and improve consistency in the appendices.

Overall, my assessment is positive but somewhat tempered. The paper contains a compelling idea, a nice theoretical framing, and promising empirical evidence, but it currently overstates the strength of the theory-to-practice bridge and under-supports some of its empirical claims. With revisions, I could see this being a valuable paper. In its current form, I would lean borderline accept at a venue that values conceptual novelty and theory-practice integration, but I think it still needs stronger empirical rigor and sharper exposition to be fully convincing.
