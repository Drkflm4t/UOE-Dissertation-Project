Review ID: MV_002
Validation Track: RQ2
============================================================

This paper tackles an important and practically relevant problem: task-specific compression of NLG models under medium-resource supervision with additional unlabeled inputs. The paper’s main contribution is not a single narrowly scoped algorithm, but rather a broad empirical study of distillation design choices for conditional generation, culminating in a pseudo-target training recipe and the proposed Joint-Teaching variant. I found the paper ambitious, generally well motivated, and unusually attentive to deployment-oriented considerations such as latency and throughput rather than only parameter count. The focus on realistic settings with limited labeled data and abundant unlabeled inputs is also valuable and underexplored in the NLG distillation literature.

The strongest aspect of the paper is the breadth and practical orientation of the empirical study. The authors compare encoder-decoder versus decoder-only backbones, pruning strategies, several KD objectives, and multiple pseudo-target generation schemes across four English-to-English generation tasks. The resulting conclusions are concrete and useful: encoder-decoder models are preferable for task-specific conditional NLG in this regime; decoder pruning is more favorable than encoder pruning when latency matters; and pseudo-targets become substantially more effective when extended beyond the standard single beam-search target to multiple sampled targets, especially when unlabeled inputs are available. These are actionable findings. The final compression results are also compelling from a systems perspective, with substantial latency and throughput gains while recovering a large fraction of teacher performance.

I also think the paper’s emphasis on unlabeled task-specific inputs is a meaningful contribution. In many real deployments, unlabeled inputs are indeed plentiful, and the paper demonstrates convincingly that generating pseudo-targets for such inputs can materially improve student quality. This is one of the most practically significant findings in the paper, and it helps distinguish the work from prior sequence-level KD studies that mostly assume fully labeled, often high-resource settings.

The proposed Joint-Teaching idea is interesting as well. The intuition—using teacher supervision on both teacher-generated and student-generated prefixes to address exposure bias more directly—is sensible, and the empirical results suggest it is beneficial on average. The paper’s attempt to connect pseudo-target training to exposure bias is also thought-provoking and gives a coherent narrative to the method family.

That said, I have several concerns about the paper’s methodology and the strength of some of its claims.

My main concern is that the paper presents itself as a “systematic study,” but the experimental design is fundamentally sequential and greedy rather than factorial. At each stage, one choice is fixed based on average dev performance and then subsequent choices are explored conditioned on that earlier decision. The authors acknowledge this, but it has important implications: interactions between choices are not measured, and some conclusions may be artifacts of the chosen path. For example, the superiority of sampling over beam-generated PTs may depend on the objective, the student architecture, or whether unlabeled data is used; similarly, the value of Joint-Teaching may depend on the PT decoding strategy or on whether the student is pruned. Because the study does not evaluate these interactions systematically, several conclusions should be framed more cautiously as conditional recommendations rather than general findings.

Relatedly, the aggregation and reporting strategy in Table 3 is somewhat problematic. The primary summary statistic is the fraction of the student-teacher gap closed, averaged over metrics and over two teacher-student pairs. This quantity can be informative, but it can also be unstable or misleading when teacher-student gaps differ substantially across tasks and metrics, or when the teacher itself is not especially strong. Averaging gap-closure percentages across heterogeneous metrics such as BLEU, ROUGE, BERTScore, and perplexity is especially hard to interpret. A method can appear strong by improving metrics with small absolute gaps or by benefiting from favorable scaling properties of the ratio. The paper does provide more detailed appendix tables, which helps, but the main claims rely heavily on these aggregated percentages and “wins,” without significance testing or variance estimates. Given the scale of the empirical claims, I would have liked confidence intervals, multiple random seeds, or at least paired significance tests on the main comparisons.

The lack of variance reporting is a notable weakness throughout. Distillation and fine-tuning outcomes can vary materially with initialization, learning rate, and decoding randomness, especially in medium-resource settings. The paper appears to select learning rates from fine-tuning experiments and then reuse them for KD for computational reasons, which may bias against some KD methods. Moreover, many of the reported improvements in dev score are fairly small in absolute terms. Without seed variance or statistical testing, it is difficult to know which differences are robust. This is particularly important because the paper makes several comparative claims among closely performing methods, such as Logits KD versus Noisy KD versus Attention-Relations KD, or sampling versus high-temperature sampling.

I also found the exposure-bias framing somewhat overstated relative to the evidence provided. The intuition that PTs may reduce the mismatch between training and inference is plausible, and Joint-Teaching is a reasonable attempt to exploit this. However, the paper repeatedly suggests that this is the first exposure-bias perspective for KD and uses it as a central explanatory mechanism, while the empirical evidence is limited. Figure 2 is based on a single setup and measures a hybrid continuation procedure rather than directly quantifying exposure bias reduction. The observed gains from multiple sampled PTs could equally be explained by data augmentation, target smoothing, or broader coverage of acceptable outputs, without requiring the exposure-bias story. Likewise, Joint-Teaching may help for reasons other than explicit exposure-bias mitigation, such as regularization or better alignment to student-induced prefix distributions. I would encourage the authors to tone down causal claims here unless they can provide stronger ablations or diagnostics.

A related issue is that the paper sometimes conflates several factors in the PT experiments. For example, moving from a single beam PT to multiple sampled PTs changes at least three things at once: number of targets per input, diversity of targets, and decoding distribution. The staged design partially disentangles these, but not fully, especially because the training protocol uses one PT per epoch rather than all PTs jointly. This choice is understandable computationally, but it means the effective training distribution differs from a more standard multi-target objective. It would help to clarify whether the gains come primarily from diversity, from more total synthetic supervision, or from the specific curriculum induced by rotating PTs across epochs.

The architecture comparison between encoder-decoder and decoder-only models is useful, but I am not fully convinced by the strength of the conclusion as stated. The paper compares off-the-shelf GPT2/OPT models against T5/BART families under task-specific fine-tuning and finds encoder-decoder models superior. This is plausible and likely true in this regime, but the comparison is not perfectly controlled: the model families differ in pretraining data, objectives, tokenization, and optimization behavior, not just architecture. The appendix discusses this reasonably, but the main text sometimes reads as if the result isolates architecture. I would suggest softening the claim to “in these off-the-shelf families and this fine-tuning regime, encoder-decoder models perform better.”

The pruning study is practical and interesting, but also somewhat narrow. Only one pruning scheme is considered, only on BART-base, and only by dropping layers while keeping first and last layers. This is enough to support a useful heuristic, but not enough to justify a broad statement that decoder pruning is generally better than encoder pruning. The result likely depends on input/output length ratios, architecture details, and pruning strategy. The paper does acknowledge some of this in the appendix, but again the main claim could be more carefully scoped.

The extreme GPT-4 setup is intriguing but underdeveloped. I appreciate that the authors include it as an exploratory extension rather than the main contribution. Still, the methodology there raises concerns. The token alignment procedure for logits KD across different tokenizers appears quite heuristic, and one sentence seems internally inconsistent: when a student token does not match the teacher’s top-5 tokens, the probability is set to one. As written, this seems wrong or at least poorly explained, since assigning probability one to unmatched tokens would distort the target distribution. Perhaps the authors mean a default loss treatment or a degenerate one-hot assignment, but this needs clarification. More broadly, because only top-5 teacher probabilities are available and the dev/test mismatch is severe, the conclusions from this section are understandably mixed. I do not view this as a major flaw since it is not central, but the section currently feels more like a preliminary note than a validated extension.

There are also some evaluation concerns at the task level. For ART10, the authors correctly note that automatic metrics are weak, and they include a small human evaluation. That is a positive step. However, the human study is limited to 50 examples and two annotators, and the reported Kendall tau of 0.52 is moderate. It supports the claim that automatic metrics may underestimate performance, but it is not strong enough to fully rehabilitate the task-level conclusions. More generally, for generation tasks like style transfer and abductive reasoning, lexical overlap metrics can be brittle, so the paper’s reliance on BLEU/ROUGE averages in the main summary should be interpreted cautiously.

I also think the paper could do a better job accounting for training-time cost. The authors explicitly deprioritize one-time training cost in favor of inference efficiency, which is a defensible deployment assumption. Still, some of the proposed methods—especially generating 48 PTs per example and Joint-Teaching with on-the-fly student PTs—may be substantially more expensive than simpler baselines. Since one of the paper’s selling points is practical guidance, even rough wall-clock comparisons or teacher-query budgets would be valuable. Without them, it is hard to assess the true cost-benefit tradeoff of the more elaborate PT schemes.

On the positive side, the paper is generally clear, and the appendices contain substantial detail. The inclusion of latency and throughput measurements on actual hardware is commendable. The new Shake7 dataset may also be useful, though I would like more discussion of its construction quality, deduplication, and potential overlap among sources. Since it is partly assembled from multiple corpora and unlabeled Shakespeare text, there is some risk of near-duplicate leakage or stylistic artifacts; the appendix description is brief.

In terms of novelty, I would characterize the contribution as moderate but meaningful. Sequence-level KD, unlabeled-data augmentation via teacher-generated targets, and student-conditioned training ideas all have precedents in adjacent literatures, but the paper’s combination, broad empirical comparison, and deployment-oriented framing are valuable. Joint-Teaching appears to be the most novel algorithmic element. The paper’s strongest contribution is probably the empirical synthesis and the demonstration that pseudo-target training can be pushed much further than the standard single beam target recipe.

To strengthen the paper, I would recommend the following. First, add variance estimates or significance testing for the main comparisons, ideally over multiple seeds for at least the key methods. Second, temper claims of generality and exposure-bias causality, making clear which conclusions are conditional on the chosen setup and model families. Third, clarify the GPT-4 tokenizer/logit alignment procedure, especially the treatment of unmatched tokens. Fourth, include at least approximate training-cost comparisons for the main PT variants. Fifth, if space permits, add one or two targeted interaction ablations—for example, whether Joint-Teaching still helps when using beam PTs instead of sampled PTs, or whether sampling still helps without unlabeled data. Even limited interaction checks would substantially improve the “systematic study” claim.

Overall, I think this is a solid and useful paper with strong practical relevance and several interesting findings, but also with methodological limitations that prevent me from fully endorsing its broader claims. My assessment is positive but not unequivocal: the work is valuable as an empirical study and recipe paper, especially for practitioners, but some conclusions should be presented more cautiously and supported with stronger statistical evidence.
