Review ID: MV_041
Validation Track: RQ1
============================================================

This paper studies a central and timely problem in RLHF: overoptimization against an imperfect reward model or preference objective. The main claim is both conceptually appealing and practically relevant: adding an SFT-style imitation term to preference optimization can be understood as an implicit adversarial regularizer, and this yields both provable robustness benefits and a simple practical algorithm, RPO, that augments DPO with an SFT loss. Overall, I found the paper strong. It addresses an important failure mode in alignment, proposes a clean theoretical formulation, derives a practical objective with a surprisingly simple implementation, and supports the claims with both theory and experiments on real LLM alignment setups. The paper is ambitious in scope, spanning offline RLHF theory under general function approximation, minimax/maximin equivalence, and empirical validation on multiple benchmarks. In my view, this is a meaningful contribution.

The strongest aspect of the paper is the bridge it builds between theory and practice. The theoretical starting point in Section 3 is a maximin objective where the policy is optimized against an adversarial reward model that minimizes a combination of MLE loss and a reward penalty term. This is a principled way to encode pessimism under reward uncertainty and distribution shift. The practical payoff is especially elegant: through the KL-regularized optimal policy characterization and the minimax reformulation in Section 4, the authors derive an implementation-friendly objective that is simply preference optimization plus imitation loss. This is a compelling result because it gives a theoretical explanation for a very practical recipe that practitioners can adopt immediately. The title claim that “your SFT loss is implicitly an adversarial regularizer” is therefore not just rhetorical; the paper does a good job substantiating it mathematically.

I also appreciated the clarity of the motivating intuition. Figure 1 on page 3 is effective: the left panel illustrates the mismatch between desired and undesired responses under uncertainty, the middle panel shows the degradation of log-probability on chosen responses during DPO training, and the right panel gives a simple three-action example where DPO can place mass on an uncovered bad action while RPO avoids it. This figure does real explanatory work and helps connect the abstract pessimism argument to concrete overoptimization behavior. The discussion in Appendix A further clarifies why monitoring chosen-response probability can be informative about reward overoptimization.

From a theoretical standpoint, the paper is quite substantial. The sample complexity result in Theorem 5.3 is meaningful: under a partial coverage condition and finite covering number assumptions on the reward class, the algorithm achieves an approximately O(1/sqrt(N)) suboptimality gap against any comparator policy whose induced distribution is sufficiently covered. I particularly liked that the analysis is framed under general function approximation rather than only linear models, which broadens the conceptual relevance. The partial coverage assumption in Assumption 5.2 is also well motivated and aligns with the offline RL literature. The equivalence result in Section 5.2 is another highlight. Showing that the minimax-derived practical policy also solves the original maximin objective is important for legitimizing the practical algorithm rather than presenting it as merely heuristic.

The empirical section is also solid and, importantly, aligned with the paper’s central claims. The experiments use two realistic model/data pipelines, Zephyr-7b-beta with UltraFeedback and Zephyr-7b-gemma with Argilla-DPO-Mix-7K, which helps demonstrate that the method is not tied to a single reference model or dataset. The training curves on pages 3 and 14 support the claim that RPO alleviates the drop in log-probability on chosen responses seen with DPO. The in-distribution pairwise GPT-4 evaluations in Table 1 show consistent gains over DPO for both series. The benchmark results in Table 2 are also encouraging: RPO improves over DPO on MT-Bench and AlpacaEval 2.0 for both beta and gemma variants, and the appendix extends this to GSM8K, ARC, and MBPP. The practical message is attractive: a simple plug-in regularizer appears to improve alignment quality without requiring extra preference data or a more complex RL pipeline.

That said, there are several points where the paper could be strengthened further.

First, while the theory-to-practice derivation is elegant, the exact relationship between the theoretical algorithm and the implemented RPO objective still feels somewhat idealized. The theory optimizes over a reward class and policy class with exact minimax/maximin structure, whereas the practical implementation uses parameterized LLMs trained by gradient descent on a surrogate objective. This gap is unavoidable to some extent, but the paper could be more explicit about which parts of the guarantee survive approximation, optimization error, and finite-capacity parameterization. Corollary 5.7 states suboptimality for minimax-based algorithms, but the actual neural optimization dynamics of Algorithm 2 are not analyzed. As written, the theory justifies the objective form more than the trained neural solution. This is still valuable, but the distinction should be stated more plainly.

Second, the assumptions behind the minimax equivalence deserve a bit more discussion. Assumption 5.5 requires compactness and a convex-like property of the reward class. The paper notes that convex reward classes satisfy this, but in realistic LLM settings the implicit reward classes induced by neural parameterizations are highly nonconvex. Since the practical significance of Theorem 5.6 is central, it would help to clarify whether the theorem should be interpreted mainly as a conceptual justification rather than a literal guarantee for neural training. I do not view this as a fatal issue, but the current presentation may overstate how directly the formal equivalence transfers to modern deep learning practice.

Third, the empirical evaluation, while promising, is somewhat narrow relative to the breadth of the claims. The paper argues that RPO mitigates overoptimization due to uncertainty and distribution shift, but the experiments mostly compare against vanilla DPO and released DPO-trained models. Since the related work section explicitly mentions methods such as ORPO, Smaug/DPO-positive, reward ensembles, constrained RLHF, and other regularized preference optimization variants, stronger baselines would make the empirical case more convincing. In particular, because some prior methods also add SFT-like or positive-example regularization, it would be useful to compare directly against those approaches rather than only DPO. This is especially important because the novelty claim is partly theoretical understanding, but the practical algorithmic form may overlap with existing heuristics.

Fourth, the evaluation methodology relies heavily on GPT-4-as-judge. This is standard practice, and the authors do take some care with prompt design and position randomization in Appendix E.2, but it remains a limitation. Human evaluation, even on a modest subset, would strengthen the alignment claims considerably. At minimum, reporting confidence intervals or bootstrap uncertainty for the pairwise win rates and benchmark scores would help assess robustness. The current tables report point estimates only, and some gains, while consistent, are not very large.

Fifth, the choice of the baseline policy for the imitation term is important both theoretically and practically, but the empirical exploration of this design choice is limited. The theory allows flexibility, and the paper mentions options such as preferred responses or initial model responses. In experiments, the chosen responses are used. An ablation comparing different baseline distributions would be very informative, especially because the central claim is that the SFT loss acts as an adversarial regularizer when the baseline is suitably covered by data. It would also help clarify whether the gains come specifically from imitating chosen responses or more generally from adding any anchor toward in-distribution behavior.

Sixth, I would have liked more ablations on the regularization strength and interaction with beta. The paper argues in Section 4 that beta in DPO mainly rescales gradients while eta changes gradient direction through the imitation term. This is an interesting claim, but the empirical section only briefly states that eta was selected as 0.005. A sweep over eta, and perhaps a joint eta/beta sensitivity analysis, would make the practical guidance much stronger. Similarly, since the paper emphasizes overoptimization during training, it would be useful to show how performance evolves over epochs for DPO versus RPO, not just the chosen-response log-probability.

There are also a few presentation and technical issues that should be cleaned up. In Section 5.2, the definition of phi in equation (5.2) appears to use pi_ref in the expectation over a0, whereas earlier sections define the baseline policy more generally as pi_base; this may be a typo or notational inconsistency and should be corrected. There are a handful of minor grammatical issues and notation slips throughout, but none are serious. I also think the paper could do a better job distinguishing what is formally proved from what is empirically suggested. For example, the PCA plots on page 12 are used to support the coverage intuition for benchmark prompts, but this is only a rough visualization and should not be read as evidence that the density ratio assumption actually holds.

Despite these concerns, my overall assessment is positive. The paper tackles an important problem, offers a genuinely insightful theoretical perspective, and derives a practical algorithm that is simple enough to matter. The conceptual contribution is stronger than a typical “add one more regularizer” paper because it explains why the regularizer should help and ties it to adversarial pessimism under uncertainty. The empirical results, while not exhaustive, are consistent with the theory and demonstrate practical promise on realistic LLM alignment pipelines. I expect this work to be of interest to both the RLHF theory community and practitioners working on preference optimization.

The most valuable revisions would be to add stronger empirical baselines, include ablations over the baseline policy and eta, report uncertainty estimates, and clarify the scope of the theoretical guarantees relative to neural optimization. But even in its current form, I think the paper makes a worthwhile contribution.

In summary, this is a strong paper with a clear idea, elegant derivation, meaningful theory, and encouraging experiments. Its central message—that SFT-style imitation can be understood as a principled adversarial regularizer against overoptimization in RLHF—is novel, useful, and likely to influence future work. I would lean toward acceptance.
