Review ID: MV_033
Validation Track: RQ1
============================================================

This paper presents a broad empirical study of task-specific knowledge distillation for natural language generation under a practically motivated setting: medium-resource labeled data, abundant unlabeled task-specific inputs, off-the-shelf pretrained models, and a strong emphasis on inference-time efficiency. The central technical message is that pseudo-target training can be made substantially more effective than the standard “single beam-search pseudo-target” recipe, especially when combined with word-level KD, unlabeled data, multiple sampled pseudo-targets, and the proposed Joint-Teaching method. Overall, I found the paper thoughtful, well-motivated, and practically useful. It addresses an important problem, asks the right systems-oriented questions, and provides a fairly comprehensive experimental investigation across multiple tasks and model families.

The paper’s main strengths are its scope, its practical framing, and the clarity of its empirical conclusions. Rather than proposing a narrowly optimized method for one benchmark, the authors explicitly study a sequence of design decisions that practitioners actually face: encoder-decoder versus decoder-only architectures, encoder versus decoder pruning, choice of KD objective, whether and how to use pseudo-targets, whether unlabeled data helps, how many pseudo-targets to generate, how to decode them, and whether student-generated pseudo-targets can be useful. The staged design in Figure 1 on page 4 is a strong organizing device. It makes the paper easy to follow and gives the work a cumulative logic: each stage motivates the next. This is especially valuable in a literature where many KD papers compare only a small subset of choices.

I also appreciate the paper’s emphasis on realistic deployment metrics. Table 2 on page 7 and Table 4 on page 9 report not only task metrics but also FLOPs, latency, throughput, memory, and parameter counts. This is a meaningful contribution in itself. Many compression papers rely too heavily on parameter count or FLOPs, whereas this paper explicitly distinguishes latency-sensitive and throughput-sensitive deployment regimes. The discussion around pruning is particularly useful: decoder pruning gives much larger latency gains than encoder pruning because of autoregressive decoding, while encoder pruning can help throughput more when inputs are long. The complexity discussion in Appendix B, especially the analysis around Figure 3 on page 18, supports these observations well.

The empirical finding that encoder-decoder models outperform decoder-only models for task-specific conditional NLG in this regime is interesting and practically relevant. The comparison in Table 2 suggests that T5 and BART variants consistently dominate GPT2/OPT variants on average task quality while remaining competitive in practical efficiency. The paper is careful not to overclaim this result beyond the studied regime, and Appendix B gives a reasonable architectural explanation. This part of the paper is not the headline contribution, but it strengthens the overall study by grounding later KD experiments in a justified student/teacher architecture choice.

The most novel and compelling part of the paper is the pseudo-target analysis. The authors argue that sequence-level KD should be revisited through the lens of exposure bias, and from that perspective derive several extensions: using pseudo-targets for unlabeled inputs, generating multiple pseudo-targets, preferring sampling over beam search, and combining teacher- and student-generated pseudo-targets via Joint-Teaching. This is a coherent conceptual thread rather than a bag of tricks. The discussion around Figure 2 on page 5 is helpful here: it provides evidence that teacher continuations remain better than student continuations even when conditioning on student-generated prefixes, which motivates using the teacher as an oracle on student trajectories.

The results in Table 3 on page 8 are strong and fairly convincing. Several conclusions stand out. First, plain sequence-level KD alone is weaker than logits KD, but combining logits KD with pseudo-targets is better than either alone. Second, unlabeled data provides a large gain, which is important because this is exactly the setting the paper argues is realistic in practice. Third, multiple pseudo-targets help, and sampling is generally better than beam search for generating them. This is a notable challenge to the standard mode-approximation recipe inherited from earlier sequence-level KD work. Finally, Joint-Teaching appears to improve over using only teacher or only student pseudo-targets. The final compression results in Table 4 are impressive from a systems perspective: large reductions in FLOPs and latency with much of the teacher-student gap recovered, and in some cases near-teacher or even slightly better-than-teacher task scores.

Another positive aspect is that the paper does not stop at the main setting. The GPT-4 extreme setup in Section 5.1 and Appendix C is exploratory but valuable. Distilling from a large decoder-only API model into a small encoder-decoder model with a different tokenizer is a realistic and timely scenario. The results are mixed, and the paper is appropriately cautious about them. I view this section as a useful extension rather than a core claim, and it broadens the paper’s relevance.

That said, there are several limitations and points where the paper could be stronger.

The biggest methodological concern is the sequential stage-wise model selection procedure. At each stage, the authors choose one option based on average development performance and then continue from there. This is understandable computationally, but it means later conclusions are conditional on earlier choices and may miss interactions between factors. For example, the best decoding strategy for pseudo-targets might depend on whether one uses logits KD alone or Joint-Teaching, or on the student architecture. The paper acknowledges that exhaustive combinatorial exploration is too expensive, but the consequence is that some conclusions should be interpreted as heuristic best practices rather than isolated causal findings. I would have liked at least a small factorial study on one dataset or one student-teacher pair to quantify interaction effects among a few key variables, especially pseudo-target count, decoding strategy, and Joint-Teaching.

Relatedly, the aggregation scheme in Table 3, based on fraction of teacher-student gap closed and wins across 32 setups, is useful but can obscure absolute effect sizes and variance. Gap-closed percentages are intuitive, yet they can be unstable when the teacher-student gap is small, and averaging across heterogeneous metrics such as BLEU, ROUGE, BERTScore, and perplexity may blur tradeoffs. The appendix contains fuller results, which helps, but the main paper would benefit from confidence intervals or standard deviations across seeds. This is especially important because the extreme setup explicitly reports high variability across learning rates and initializations. Even in the main setting, some of the gains between neighboring methods in Table 3 are modest, and it is hard to judge robustness without variance estimates.

The novelty claim around exposure bias should also be stated carefully. The paper’s framing of pseudo-target KD as a way to mitigate exposure bias in the student is interesting and, to my knowledge, less emphasized in prior NLG distillation work. However, the evidence remains somewhat indirect. Figure 2 is suggestive, but it is a single setup analysis on SQuAD17 with T5-S/T5-L. The paper would be stronger if it included a more direct empirical test of the exposure-bias hypothesis across tasks, for example by measuring performance under perturbed prefixes or by comparing teacher- versus student-conditioned continuation quality more systematically. As written, the exposure-bias perspective is plausible and useful, but not fully established as the mechanism behind the observed gains.

I also have some concerns about fairness and completeness of the decoder-only versus encoder-decoder comparison. The paper compares off-the-shelf GPT2/OPT models against T5/BART under task-specific fine-tuning, which is a reasonable practical comparison, but these model families differ in pretraining data, objectives, and age. The conclusion that encoder-decoder models are better for conditional NLG in this setup is likely true, but it should be interpreted as a comparison of available model families rather than a clean architectural ablation. The appendix discussion helps, yet the main text could be more explicit about this caveat.

The proposed Joint-Teaching method is promising, but I would have liked a deeper analysis of why it works and when it fails. The paper uses a fixed 50/50 mixture between teacher- and student-generated pseudo-targets. This seems somewhat arbitrary. Appendix A notes that alpha could be scheduled, but no ablation is provided. Since the method’s intuition is tied to the student becoming closer to the teacher over time, a curriculum or annealing study would be especially natural here. Similarly, it would be useful to know whether Joint-Teaching helps more for weaker students, longer outputs, or tasks with higher exposure-bias sensitivity.

The paper’s task coverage is broad, but the datasets are all English-to-English and mostly relatively short-output tasks. The authors acknowledge this in the limitations section on page 10. This is an honest limitation, but it matters because sequence-level KD has historically been very important in machine translation and other longer-output settings. The paper positions itself as a general KD recipe for NLG; I think that is directionally fair, but the claims should remain bounded to the studied regime. In particular, it is unclear whether the same conclusions about sampling versus beam search or about decoder pruning would hold for much longer outputs or multilingual generation.

There are also a few presentation issues. Some terminology could be tightened: “Pseudo-Targets” are essentially sequence-level KD targets, and while the paper explains this, the proliferation of names across stages can be a bit hard to track. The appendix tables are comprehensive but dense. A concise summary table of the final recommended recipe, with exact training steps and costs, would improve usability for practitioners. I also noticed some duplication and awkward phrasing in Appendix C around tokenizer alignment, suggesting that this section could use editing.

On the experimental side, the omission of training-time cost from the main comparison is understandable given the deployment focus, but still somewhat limiting. The paper’s best-performing methods rely on generating many pseudo-targets, including for unlabeled data, and sometimes on on-the-fly student generation. For some users, this one-time cost is acceptable; for others, it is not. Since the paper explicitly argues for realism, a rough accounting of preprocessing and distillation wall-clock cost for the main methods would make the recommendations more actionable. The limitations section acknowledges this clearly, which I appreciate.

The extreme GPT-4 setup is interesting but less conclusive. The tokenizer-alignment procedure in Appendix C is clever, yet also somewhat ad hoc, especially because only top-5 teacher token probabilities are available and unmatched student tokens are assigned probability one. This makes the logits KD signal rather approximate. The mixed results are therefore not surprising. I do not view this as a flaw in the main paper, since the authors present it as an exploratory extension, but readers should not overinterpret those findings.

Despite these concerns, I think the paper makes a solid contribution. Its strongest value is not a single algorithmic novelty in isolation, but a coherent and practically grounded study that yields several actionable lessons: prefer encoder-decoder students for conditional NLG in this regime, prune decoder layers when targeting latency, use logits KD as the backbone objective, combine it with pseudo-target training, exploit unlabeled inputs, generate multiple pseudo-targets, prefer sampling over beam search, and consider mixing teacher- and student-generated pseudo-targets. These are useful conclusions, and the paper backs them with substantial experimentation across tasks and model pairs.

If the authors revise the paper, I would encourage them to strengthen three areas in particular. First, add robustness statistics or seed variance for the main comparisons, at least for the key methods in Table 3. Second, include a small interaction study or ablation around Joint-Teaching’s mixing coefficient and pseudo-target count/decoding strategy. Third, sharpen the causal claims around exposure bias by either adding more direct evidence or framing it more explicitly as a motivating interpretation rather than a demonstrated mechanism.

In summary, this is a strong empirical paper on an important topic. It is well motivated, technically sound, and unusually attentive to deployment-relevant considerations. The pseudo-target findings are practically significant, and the Joint-Teaching idea is interesting and promising. While some claims could be better isolated and some analyses deepened, the paper provides real value to both researchers and practitioners working on efficient NLG systems. I would be positive on acceptance.
