Review ID: MV_034
Validation Track: RQ2
============================================================

This paper studies an important and timely problem: how to obtain formal privacy guarantees when aligning LLM behavior via activation steering using private demonstrations. The core proposal, PSA, is conceptually simple: compute per-layer steering vectors from positive/negative demonstration pairs, clip the per-example activation differences, and add Gaussian noise so that the released steering vectors satisfy differential privacy. The paper also introduces a membership inference attack tailored to activation editing, based on canary-triggered generations from black-box text outputs. Empirically, the authors evaluate across several alignment datasets, multiple open-source model families, and a range of utility metrics.

Overall, I find the paper interesting and potentially impactful. The problem is well motivated, and the main idea is elegant: activation steering is especially amenable to DP because the private computation can be confined to a one-time release of a small set of vectors, after which arbitrary downstream generations are pure post-processing. This is a meaningful conceptual contribution relative to DP fine-tuning and DP in-context learning, where privacy accounting is often more operationally cumbersome. The empirical scope is also fairly broad. At the same time, I have several concerns about technical precision, evaluation methodology, and the strength of some claims. My current assessment is positive but not fully convinced; with clarification and stronger experimental rigor, this could become a strong paper.

The main strength of the work is the framing. The observation that activation editing can be privatized “offline” by releasing only noisy steering vectors is compelling. If correct, this gives a practically attractive privacy mechanism with no per-query privacy cost, which is a real advantage over many inference-time privacy methods. The paper also addresses a gap in the literature: privacy for activation steering does appear underexplored, and the proposed attack is a useful step toward empirical auditing in this setting. The experiments span seven behaviors and several model families, which helps support the claim that the approach is not narrowly tuned to one benchmark.

That said, the technical presentation of the DP mechanism needs tightening. The algorithm clips each difference vector and then averages them with Gaussian noise. This is standard in spirit, but the notation and sensitivity argument are not fully clean. In Algorithm 1, the clipping formula appears to divide by max{C_l, ||d_i^l||_2}, which would bound the norm by at most 1/C_l times the original norm if I read it literally; the text says “project to an L2 ball of radius C_l and then scale by the same constant C_l,” which suggests the intended formula may instead be d_i^l / max{1, ||d_i^l||_2 / C_l}, or equivalently C_l d_i^l / max{C_l, ||d_i^l||_2}. As written, the algorithm and prose do not match. This matters because the sensitivity and noise calibration depend directly on the clipping convention. Relatedly, the Gaussian noise is written as N(0, sigma^2), but since the output is a d-dimensional vector, the intended mechanism is presumably isotropic Gaussian noise N(0, sigma^2 I_d). This should be stated explicitly.

More importantly, the sensitivity constant used in the noise scale deserves a more careful derivation. The paper sets sigma = 2 sqrt(2 ln(1.25/delta)) / (n epsilon), which corresponds to sensitivity 2/n under unit-norm clipping. That is plausible under add/remove or replace-one neighboring datasets depending on conventions, but the paper should state the adjacency model explicitly and prove the bound. Since the demonstrations are triples (p_i, c_i+, c_i-), replacing one record changes one clipped difference vector, so the L2 sensitivity of the average is at most 2/n if each clipped vector has norm at most 1. But because the clipping notation is ambiguous, the proof is currently incomplete. Also, if different layers use different clipping thresholds C_l, then either the vectors are normalized to unit norm before averaging or the sensitivity should scale with C_l. The current formula appears independent of C_l, which again suggests the algorithm description is inconsistent.

The privacy accounting across layers is also somewhat underdeveloped. The paper uses basic composition to claim total privacy of (|S| epsilon, |S| delta) when releasing steering vectors for |S| layers. That is fine as a simple bound, but the paper should be more explicit about whether the same private dataset is reused across all layers, which it is, and therefore composition is indeed necessary. However, the statement that basic composition is tighter than advanced composition for fewer than five layers is too casual and not generally true without specifying the parameter regime. Since the paper already reports total epsilon values, it would be easy to include a more principled accountant or at least compare basic and advanced composition numerically. Given that one of the selling points is formal privacy, this part should be more rigorous.

I also think the paper overstates the novelty slightly. The application to activation steering is new, and that is valuable. But algorithmically, PSA is essentially “clip per-example representation differences and add Gaussian noise to their mean.” That is a straightforward instantiation of the Gaussian mechanism rather than a fundamentally new DP technique. I would encourage the authors to frame the novelty more as a new problem setting, a clean DP formulation for steering, and a new empirical attack/audit methodology, rather than as a novel DP algorithmic advance.

On the empirical side, the breadth is good, but the reporting is often too qualitative. Figure 3 is central, yet the text repeatedly says “comparable” without confidence intervals, standard deviations, or significance tests. Since PSA injects randomness, variance across noise draws is essential. Are the reported results from a single noise sample per dataset/layer/model, or averaged over multiple privatized steering vectors? If only one draw is used, the conclusions about utility preservation are much weaker. At minimum, the paper should report mean and standard deviation over several random seeds for the DP noise and generation randomness. This is especially important because the paper itself notes that PSA sometimes outperforms non-private steering; without uncertainty estimates, it is impossible to know whether these differences are meaningful or just noise.

The choice of privacy parameters is also not fully satisfying. The paper says it adds Gaussian noise with standard deviation 0.02 and fixes delta = 1/(5n), then derives epsilon values around 2 for most datasets and up to 6.6 for smaller ones. This is a somewhat unusual presentation because in DP one typically fixes epsilon and delta and derives the required noise, not vice versa. Choosing sigma first may be reasonable for utility reasons, but then the privacy guarantees are whatever they happen to be, and some of the resulting epsilons are not especially strong. An epsilon of 6.6 is fairly weak by many standards. The paper should discuss this more candidly and ideally provide privacy-utility curves over a range of sigma or epsilon values. Right now, the claim of “minimal loss in performance” is only demonstrated at one operating point, and the reader cannot assess how utility degrades as privacy becomes stronger.

Relatedly, the clipping thresholds C_l are critical hyperparameters, but I could not find a clear description of how they are chosen in practice. The text says they can be optimized or estimated using a small public dataset. Were they tuned separately per model, per behavior, and per layer? Was any private data used for tuning? If so, that would incur additional privacy cost unless done carefully. If public data were used, what public data? This is not a minor implementation detail; clipping is central to both privacy and utility.

The evaluation of text generation quality via GPT-4 as judge is acceptable as a supplementary signal, but the current presentation is weak. Table 3 reports scores that are often extremely low for some behaviors, and the interpretation is unclear. For example, sycophancy and AI coordination scores near 0–1.5 out of 10 suggest either the rubric is unusual or the generations are poor across the board. The paper should explain what exactly is being scored and whether higher always means “better alignment” or “better text quality” or some combination. Right now the metric conflates behavior adherence and generation quality. Also, using only GPT-4 judgments without human validation is not ideal for a paper making nuanced claims about alignment behavior.

The MMLU evaluation is a useful sanity check, but Table 5 appears incomplete or malformed: the zero-shot row is truncated, and one hallucination entry is blank. This makes it difficult to trust the table as presented. More broadly, converting MMLU to A/B format and reporting average probability of the correct answer is unusual enough that it deserves more explanation. Since steering is applied at inference time to all tokens and selected layers, it would be helpful to know whether the effect on general capabilities depends strongly on the steering multiplier lambda or on the chosen behavior. A fuller ablation here would strengthen the claim that PSA has limited collateral damage.

The proposed membership inference attack is interesting, but I have mixed feelings about how representative it is of the privacy risk PSA is supposed to address. The attack inserts synthetic canaries into demonstrations and then tests whether the steered model preferentially emits the target canary when prompted with the anchor canary. This is a reasonable audit for memorization-like leakage through steering vectors, and it is useful as a lower-level probe. However, it is also a somewhat artificial setup. The canary is deliberately designed to create a strong anchor-target association, which may not reflect realistic private demonstrations in alignment datasets. The paper should be clearer that this is an auditing mechanism rather than a comprehensive model of real-world privacy leakage. It would be stronger to complement it with attacks on naturally occurring sensitive attributes or with reconstruction-style probes from semantically similar prompts.

There are also methodological questions about the attack. The threshold tau is chosen differently for Llama and Qwen; how was it selected? Was it tuned on held-out attack data? If tuned on the evaluation trials, the reported FPR/FNR may be optimistic. The attack generates 100 outputs at temperature 0.8 and counts target occurrences. This introduces substantial stochasticity; confidence intervals over the 1000 trials should be reported. Moreover, the empirical epsilon calculation from FPR/FNR is fine as an audit heuristic, but it should not be overinterpreted as an estimate of the true DP epsilon of the mechanism. The paper mostly avoids this confusion, but some wording suggests that lower empirical epsilon means “better protected” in a way directly comparable to theoretical epsilon. These are different notions; one is attack-specific and lower-bounds distinguishability under a particular adversary, while the other is a worst-case guarantee over all adversaries and outputs.

Another concern is the baseline selection. The paper compares PSA mainly to mean steering and PCA steering, plus zero-shot. That is reasonable within activation editing, but if the broader claim is about privacy-preserving alignment with private datasets, it would be useful to compare against at least one alternative privacy-preserving approach, such as DP in-context learning or a lightweight DP fine-tuning baseline on the same demonstrations, even if only on a subset of models/tasks. Without such a comparison, it is hard to assess whether activation steering is uniquely attractive from a privacy-utility standpoint or simply one of several viable options.

The scaling claim in Section 5.4 is plausible but under-supported. The paper states a “scaling rule” that larger models exhibit a better privacy-utility tradeoff, based mainly on one model family and one dataset figure. This is more of an empirical trend than a rule. I would soften the language unless the authors can provide broader evidence across behaviors and families, or a mechanistic explanation beyond speculation that larger instruction-tuned models already encode the relevant behavior.

There are also several presentation issues that should be fixed. The manuscript contains visible formatting artifacts and footnote markers embedded in text (“111”, “222”, etc.), duplicated figure/algorithm blocks, and some notation inconsistencies. In Problem Definition, the dataset is indexed from i=0 to n but later sums run from 1 to n. Algorithm 1 has a typo in the difference vector definition, using h_l((p,c+)) instead of h_l((p_i,c_i+)). Table 5 is incomplete. These are not fatal, but they detract from confidence in the technical care of the work.

Despite these criticisms, I think the paper has real merit. The central idea is simple in a good way, the problem is important, and the offline-DP perspective for steering is appealing. If the authors can clean up the DP derivation, clarify clipping and sensitivity, strengthen the experimental reporting with uncertainty estimates and privacy-utility curves, and better position the MIA as an audit rather than a complete privacy evaluation, this would be a valuable contribution.

My main suggestions for improvement are as follows. First, provide a precise theorem and proof for the privacy guarantee, including the exact clipping operator, adjacency notion, vector-valued Gaussian mechanism, and composition across layers. Second, report results over multiple random seeds for both DP noise and generation, with error bars or confidence intervals. Third, include privacy-utility tradeoff curves by varying sigma or target epsilon, rather than fixing one noise level. Fourth, explain how clipping thresholds and selected layers are chosen, and ensure any tuning does not consume unaccounted private budget. Fifth, strengthen the privacy evaluation by adding at least one more realistic attack setting beyond synthetic canaries, or by more clearly delimiting the scope of the current attack. Sixth, fix the presentation issues and incomplete tables.

In summary, I view this as a promising paper with a strong problem statement and a neat application of DP to activation steering, but one that currently falls short of the rigor expected for a top-tier venue in several places. My overall recommendation is cautiously positive if the contribution is judged primarily on novelty of setting and practical relevance, but I would want the final version to address the technical and empirical concerns above.
