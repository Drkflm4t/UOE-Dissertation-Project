# Review ID: MV_012

**Validation Track:** `RQ1`

---

```
Summary: The paper presents LOGICLLAMA, a LLaMA-7B model fine-tuned with LoRA for translating natural language into first-order logic and for correcting FOL outputs produced by larger LLMs such as GPT-3.5. The work also introduces MALLS, a 34K GPT-4-generated NL-FOL dataset with syntactic verification and prompt-based diversity controls. Methodologically, the paper studies direct translation, one-shot correction, and a chain-of-thought correction framework trained via synthetic perturbation-based supervised fine-tuning followed by RLHF using a custom logical-equivalence-based reward. Experiments on FOLIO and LogicNLI show that direct translation outperforms GPT-3.5, while the correction pipeline narrows the gap to GPT-4 at substantially lower cost.

Strengths: Addresses an important and underexplored task: sentence-level natural-language-to-first-order-logic translation, which is highly relevant for reasoning, theorem proving, and neuro-symbolic NLP.; Introduces a practically useful specialized model (LOGICLLAMA) that can both directly translate NL to FOL and iteratively correct outputs from stronger proprietary models.; Presents a sizable new dataset (MALLS, 34K pairs) that is substantially larger than prior gold datasets and appears more diverse in vocabulary, contexts, and logical structures.; The prompting/data-collection pipeline is thoughtfully designed, including n-gram frequency control, prompt diversification, operator balancing, predicate decomposition encouragement, and CFG-based syntactic verification.; The synthetic perturbation strategy for generating correction trajectories is a creative way to obtain supervision for stepwise correction without manual annotation.; The SFT+RLHF formulation is novel in this context and technically interesting, especially the use of a verifier-style reward based on logical equivalence rather than human preference modeling.; Empirical results are strong: on FOLIO, RLHF CoT correction improves GPT-3.5 outputs to near GPT-4 performance, and direct translation surpasses GPT-3.5.; The paper is generally clear, with helpful figures illustrating direct translation vs. correction modes and the training pipeline.; The work is computationally practical, emphasizing LoRA fine-tuning on a single GPU and lower-cost deployment relative to GPT-4.; The appendix provides useful implementation details, including CFG grammar, prompting templates, and evaluation details, which supports reproducibility.

Weaknesses: The core training dataset is silver rather than gold: MALLS is generated by GPT-4 and only lightly filtered for NL-FOL semantic alignment, so annotation noise may be substantial.; The evaluation is limited to two benchmarks, one of which (LogicNLI) is relatively templatic and near-saturated for strong models, reducing the breadth of empirical validation.; Comparisons are primarily against GPT-3.5/GPT-4 prompting rather than against prior dedicated semantic parsing or NL-to-logic systems, making it harder to position gains relative to the broader literature.; The proposed logical equivalence metric is approximate and heuristic, relying on literal extraction, greedy binding, dummy literals, and truth-table overlap rather than full theorem-proving-based equivalence checking.; The RLHF setup is not compared against simpler alternatives such as self-training, rejection sampling, or direct optimization on final-rule rewards without CoT, so the necessity of the full pipeline is not fully isolated.; The paper does not provide human evaluation or manual error analysis quantifying semantic faithfulness of MALLS or of model outputs beyond BLEU/LE.; Cost claims are directionally plausible but not rigorously benchmarked end-to-end with full inference-time accounting for iterative correction.

Methodological Flaws: A significant scientific validity concern is that MALLS semantic alignment is only weakly checked via term-frequency overlap between NL and FOL (page 4), which is not a reliable test of logical faithfulness. This means the model may learn from systematically misaligned supervision.; The logical equivalence reward is not true first-order logical equivalence. It reduces formulas to extracted literals and compares truth tables under heuristic literal bindings, which ignores quantifier semantics, variable binding structure, predicate arity mismatches, and deeper logical equivalence issues. As a result, both evaluation and RL reward may mis-score outputs.; The greedy literal-binding procedure for LE can overestimate similarity by selecting the best alignment and padding with dummy literals, potentially inflating reported performance and reward quality.; The paper states that GPT-3.5 rarely generates syntactically invalid rules and therefore restricts perturbations to valid-rule space, but later notes that GPT-3.5 sometimes generates invalid FOL and requires a naive-correction preprocessing step before RLHF. This weakens the justification that the synthetic perturbation space adequately matches real error modes.; The RLHF section frames the method as using a verifier as reward, but there is no evidence that PPO is stable or superior here; no ablation isolates whether gains come from RLHF itself versus iterative correction or the synthetic SFT stage.; Empirical validity is somewhat limited because there are no statistical significance tests, no multiple-run variance estimates, and no robustness analysis across prompts or decoding settings for GPT baselines.; Some few-shot examples in the prompt template shown in the appendix appear inconsistent or erroneous (e.g., NL/FOL mismatches), which raises concern about prompt quality during dataset generation and could introduce noise into MALLS.
```
