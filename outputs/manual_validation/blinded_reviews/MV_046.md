# Review ID: MV_046

**Validation Track:** `RQ2`

---

This paper proposes CoT-Influx, a plug-and-play prompting module for math reasoning that aims to increase the effective number of few-shot chain-of-thought exemplars within a fixed context window. The method combines retrieval, shot-level pruning, and token-level pruning, with the pruning policy trained by reinforcement learning against a frozen target LLM. The paper also introduces a GPT-4-generated/evolved math CoT dataset, MRD3, used for training and retrieval. Empirically, the paper reports gains over standard few-shot CoT, retrieval baselines, and prompt compression baselines on GSM8K and several smaller arithmetic benchmarks, with the strongest headline result being that LLaMA2-70B plus CoT-Influx reaches 59.6 EM on GSM8K.

Overall, the paper addresses an important and timely problem: how to better exploit limited context budgets for in-context reasoning. The central intuition is sensible and practically relevant. In-context exemplars are often redundant both at the example level and token level, and math prompting is indeed a setting where naive compression can destroy crucial structure. The paper’s coarse-to-fine pruning formulation is easy to understand, and the empirical comparisons suggest that jointly selecting examples and preserving math-critical tokens can outperform generic retrieval or generic compression. The work is also commendable for evaluating across multiple LLaMA2 scales and several datasets rather than only a single benchmark.

That said, I find the paper promising but not yet fully convincing at the level expected for a top-tier ML venue. My main concerns are about novelty relative to existing retrieval/compression/prompt-optimization work, methodological clarity, fairness of comparisons, and the extent to which the reported gains can be attributed specifically to the proposed RL pruner rather than to the newly constructed MRD3 dataset and other prompt design choices.

The strongest aspect of the paper is the problem framing. The pilot study usefully motivates that more exemplars can help, random exemplars can hurt, and generic compression methods may remove math-critical tokens. This is a good setup for a method that jointly reasons about which exemplars to keep and which tokens within them to preserve. The empirical trend that larger models benefit from more curated exemplars is plausible and interesting. The compatibility with self-consistency and self-verification is also a practical plus.

However, the novelty is somewhat incremental when decomposed into components. The method combines: retrieval of candidate exemplars, a learned shot selector, a learned token selector, and RL optimization using downstream task reward. Each ingredient has precedent in prompt retrieval, prompt compression, and discrete prompt optimization. The paper’s main novelty is their combination for math CoT prompting plus the claim that math requires preserving specific token types and structure. That may still be publishable if the empirical evidence is especially strong and the method is carefully validated, but currently the paper does not sufficiently disentangle what is new algorithmically from what is due to dataset engineering and benchmark-specific tuning.

A major issue is that the comparisons do not appear fully controlled. CoT-Influx uses MRD3, a GPT-4-generated and evolved dataset with standardized formatting and difficulty diversification. This is likely a substantial source of improvement by itself. Although Table 6 provides an ablation on the dataset, the main baseline comparisons in Tables 2 and 3 seem to compare CoT-Influx using MRD3 against standard few-shot CoT prompts and retrieval baselines that may not benefit from the same curated/evolved exemplar pool or formatting. If so, the gains conflate at least three factors: better exemplar corpus, better retrieval pool size, and better pruning policy. For a fair assessment of the pruning method, all retrieval and compression baselines should operate over exactly the same candidate pool, with the same formatting, same manual 8-shot appendix if used, same token budget, and same decoding settings. The paper suggests some of this but does not document it rigorously enough.

Relatedly, the training and inference prompt composition is potentially confounded by appending the manually designed 8-shot CoTs during training, and Algorithm 1 suggests these manual few-shot CoTs are also included in the final input. This is a crucial detail. If CoT-Influx’s final prompt includes both pruned retrieved exemplars and the canonical manual 8-shot CoT prompt, then the comparison to “Few-shot-CoT” or retrieval baselines is not apples-to-apples. Even if the manual 8-shot prompt is only used during training for stabilization, the paper needs to state this unambiguously and report results with and without this component at inference. As written, lines 14 and 22 in Algorithm 1 imply the manual few-shot prompt is concatenated into the final input. This could materially affect the reported gains and must be clarified.

The RL formulation is also under-specified and somewhat questionable. The reward in Eq. 5 multiplies a performance term by a token-length term [t/T]^w, but the description of the sign of w is confusing and possibly inconsistent. If w>0 penalizes exceeding the token limit, that does not by itself enforce the hard constraint t<=T, and if t>T the reward could still increase depending on the exponent. More fundamentally, the paper states the final prompt must fit the context window, but the reward seems to softly encourage certain lengths rather than enforce feasibility. How are infeasible prompts handled during training and inference? Are sampled actions rejected, truncated, or assigned zero reward? This is central to reproducibility.

The policy architecture description is also too vague for replication. The “state” is described as belonging to R^{k x N}, while the hidden states are BERT embeddings in R^{k x N x D}. It is not clear what exactly is fed to the MLP, whether shot-level decisions are based on pooled token embeddings or tokenwise embeddings, how token-level decisions are conditioned on shot-level selections, whether the token pruner sees the target question, and whether the policy is autoregressive or independent across tokens/examples. The equations suggest independent Bernoulli decisions via sigmoid, but the text also mentions sampling from a categorical distribution with softmax. These are not equivalent parameterizations. The paper needs a precise description of the action distribution and training objective.

Another concern is the use of BERT-Large as the feature extractor for exemplar selection and token pruning. Since the target models are decoder-only LLMs, it is not obvious that BERT token salience aligns with what helps the target LLM reason. The paper argues that RL bridges this mismatch, but then the policy is effectively learning from sparse, high-variance rewards over a huge combinatorial action space. The paper would benefit from stronger evidence that the learned policy is robust and not simply exploiting superficial formatting regularities in MRD3. For example, qualitative analyses of retained tokens across many examples, or comparisons to simpler heuristics such as preserving all numbers/operators/answer markers plus pruning stopwords, would help establish that the RL policy learns something nontrivial.

The empirical gains, while positive, are modest on the main benchmark relative to the complexity of the method. On GSM8K, the improvement over standard few-shot CoT is around 2.1 to 4.6 absolute points depending on model size, and over TopK retrieval it is around 1.4 to 8.7 points. For LLaMA2-70B, the gain over few-shot CoT is about 4.2 points. These are meaningful but not overwhelming, especially given the additional machinery of GPT-4 data generation, RL training, and an auxiliary encoder. The paper should better justify why this complexity is preferable to simpler alternatives, such as stronger supervised retrievers, reranking by target-model likelihood, or structure-aware rule-based compression tailored to math prompts.

I also have concerns about the baseline set. The retrieval baselines are relatively basic: random, TopK, BM25. The paper cites stronger supervised retrieval methods in related work but does not compare against them. Since the core claim is that model-specific exemplar selection matters, a learned retriever or reranker conditioned on the target model would be a much more relevant baseline. Likewise, for compression, the chosen baselines are generic prompt compressors not specialized for math. A stronger baseline would be a structure-preserving compressor that keeps equations, numbers, answer templates, and step delimiters while pruning natural-language filler. Without such baselines, the paper may overstate the uniqueness of its token-pruning contribution.

The evaluation scope is somewhat narrow despite using five datasets. AddSub, MultiArith, SVAMP, and SingleEq are relatively small and simple arithmetic benchmarks; gains there are useful but not sufficient to establish broad reasoning improvements. Since the method is trained on a dataset built from GSM8K/MAWPS/AQuA-derived examples, there is also a risk of benchmark overlap in style or content. The paper should discuss contamination and near-duplicate concerns more carefully, especially because GPT-4 is used to mutate and reformat examples. At minimum, the authors should explain how they prevent train-test leakage at the problem-template level and whether retrieval excludes examples from the same source benchmark split as the test question.

The claim of “no additional inference costs” is overstated. Even if the final prompt fits within the original context window, the method still requires running BERT-Large and the pruning module at inference time, plus retrieval over the exemplar corpus. Table 8 does report some overhead, which is good, but this directly contradicts the stronger claim in the introduction. The paper should instead say there is no increase in target LLM context length or generation budget, not no inference overhead overall.

There are also several presentation and correctness issues that reduce confidence. The paper contains many grammatical problems and repeated phrases, and some statements are imprecise or contradictory. Examples include “double the context window size in tokens” despite staying within the original context window after pruning; “without any inference overhead” versus Table 8; and the claim in the ablation text that “targeted pruning of tokens with low attention scores can lead to an estimated 10% increase in accuracy,” which does not seem directly supported by Table 7 and is phrased unclearly. The algorithm and equations need careful proofreading. There are also formatting issues in Table 1 and some notation inconsistencies.

For strengths, I would highlight the following. First, the paper tackles a practically important bottleneck in few-shot reasoning: context budget allocation. Second, the coarse-to-fine perspective is intuitive and likely useful beyond math. Third, the empirical observation that generic compression harms math prompting is valuable. Fourth, the paper includes ablations on dataset construction and pruning stages, which is a good start. Fifth, the method appears reasonably modular and black-box with respect to the target LLM.

For weaknesses, the most important are: insufficiently controlled comparisons; ambiguity about whether manual 8-shot CoTs are included at inference; under-specified RL and policy details; limited strength of baselines; possible confounding from MRD3 dataset engineering; and overclaiming around efficiency and generality.

To strengthen the paper, I would strongly recommend the following additional experiments and clarifications. First, provide a fully controlled comparison where all methods use the exact same candidate exemplar pool, formatting, token budget, retrieval stage, and decoding setup. Second, explicitly report results with and without the appended manual 8-shot CoTs at both training and inference. Third, compare against stronger learned retrieval/reranking baselines and simple math-aware rule-based compression baselines. Fourth, include an ablation replacing RL with supervised or heuristic pruning objectives, such as target-model perplexity-based token retention, to show RL is necessary. Fifth, clarify the hard handling of token-budget violations and provide all hyperparameters, including k, k’, w, d_thr, t_repeat, reward smoothing, and decoding settings. Sixth, analyze robustness across random seeds and report variance, since RL-based discrete optimization can be unstable. Seventh, provide contamination safeguards and source-split details for MRD3. Eighth, include qualitative examples showing what tokens are retained/dropped and failure cases where pruning hurts.

My overall assessment is that the paper has a good intuition and some promising empirical results, but in its current form it falls short of the rigor needed for strong acceptance. The main idea is interesting, yet the evidence does not cleanly isolate the contribution of the proposed pruning method from dataset and prompt engineering, and several methodological details remain unclear. I would lean borderline reject at a top-tier conference, while noting that with stronger controls, clearer exposition, and more competitive baselines, this line of work could become compelling.

If I were to summarize in one sentence: promising practical idea, but the current experimental design and presentation do not yet support the strength of the claims.
