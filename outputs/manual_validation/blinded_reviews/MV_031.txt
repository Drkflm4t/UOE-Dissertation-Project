Review ID: MV_031
Validation Track: RQ2
============================================================

This paper presents a broad empirical study of task-specific knowledge distillation for natural language generation under a practically motivated setting: medium-resource labeled data, abundant unlabeled task-specific inputs, off-the-shelf pretrained models, and a strong emphasis on inference efficiency. The central technical theme is to revisit sequence-level distillation through pseudo-targets, arguing that prior practice has underused them by relying on a single beam-generated target per labeled example. The paper studies multiple pseudo-targets, sampled rather than beam-decoded pseudo-targets, pseudo-targets for unlabeled inputs, and a new “Joint-Teaching” method that mixes teacher- and student-generated prefixes while applying word-level KD. The experiments span several English-to-English NLG tasks and compare architectural choices, pruning strategies, and KD objectives.

Overall, I found the paper interesting, practically relevant, and stronger as a systematic empirical study than as a sharply isolated algorithmic contribution. The work addresses a real gap between much of the KD literature and realistic deployment settings, especially the use of unlabeled task-specific data and the explicit consideration of latency and throughput. The empirical findings are often useful and actionable: encoder-decoder models outperform decoder-only models for these conditional generation tasks; decoder pruning is preferable to encoder pruning under their efficiency criteria; and pseudo-target training can be substantially more effective when expanded beyond the standard single beam output. The paper is also generally well motivated and the exposure-bias perspective gives a coherent narrative tying together several design choices.

That said, the paper also has important weaknesses. The main concern is that many conclusions are derived from a sequential decision pipeline in which one choice is fixed before evaluating the next. This makes the study practical, but it weakens causal attribution: later gains may depend on earlier choices, and interactions between factors are not systematically explored. In addition, some of the strongest claims are framed broadly, while the empirical scope is narrower than the wording suggests: four English-to-English tasks, relatively short outputs, two main teacher-student pairs in the KD stages, and a specific labeled/unlabeled ratio. The proposed Joint-Teaching idea is promising, but the evidence for its mechanism is somewhat indirect, and the method itself is only lightly analyzed. Finally, the paper emphasizes realistic deployment but largely omits training-time cost accounting, even though pseudo-target generation and repeated KD passes are central to the proposed recipe.

The strongest aspect of the paper is its practical orientation. Many KD papers optimize for benchmark accuracy while abstracting away deployment constraints; here, the authors explicitly measure latency and throughput on hardware, compare architectures under those metrics, and study compression in a task-specific fine-tuning regime. This is valuable. The finding that encoder-decoder models remain preferable to decoder-only models for conditional NLG fine-tuning is not entirely surprising, but it is useful to document in a controlled comparison with efficiency metrics included. Likewise, the decoder-pruning result is intuitive yet practically important, and the paper presents it clearly.

The pseudo-target study is also a meaningful contribution. The progression from single labeled pseudo-targets to unlabeled pseudo-targets, multiple pseudo-targets, and sampled pseudo-targets is sensible and empirically convincing within the paper’s setup. In particular, the unlabeled-data result appears strong and is probably the most impactful takeaway. If robust, it suggests that task-specific KD for NLG should be viewed partly as a semi-supervised learning problem, where the teacher provides synthetic supervision over abundant in-domain inputs. This is a useful framing and likely to matter in practice.

I also appreciated that the paper does not oversell sequence-level KD alone. The results show that pure sequence-level KD underperforms logits KD, while the combination works better. That is a nuanced and credible outcome. Similarly, the GPT-4 “extreme setup” section is appropriately presented as exploratory and mixed rather than as a headline success.

My main methodological reservation concerns the “systematic study” framing. The paper uses an eight-stage greedy selection process, choosing the best option on development performance at each stage and then carrying that choice forward. This is understandable computationally, but it is not equivalent to a systematic factorial study. Interactions could be substantial here: for example, the best KD objective may depend on whether pseudo-targets are sampled or beam-generated; the value of multiple pseudo-targets may depend on whether unlabeled data is included; the benefit of Joint-Teaching may depend on student capacity or pruning type. Because only one path through the design space is explored, some conclusions may be contingent on earlier decisions. The paper acknowledges cost constraints, but the title and presentation should be a bit more careful about the scope of the claims.

Relatedly, the aggregation and reporting choices sometimes obscure the underlying variability. Table 3 reports “fraction of student-teacher gap closed,” averaged over metrics and over two teacher-student pairs. This is a compact summary, but it can be unstable or hard to interpret when teacher-student gaps differ greatly across tasks and metrics, or when a student occasionally surpasses the teacher. It also mixes metrics with different semantics, including perplexity and overlap-based generation metrics. The appendix may contain the raw scores, but the main paper would benefit from more direct reporting of absolute performance deltas, variance across runs, and statistical uncertainty. As presented, many conclusions rely on single averaged numbers and win counts over 32 setups, but there is little sense of run-to-run stability.

A second concern is the novelty and precision of the exposure-bias argument. The paper claims to provide the first exposure-bias perspective for KD and uses this to motivate pseudo-target augmentation and Joint-Teaching. The intuition is reasonable: training on teacher- or student-generated prefixes can reduce the mismatch between training and inference contexts. However, the evidence is mostly correlational. Figure 2 is suggestive, but it is only a single setup analysis and does not directly establish that the observed gains from multiple sampled pseudo-targets arise because of reduced exposure bias rather than simply more diverse synthetic supervision or stronger data augmentation. In other words, the methods may work, but the mechanism remains underdetermined. I would encourage the authors to soften mechanistic claims or add more targeted evidence.

The Joint-Teaching method is the most novel algorithmic component, but it is also underanalyzed. The method appears to alternate between teacher-generated and student-generated pseudo-targets, with a fixed 50% probability for student PTs. This raises several questions. Why 50%? Is there a curriculum effect where student PTs should be introduced later, once the student is competent enough? How sensitive is performance to this ratio? Are student PTs sampled or beam-generated, and does that matter? Does the teacher provide logits conditioned on the full student-generated prefix at every step, and what is the computational overhead relative to teacher-only PT training? Since the paper’s narrative emphasizes exposure bias, a more careful ablation around these design choices would strengthen the contribution substantially.

The experimental scope is good but not fully sufficient to support some of the broader claims. The tasks are all English-to-English and mostly have short outputs. This matters because some of the paper’s arguments, especially around exposure bias and decoder pruning, may behave differently for longer-form generation or translation. The authors do acknowledge this in the limitations, which is good. Still, the paper would be stronger if at least one longer-output task or one non-English task were included, or if the claims were more explicitly bounded to the studied regime.

I also have some concerns about the architecture comparison. The paper concludes that encoder-decoder models outperform decoder-only models for task-specific conditional NLG. This is plausible and likely true in many settings, but the comparison may not be fully balanced. The model families differ in pretraining objectives, tokenization, optimization behavior, and instruction-following tendencies, not just architecture. The paper notes some of this in the appendix, but the main-text conclusion risks attributing differences to architecture alone. A more cautious phrasing would be appropriate unless the comparison controls more tightly for confounds.

The pruning study is useful, but again somewhat narrow. Only one pruning strategy is used, on one model family, by keeping first and last layers. This is enough to support a practical recommendation within BART-like encoder-decoder models, but perhaps not enough for a general rule of thumb. It would help to know whether the decoder-pruning advantage persists under alternative layer-selection heuristics or with T5-family pruning.

The GPT-4 section is interesting but limited. Because logits are available only through an internal version and only two datasets are used, the external reproducibility and generality of this section are weak. I do not view this as a fatal issue because the paper does not rely on this section for its main claims, but it should remain clearly secondary.

On evaluation, I appreciate the inclusion of multiple automatic metrics and some human evaluation for ART10. Still, the paper would benefit from more discussion of metric suitability per task. For example, BLEU and ROUGE are imperfect for style transfer and abductive reasoning, and averaging across them may blur meaningful differences. The human evaluation on ART10 is a good step, but if ART10 is the task where automatic metrics appear least aligned, more detail in the main paper would help.

The paper is generally well written and organized. The staged design is easy to follow, and the practical recommendations are clearly stated. However, there are a few places where the prose overstates certainty. Phrases like “should be utilized” or “it is better to prune layers from the decoder” read stronger than the evidence warrants across all settings. I would recommend tempering these to “in our setup” or “for the studied tasks and models.” There are also a few minor presentation issues, such as some awkward footnote formatting and occasional grammatical slips, but nothing major.

For improvement, I would most strongly recommend the following. First, add variance estimates over multiple random seeds for the key comparisons, especially Table 3. Second, include at least a small interaction study rather than only the greedy pipeline, for example crossing decoding method with number of pseudo-targets and unlabeled-data usage on one representative task/model pair. Third, analyze Joint-Teaching more deeply: sensitivity to the teacher/student PT mixing ratio, when student PTs are introduced, and whether gains persist across both student architectures separately. Fourth, report training-time and pseudo-target generation cost more concretely, since the proposed recipe relies heavily on synthetic data generation. Fifth, moderate the broadness of some claims, especially around architecture and exposure bias, to better match the empirical scope.

Despite these concerns, I think the paper makes a meaningful contribution. Its main value lies in consolidating and extending practical KD design choices for NLG under realistic assumptions, and in showing convincingly that pseudo-target training is more powerful than the standard single beam-generated target recipe suggests. The unlabeled-data result and the practical efficiency analysis are particularly compelling. The novelty is moderate rather than dramatic, but the empirical study is useful and likely to influence how practitioners approach task-specific NLG distillation.

My overall assessment is positive but not unequivocal. I would lean toward acceptance if the venue values strong empirical studies with practical relevance, provided the authors tighten the claims and, ideally, strengthen the analysis of robustness and interactions. If the bar is primarily for sharply novel methods with airtight causal evidence, then the paper is somewhat less convincing. As it stands, I view it as a solid and useful paper with clear practical insights, but with methodological limitations that should be acknowledged more explicitly.
