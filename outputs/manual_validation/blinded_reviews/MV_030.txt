Review ID: MV_030
Validation Track: RQ1
============================================================

This paper tackles an important and underexplored problem: moving spoken dialogue agents beyond turn-based, half-duplex interaction toward genuinely synchronous, full-duplex conversation. The central idea is to adapt a pretrained autoregressive LLM so that it can operate against a real-world clock, continuously listening and speaking, while handling overlaps, backchannels, and latency. Overall, I found the paper technically interesting, timely, and ambitious. It combines a clear problem formulation, a novel modeling proposal, a practical training recipe, and a reasonably broad empirical evaluation. In my view, this is a strong submission with meaningful potential impact on spoken dialogue research.

The paper’s main conceptual contribution is the notion of a “Synchronous LLM” that injects temporal structure into a standard decoder-only LLM through periodic synchronization tokens and chunked interleaving of the two speakers’ streams. The mechanism is explained clearly in Figure 1 on pages 2–3: at each current chunk, the model conditions on its own generated chunks and the user’s observed chunks up to the previous step, then predicts an estimated user chunk to compensate for latency before generating its own next chunk. This is a clever and intuitive way to reconcile autoregressive next-token prediction with the realities of streaming dialogue. I especially appreciated that the paper does not merely claim “real-time” interaction in a vague sense, but explicitly frames the problem around wall-clock synchrony and delayed access to the interlocutor’s current speech.

A second strong contribution is the tokenization and sequence-format design. The discussion around deduplication on pages 4–5 is thoughtful and well motivated. The authors argue that raw HuBERT token streams contain many repeated tokens due to silence and duration encoding, which dilute semantic density; Figure 3 on page 5 supports this claim by showing a substantial reduction in tokens per second after deduplication. The proposed solution—training on deduplicated sequences while preserving coarse timing through periodic speaker tags—is elegant. Figure 2 on page 5 is particularly helpful in illustrating the original interleaved token stream, the deduplicated prediction target, and the interpolation procedure used for synthesis. This design seems to strike a sensible balance between semantic efficiency and temporal grounding.

The training recipe is another notable strength. The three-stage curriculum described on pages 5–6 is pragmatic and well aligned with the data bottleneck in spoken dialogue. Starting from synthetic spoken dialogue generated from text SFT data, then moving to synthetic full-duplex dialogue without overlaps, and finally fine-tuning on about 2k hours of real dual-channel spoken dialogue is a compelling strategy. The scale of synthetic data used—212k hours total, summarized in Table 1 on page 5—is impressive and helps explain how the method can leverage the semantic competence of Llama3-8b despite limited real spoken dialogue supervision. This is one of the paper’s most practically valuable ideas: it offers a plausible path for building richer spoken dialogue systems without requiring prohibitively large full-duplex speech corpora.

Empirically, the paper presents a fairly convincing case that the proposed approach improves semantic quality substantially while preserving turn-taking naturalness. The semantic evaluation in Section 5.1 is strong in spirit: generated spoken dialogues are transcribed with ASR and scored using perplexity under a text dialogue model. Figures 5 and 6 on pages 6–7 show a large gap between SyncLLM and dGSLM, both in-distribution and out-of-distribution. The reported improvement is substantial enough that it is unlikely to be explained by noise alone. The human evaluation in Table 3 on page 8 is especially compelling: SyncLLM-F improves Meaningfulness MOS from 1.55 to 3.74 over dGSLM while keeping Naturalness MOS roughly comparable (3.90 vs 3.95). That is a very meaningful result, because many systems can improve content quality only by sacrificing timing realism, whereas this paper appears to avoid that tradeoff.

The naturalness evaluation is also thoughtfully designed. Rather than relying only on subjective judgments, the paper measures correlations of turn-taking event durations—IPUs, pauses, and floor-transfer offsets—between generations and ground-truth continuations. Table 2 on page 7 shows consistent gains over dGSLM, including on the out-of-distribution CANDOR test set. I also appreciated the inclusion of re-synthesized ground truth as a topline, which helps contextualize the ceiling imposed by the tokenizer/vocoder pipeline. The prompt-vs-generation correlation analysis in Table 7 on page 13 is a useful supplementary perspective on style consistency.

The interaction experiments are another positive aspect. Many papers on dialogue generation stop at continuation, but this work explicitly simulates two-agent interaction under one-chunk latency, including cross-dataset interaction between a Fisher-trained and a CANDOR-trained model. Figure 7 on page 8 and Table 4 on page 9 suggest that the interaction setting remains coherent and significantly better than the baseline, though somewhat weaker than single-model continuation. This is an important result because it tests the core claim that the model can function under delayed, streaming conditions rather than only in offline continuation mode. The latency analysis in Appendix C and Table 6 on page 12 / Figure 8 on page 13 is also useful: performance appears robust up to around 200 ms and degrades at 240 ms, which is a realistic and informative finding.

That said, there are several limitations and points that would benefit from clarification.

First, the evaluation of semantic quality via ASR transcription and text-model perplexity is reasonable but imperfect. Because overlapping speech is ignored during transcription, this metric may undercount failures or successes that are specific to full-duplex interaction. It also conflates speech intelligibility, ASR robustness, and dialogue coherence. The human Meaningfulness MOS partly addresses this concern, but I would have liked a more direct semantic evaluation that preserves overlap structure or at least reports ASR word error rates on generated speech. As written, it is difficult to disentangle whether gains come primarily from better language modeling, cleaner speech token generation, or easier-to-transcribe outputs.

Second, the comparison to dGSLM is important but not entirely comprehensive. dGSLM is indeed the closest prior full-duplex baseline, but the paper’s method benefits heavily from initialization from Llama3-8b and massive synthetic text-derived speech data. In one sense this is exactly the point of the paper, but it also means the comparison mixes architectural advantages with scale and pretraining advantages. A stronger ablation would isolate how much of the gain comes from synchrony modeling itself versus simply inheriting a powerful text LLM and large synthetic dialogue corpus. For example, a turn-based Llama3-derived spoken dialogue baseline without the synchronous chunking mechanism would help quantify the incremental value of the proposed full-duplex formulation.

Third, some design choices are plausible but under-ablated. The chunk sizes of 160/200/240 ms are compared in the main results, which is good, but other core choices are less explored. The interpolation heuristic described on pages 4–5 is simple and may distort duration patterns within a chunk; the authors state that the effect is imperceptible, but no objective or subjective evidence is shown specifically for this claim. Similarly, the decision to always include one speaker tag per chunk for speaker 0 but not necessarily speaker 1 is sensible for disambiguation, yet it would be useful to know whether alternative synchronization schemes were tried. The appendix includes one ablation on sentence-level versus turn-level interleaving in stage 1 (Table 5 on page 12), which is helpful, but the paper would be stronger with more ablations on the synchrony mechanism itself.

Fourth, the human evaluation, while useful, is still somewhat limited in scale and scope. Thirty-two annotators and 180 items is acceptable, but given the centrality of naturalness and meaningfulness claims, more detail would help. For instance, were raters blind to system identity and balanced across conditions? How were prompts sampled across durations and overlap patterns? Were significance tests performed between systems beyond overlapping bootstrap intervals? Also, because the task explicitly asks raters to ignore voice quality, the evaluation may understate practical user experience differences caused by the vocoder. Since the paper acknowledges speech quality limitations on page 9, a separate MOS for audio quality or intelligibility would have rounded out the picture.

Fifth, the paper’s framing around “full-duplex” is compelling, but the actual experiments remain simulated rather than deployed in a real interactive system with humans. The LLM-LLM interaction setup is a reasonable first step, yet it does not fully validate whether the model behaves appropriately with real users who may produce disfluencies, unexpected interruptions, or acoustically noisy input. The paper would benefit from either a small live user study or at least a more explicit discussion of what remains untested in moving from simulated interaction to real deployment.

There are also a few presentation issues. Some parts of the writing could be tightened for precision and grammar. A few examples include minor typographical issues and awkward phrasing in Sections 4 and 5.4. These do not impede understanding, but the paper would benefit from careful proofreading. In addition, some methodological details are deferred to the appendix when they are fairly central, such as more specifics on the text dialogue datasets used in stages 1 and 2, prompt sampling procedures, and the exact text dialogue model used for perplexity evaluation. Reproducibility would improve if these were stated more explicitly in the main paper or appendix.

Despite these concerns, I think the paper makes a substantial contribution. The problem is important, the proposed method is novel and well motivated, and the empirical results are strong enough to support the main claims. In particular, the combination of temporal synchronization, latency-aware user-chunk estimation, and synthetic-to-real training for full-duplex spoken dialogue feels like a meaningful step forward rather than an incremental tweak. The gains in dialogue meaningfulness over the prior full-duplex baseline are large, and the naturalness results suggest that these gains are not achieved by abandoning conversational timing.

My overall assessment is positive. I would recommend acceptance. To further strengthen the work in a revision, I would encourage the authors to add stronger ablations isolating the contribution of synchrony modeling from that of LLM pretraining and synthetic data scale; provide more detail and, if possible, significance analysis for the human evaluation; and discuss more explicitly the gap between simulated two-model interaction and real human-agent deployment. But even in its current form, this is a strong and interesting paper that I expect will be of broad interest to researchers working on speech, dialogue, and multimodal language models.
