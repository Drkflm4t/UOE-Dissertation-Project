# Review ID: MV_018

**Validation Track:** `RQ2`

---

This paper studies reward overoptimization in offline RLHF and proposes a theoretically motivated objective whose practical instantiation is simply DPO plus an SFT-style imitation term, termed RPO. The central claim is appealing: the SFT loss can be interpreted as an implicit adversarial regularizer arising from a maximin formulation over uncertain reward models, and this regularization provably mitigates overoptimization under partial coverage assumptions. The paper combines theory and LLM experiments, and the overall message is timely and potentially impactful. I found the high-level idea interesting and the practical prescription simple enough to matter. At the same time, I have substantial concerns about the precision of the theoretical claims, the novelty relative to existing preference-optimization variants that already add supervised terms, and the strength of the empirical evidence. My overall assessment is moderately positive on the idea, but I do not think the current version fully supports several of its strongest claims.

The main strengths are as follows. First, the paper addresses an important and concrete problem in RLHF: overoptimization due to reward uncertainty and distribution shift. This is a real issue in both theory and practice, and connecting it to partial coverage in offline preference learning is sensible. Second, the proposed practical algorithm is extremely simple. If the contribution were only “add a chosen-response SFT term to DPO,” that would not be enough for a top-tier venue, but the paper’s attempt to derive this from a principled adversarial objective gives it more substance. Third, the theoretical framing is interesting: the adversarial reward model minimizes MLE loss plus a reward penalty, and the resulting minimax reformulation leads to a DPO-like term plus imitation. This is a neat conceptual bridge between pessimism/robustness in offline RLHF and common engineering practice. Fourth, the empirical results, while limited, are directionally consistent: RPO appears to improve over DPO on the reported in-domain and benchmark evaluations for two model families.

That said, my biggest concern is that the paper overstates the theoretical-practical equivalence. The theory is developed for a fairly abstract offline contextual bandit setting with a reward class over compact action spaces, exact optimization, and explicit assumptions on coverage and reward-class regularity. The practical algorithm is then obtained by reparameterizing rewards through policies and directly optimizing a neural policy class. However, the actual training objective used in experiments is just DPO plus chosen-response log-likelihood, with a tuned coefficient. The paper presents this as an implementation of the theoretically justified minimax objective, but the gap between the formal setting and the actual neural optimization is large. In particular, the equivalence theorem appears to require assumptions on the reward class that are not obviously satisfied by the implicit reward class induced by a parameterized LLM policy. The paper should be much more explicit that the theory justifies a class of objectives, while the experimental RPO is a heuristic instantiation rather than a theorem-backed implementation in the strict sense.

Relatedly, Assumption 5.5 is doing a lot of work. The “convex-like” condition on the reward class is quite abstract and seems tailored to invoke a minimax theorem. The paper says this holds for convex classes such as linear or Lipschitz-continuous model classes, but the practical setting is not a convex reward class. Since the main practical contribution depends on the maximin/minimax equivalence, the paper should clarify exactly which realistic function classes satisfy the assumptions and whether the policy-induced reparameterization preserves them. As written, the theory feels elegant but somewhat disconnected from the neural setting that motivates the paper.

I also found some inconsistencies and imprecisions in the formal development. In Section 3, the baseline policy in the adversarial objective is denoted as pi_base, but in Section 5.2 the definition of phi appears to use pi_ref in place of pi_base in the expectation over a^0. If this is not a typo, it changes the objective materially; if it is a typo, it is an unfortunate one because the baseline policy is central to the method. There are several similar presentation issues. The manuscript contains repeated stray sentences in the introduction and contribution sections, suggesting editing problems. Equation explanations are occasionally sloppy; for example, the notation for the implicit reward in the DPO gradient appears malformed, and some references to theorem/proposition numbering are inconsistent. These issues do not invalidate the core idea, but they reduce confidence in the technical care of the paper.

On the theoretical side, the sample complexity result is interesting but should be interpreted cautiously. The bound in Theorem 5.3 depends on a partial coverage coefficient and a KL term to the reference policy. This is reasonable, but the paper’s narrative sometimes suggests that the method “provably mitigates overoptimization” in a broad sense. In fact, the guarantee is a standard-style offline learning comparison bound under realizability, bounded rewards, finite covering number, and partial coverage with respect to the comparator policy and baseline. This is useful, but it is not a direct theorem about avoiding reward hacking in realistic LLM training. I would encourage the authors to tone down the rhetoric and state more precisely what is proved: a suboptimality guarantee for the maximin objective under specific assumptions, and an equivalence result under additional regularity assumptions.

The role of the baseline policy also deserves more scrutiny. The theory emphasizes that pi_base should be suitably covered by the data, and the experiments instantiate it as the chosen-response distribution. But this choice is not innocuous: using chosen responses effectively biases the method toward imitation of the preferred samples, which may help in some datasets but could also reduce gains from preference optimization or amplify annotation artifacts. The paper briefly says the baseline can be adapted flexibly, but there is no empirical study of this design choice. Since the title and central claim are about “your SFT loss” being an adversarial regularizer, it is important to know whether the effect depends critically on using chosen responses, the reference model outputs, or some other baseline. An ablation over baseline choices is essential.

The empirical section is promising but not yet convincing enough for the strength of the claims. The experiments are limited to two 7B model families and compare mainly against DPO and released Zephyr checkpoints. Since several recent methods already combine preference optimization with supervised or odds-ratio style regularization, stronger baselines are needed, especially ORPO and DPO-positive/Smaug-like variants that the related work itself acknowledges as algorithmically similar. Without these comparisons, it is hard to assess whether the contribution is genuinely new empirically or mainly a theoretical reinterpretation of an already known recipe. The paper should also compare against a plain “DPO + SFT” baseline implemented in the most straightforward way, because that is effectively what RPO is in practice. If RPO differs only by coefficient scaling or interpretation, that should be made explicit.

The in-domain evaluation is particularly weak. The authors evaluate on 150 filtered examples judged by GPT-4 after discarding undesired annotations. This is a very small sample, and filtering based on judge formatting issues can introduce bias. Confidence intervals or significance tests are absent. The reported gains over DPO are also modest in some cases, especially on benchmark metrics where differences are small. For example, on AlpacaEval 2.0 and MT-Bench, the improvements over DPO are consistent but narrow. Given the variance of LLM-as-a-judge evaluations, it is difficult to know whether these are robust. At minimum, the paper should report multiple seeds, confidence intervals, and perhaps human evaluation on a subset. It would also help to report training curves for benchmark performance or preference accuracy, not just chosen-response log-probability.

I am also not fully convinced by the interpretation of chosen-response log-probability decrease as evidence of overoptimization. It is plausible and consistent with prior observations, but it is still an indirect proxy. A stronger empirical case would include analyses showing that DPO increasingly prefers responses that are out-of-distribution or judged worse by a stronger evaluator despite higher implicit reward, and that RPO specifically reduces this mismatch. For example, one could measure preference accuracy on held-out comparisons, calibration of the implicit reward, or divergence from the chosen-response distribution. As it stands, the paper’s main mechanistic empirical evidence is that RPO keeps chosen-response likelihood higher, which is expected because it explicitly optimizes that quantity.

The generalization discussion in Section 5.3 and the PCA plots are also not very persuasive. The corollary itself is mathematically straightforward: if the new prompt distribution is covered by the training prompt distribution, the bound scales with the density ratio. But the empirical “verification” via 2D PCA of embeddings is not a meaningful test of the bounded density ratio assumption. Visual overlap in a low-dimensional projection does not establish coverage. I would recommend removing or substantially softening this argument. It is fine to say the benchmarks are plausibly related in distribution, but the current presentation overreaches.

Another concern is novelty positioning. The paper acknowledges prior methods such as ORPO and DPO-positive that also add supervised-style regularization. The claimed novelty is the theoretical justification that SFT acts as an adversarial regularizer mitigating overoptimization. This is potentially enough for a theory-oriented contribution, but then the empirical section should be framed more as validation of the theory rather than as a new state-of-the-art practical method. Conversely, if the paper wants to claim a practical algorithmic contribution, it needs stronger empirical differentiation from these prior methods. Right now it sits somewhat awkwardly between the two.

I did appreciate the attempt to explain how RPO differs from DPO through gradient direction rather than merely gradient scale. However, that discussion is somewhat oversimplified. Adding an SFT term certainly changes the gradient direction in parameter space, but the practical effect depends heavily on parameterization, optimizer, and data mixing. Also, the statement that DPO’s beta “only changes the scale of the gradient instead of the direction” is not generally true once one considers the sigmoid weighting term and the dependence of the implicit reward on beta. This section should be made more careful.

There are also several concrete issues that should be fixed in revision. The manuscript has noticeable proofreading problems, including duplicated sentences in the introduction and contributions, typos such as “fune-tuning,” “overoptimzation,” and inconsistent theorem/proposition references. Equation numbering and notation should be checked carefully. The definition of phi in Section 5.2 should be reconciled with the earlier objective. The experimental tables should clearly indicate whether higher is better for each column and include uncertainty estimates. The training details should specify the exact mixing ratio between DPO and SFT losses, whether the SFT term uses the same prompts as the preference pairs, and whether any additional data preprocessing was performed.

To strengthen the paper, I would suggest the following. First, tighten the theory-practice connection and explicitly state the limitations of the equivalence result in neural settings. Second, add ablations on the baseline policy choice, the regularization coefficient eta, and the interaction with beta. Third, compare against stronger baselines that already incorporate supervised regularization, especially ORPO and DPO-positive-style methods, as well as a naive DPO+SFT baseline. Fourth, report multiple seeds and confidence intervals, and ideally include some human evaluation or at least a more robust automatic evaluation protocol. Fifth, provide more direct evidence for the claimed mitigation of overoptimization beyond chosen-response likelihood. Sixth, clean up the manuscript substantially.

In summary, I think the paper contains a genuinely interesting conceptual contribution: a maximin view under reward uncertainty that yields an SFT-regularized preference objective, offering a principled interpretation for a practically useful recipe. However, the current version does not fully substantiate its strongest claims. The theory relies on assumptions that are not well aligned with the neural implementation, the empirical evidence is somewhat narrow and missing key baselines, and the presentation has enough inconsistencies to undermine confidence. If revised carefully, this could become a strong paper, but in its present form I view it as promising rather than fully convincing.

My recommendation would be borderline accept to weak reject depending on venue standards and reviewer calibration. I lean slightly negative for a top-tier conference because of the missing empirical baselines and the looseness in the theory-to-practice bridge, but I would be open to being persuaded by a revision or rebuttal that addresses these points clearly.
